# Who is listening

Renata, 21:12: "Thursday? One question about the runway."

Lucía, 21:19: "Thursday works. Nine o'clock?"

Renata, 21:20: "Nine. Bring Mateo."

Renata was the first to invest in Tolva, the fourteen-person Montevideo company whose software decides which truck goes to which warehouse door, and when. She works from São Paulo and holds the third seat on its board, beside the two founders. Runway is investor shorthand for how long a company's cash lasts at its current spending, and Renata rarely asks about it at nine at night.

Lucía, Tolva's co-founder and chief executive, has two days and a spreadsheet full of answers. It is late May. Tolva has eleven months of cash at today's spending. Veldhaven, Tolva's largest client, pays a fee as each of its three sites goes live. Together those fees would add about four months of runway. Mateo, her co-founder and chief technology officer, has just written the design note that names the three things the September date rests on. The one most likely to move it is Veldhaven's security review.

What Lucía does not have is the question.

## A guess about March

Before she touches a slide, Lucía writes three lines about the one person who will ask it:

- **Knows:** the March signing, April's update, eleven months of cash.
- **Has to decide:** whether Veldhaven is still the right bet.
- **Stands to lose:** the client name she wants on the first slide of the next round.

The third line comes fastest. In March, Renata pushed hard for the Veldhaven deal and called it the logo the next round needed: a well-known client to show new investors. So Lucía builds Thursday's opening around one message: the logo is safe.

Then she does the part that is easiest to skip. On Wednesday morning she sends one question.

Lucía, 08:04: "So I can bring the right numbers: where does the answer go after Thursday?"

Renata, 08:31: "My partners, Monday. They set the reserve for your next round. I need months of cash if September moves, and a number I won't have to correct later."

The reserve is the money Renata's fund sets aside for Tolva's next round. Two lines of the sketch are wrong. Renata has nothing to decide about Veldhaven. Lucía writes the sketch again:

- **Knows:** the March signing, April's update, eleven months of cash; nothing yet about what the September date rests on.
- **Has to decide:** what to tell her partners on Monday about money for Tolva's next round.
- **Stands to lose:** her word with her partners, if the months she quotes turn out wrong.

It was a fair guess. It was also a guess about March.

## Three lines, then one question

When a message asks someone to decide, or carries bad news, start with the listener sketch. It is three lines about one named person before one message: what they already know, what they have to decide, what they stand to lose. Then check the sketch with one question, because everything in it comes from your side of the table. The lines take two minutes, a target this book sets; the question takes one message. That is cheap next to a meeting spent answering the wrong question.

The point sentence opens with "this listener"; the sketch tells you who that is and what they are listening for. On the page, the sketch fits one line:

`[name] knows [what last reached them]; has to decide [what, after your meeting]; stands to lose [what goes wrong for them if your answer turns out wrong]`

Lucía's second sketch fills the same three slots. Before you trust yours, check it against four mistakes:

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| A job title ("investors want growth") | One named person; for a group, the one who decides |
| What you know | What has reached them |
| The meeting | The decision after it |
| The loss, asked in its own words | A question about where your answer goes next |

Renata hears about Tolva once a month, in an update Lucía writes and has already smoothed. Her know line stops at April's update; nothing about the integration since has reached her.

> **From practice:** Before you write to someone senior, reread the last message they got from you about this work. That message, and whatever others have told them since, is what they know. Start from it, and if it was wrong, correct it first.

Renata's decision is on Monday, in a room Lucía will never enter. If you cannot write the decide line, you are not ready to write the message. You are ready to ask.

Write the loss as what goes wrong for them if your answer turns out wrong. With her partners waiting on Monday, Renata will hear every number as one she may have to defend in front of them. Hand her one she can defend, and she can listen to the rest. Hand her reassurance about the logo, and she has to dig for the number herself.

> **Line to keep:** What someone stands to lose decides what they can hear.

The third line is the one you are most likely to have guessed. People may keep a possible loss to themselves, because naming it can feel like showing a weak spot. Never ask the third line in its own words. "What do you stand to lose?" sounds like a therapist, and you would never say it to an investor. Ask what happens to your answer next: who hears it, and what they will do with it. Then listen for the loss in the reply, the way Renata's came through in "a number I won't have to correct later".

## Practice: the runway answer

**Situation.** Thursday, nine o'clock, the board call. Renata asks how many months of cash Tolva has if September moves. If Lucía had kept the opening she built on her first sketch, this is what Renata would hear.

**First draft (built on the guess).**
"Veldhaven is solid. Their operations team is committed, the integration is moving, and Mateo has measured that we can run all three sites with room to spare. September is realistic, and when we raise, their logo goes on the first slide."

**What's wrong.** No sentence in it holds a number of months. It is the accurate answer to a question nobody asked: is the logo safe? It is written for the Renata of March. "Realistic" carries no basis, and the whole draft is the easiest version for Lucía to say, which is part of why she wrote it.

**Better.**
"Eleven months at today's spending, with no Veldhaven money counted, so a September slip doesn't change the number you take to your partners. Once all three sites are live, their fees add about four months. The date rests mostly on their security review: one round, mid-September; two, about two weeks later."

**Why it works.** The number Renata will repeat on Monday comes first, in a form no slip can lower. The upside comes with its condition, and the date with what it rests on. Nothing in it asks her to trust a mood.

**Your turn (5 minutes now, 5 when the reply comes).** Pick one message you have to send this week that asks someone to decide: an update upward, a price, a design choice a client's architect has to approve. Write the three lines about the one person who decides, one sentence each. Circle the line you could not write without guessing, write the one question that would check it, and send that question first. When the reply comes, correct the sketch and write your first sentence for that person. **Done means** your first sentence uses something only the reply told you, and a colleague who reads it can name the person and the decision they face.

**If you do only one, do this one.**

## The same date, for Claire

On Thursday, Renata asks her question in one line, and Lucía answers with the number first. Renata writes it down and asks what could delay the extra four months. If sites two and three slip past December, Lucía tells her, their fees arrive in the new year instead. "Eleven I can defend on Monday," Renata says. The call takes twelve minutes, and Mateo never opens his note.

That afternoon Lucía writes to Claire about the same date. Claire runs operations at Veldhaven, and a board approved the project on her word. Earlier in May she got a summary from Tolva that said "on track", and nobody on the call the next morning could tell her what the date depended on. Mateo's note answered her the next day. Lucía sketches her first:

- **Knows:** her own warehouses, better than anyone at Tolva; the May summary; Mateo's note on what the date rests on, and the review she booked for August.
- **Has to decide:** what to tell her board about September, and what her own teams must do to keep it.
- **Stands to lose:** her word with her board, and her December peak if site one's delay runs into sites two and three.

Claire's May question is the check this time: she has already said, in her own words, what she is listening for. Then the message:

"Site one goes live on 14 September if your security review passes in one round, and about two weeks later if it needs a second. Either way, the plan for sites two and three stays before December: they wait for the same review, and even a second round would end weeks before their dates. The 14th depends on a first round that passes. Could your security team send us the review's questions this week, so we can answer each one before August?"

One set of facts sits under both messages: the review, the two weeks, the three sites. Renata's opens with months and says "mid-September", because her decision runs in months. Claire's opens with the 14th, because her teams plan by the day. It ends with something she can do this week to protect the very thing she stands to lose. Cash never appears in Claire's message, and Renata never hears about the review's questions. What comes after each first sentence, and in what order, is the work of *Give it a shape*.

## Words to use

To check the sketch before the meeting:

- "So I can bring the right [numbers / detail]: where does the answer go after [the meeting]?"
- "Who will you need to explain this to afterward?"
- "What do you need to leave the call with?"
- "Is there anything you've heard about this that I should know first?"

To open, once you know:

- "You asked about [the thing]. The short answer is [the number]."
- "The number you can take to [their meeting] is [number]. Here is what it rests on."
- "Before the detail, the part that touches your [decision]: [one sentence]."

When they won't say:

- "Then I'll bring the number and what it depends on, and you can test both."

### Phrases you will hear

- "a logo": a well-known customer whose name helps a company win other customers or raise money.
- "burn rate": how much cash a company spends each month.
- "skin in the game": they lose something if it fails.
- "read the room": notice how the people present are reacting before you go on.
- "where they're coming from": their situation and their reasons.

## When it backfires

The question can go unanswered. Some people won't say who they answer to, and some can't. Then write both readings of the line you guessed and find the answer that survives both. Lucía's eleven months would have served the logo reading too: an investor worried about a logo still needs to know the company survives a slip.

Power changes how you ask. Upward, ask about the next step and give your reason in the same message, as Lucía did with "so I can bring the right numbers". With someone who reports to you, the same question can feel like a test, so say why you ask before you ask it. With a client who holds the contract, ask what they need to take to their own boss.

Culture changes how direct the question can be. In some teams, asking about a senior person's next meeting is ordinary; in others it reads as prying. If you can't tell, ask only about the decision ("What do you need to leave the call with?") and notice how much more they offer.

Leaving facts out is where the sketch can turn dishonest. A message fitted to its reader changes order and emphasis. It never drops the fact this reader would least like to hear. The test: if a fact would change what this reader decides, it goes in, and first if it changes the answer.

Used too often, the sketch becomes a ritual. Two minutes before every chat message is two minutes wasted.

In a second language, write the three lines in the language you think in fastest; they are for you. Keep the check short enough to say without planning: "Who hears this after you?"
