# The hard conversation

What is the first sentence of the conversation you are putting off?

By Thursday morning Tyler had seven. "Got five minutes?" "Nothing serious." "How's everything?" "You know I respect your work." "Some people are worried." "I'm not blaming anyone." "We should all be more careful." He had typed them into a note, one under another, and none of them contained the word *merge*.

Tyler leads delivery at Tolva, the Montevideo company whose scheduler sends trucks to their doors at Veldhaven, a food wholesaler in Rotterdam. Two nights earlier, on 6 October, a change written by a coding agent had put the wrong labels on site one's doors for four hours. Its tests had passed, and an engineer Tyler trusted had merged it unread. One of Veldhaven's dock supervisors caught it.

At Wednesday's stand-up he had tried one on everybody: "Let's all be a bit more careful what we merge this week." By noon three engineers had asked whether he meant them. The one he meant wasn't among them.

## What a hint does

Tyler's gentle sentence worried three people who had merged nothing. For the fourth it named no day, no merge and no request, so she had nothing to answer and could only guess how bad it was.

When you hear trouble coming, you want one thing answered first: what happens to me?

Our target: the subject in your first sentence and, if you can promise it, what isn't at stake in your second. Bring facts the other person can check, say what you may have wrong, and ask. You can write all of it down tonight. Then both of you can look at one thing that happened.

> **Line to keep:** A fact they can answer does less harm than a hint they can't.

## The first minute

The shape is the second-question test from *Start here*, adjusted for this room: its limit is what you may have wrong, and its ask is a question. Tyler answered each question on paper.

- **What is the point?** Tuesday's merge went in unread.
- **What does it rest on?** The merge record: twenty past four, no reader's name.
- **Where does it stop?** I don't know what happened before twenty past four.
- **What do you want from me?** Her account, before mine; then a way to get the next one read.
- **Who answers for it?** In August I left "who reads it" with two names and no date.

He opened the record himself. Then he asked Mateo, who runs technology, whether she would stay on site two, since Tyler couldn't promise that alone. He wrote to her: "Could we talk at two, about Tuesday's merge? You're staying on site two, whatever we find. I want to hear what happened before it." An invitation that says only "Can we talk?" starts the conversation without you.

At two, on a video call, he said this:

"This is about Tuesday's merge. You're staying on site two, whatever we find; I've asked Mateo. The record shows you merged at twenty past four, with no reader's name. Some of that is mine: in August I left 'who reads it' without a date. I don't know what happened before twenty past four. What did?"

"I asked you," she said.

"When?"

"In the team chat, at three. 'Tests pass on site two's doors. Who reads this before it goes in?' You wrote one line. 'Great, let's keep site two moving.'"

Tyler found it while she waited. He had answered between two calls, and he had read as far as "pass".

"So you asked who reads it," he said. "I answered something else, and you took that as a yes."

"Yes. And I read the tests and never opened the change. I saw forty passes and stopped." Then: "Until your message this morning, I was sure I was off Veldhaven."

"Then Tuesday's answer is mine too. The unopened change is yours. What would make the next one different?"

"One name on it, before anyone merges. And when I ask a question, an answer to that question."

By four they had it in writing. With no reader's name, an agent's change waits, and she phones instead of typing. Tyler takes "who reads it" back to Mateo and Lucía, Tolva's two founders, on Monday, for one name and a date. In August, Tyler would have moved to the next item. Leaving with a decision that lasts is the habit of the problem-solver, the thinker type he is growing toward.

His limit did the most work. Had he opened with "You merged it unread," his own message would have come out as her defense. Because he said where his knowledge stopped, it came out as the next fact.

## Say what a record would show

Feedback runs on the observable version from *The exact word*: what a recording would show or a number would state. Aim it at the work, where something can change.

| Instead of | Say | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "You were careless." | "The change has no reader's name on it." | Either of you can look. |
| "You never speak in reviews." | "In the last two reviews, I didn't hear your view of the design." | "Never" starts an argument about never. |
| "The client isn't happy with you." | "Their architect asked me twice whether you'd read the spec. What should I tell him?" | It says who and what, and leaves room for an answer. |
| "Great work on the release." | "You wrote the rollback first, so we lost ten minutes instead of a night." | Now the praise can be repeated on purpose. |

It fills the first of four slots: `[what a record would show] [what it cost] [what I may have wrong] [what I'd like next time]`. Filled in: "The change went in with no reader's name. Site one ran on wrong labels for four hours. I may not know who you asked. Next time, phone me and wait." Describing the behavior, its effect and a next step is old advice in feedback training. The third slot is the one to protect when you're short of time.

Put the bad part before the kind part. Only the answer to "what happens to me?" may come earlier. If that answer is the bad part, say it before its reasons: "The senior role is a no for this round. I'll tell you why, and what would change it."

> **Research says:** In one study of people with mixed news to give, taking the receiver's perspective moved them toward the order receivers preferred, with the bad part first. So picture what they're waiting to hear before you choose your order. It is one paper without a direct repeat, and its subject was news, so the step to feedback is ours.

*The update* gives that order's cost: the relief left people less motivated to change, and feedback is for change. So we'd end on it.

## When the feedback is for you

It may reach you as a label, and labels sting. Ask for the day behind it. A client's lead has just told an engineer, "It's impossible to get an answer from you."

"Which day should I look at?" the engineer asks.

"Monday. I asked which field holds the pallet count, and you sent me a link."

"So you needed one field name and I sent you a manual. I'll answer in the thread first and link after. What I'm not sure of: was it only Monday?"

That is a read-back: what I understood, what I will do, what I am unsure of. The engineer never agreed to "impossible". If one fact is wrong, correct that fact and leave the rest standing. If you need time, ask for it with a date, and keep the date.

## An apology they can check

An apology makes a claim: I know what I did to you, and it will be different. Expect the second question: why would I believe that? Answer it first, with a change they can see without taking your word.

### Practice: the apology

**Situation.** On Wednesday, Claire, who runs operations at Veldhaven, took the night to her chief executive. That afternoon Lucía writes to her.

**First draft (what a careful founder writes).**
"Claire, I'm so sorry for the trouble last night. The change had passed every test, and as soon as your supervisor called, our engineers responded at once. We take this very seriously, and I promise it will never happen again. I know the team is doing everything it can, and I hope we can still count on November."

**What's wrong.** The defense comes before the damage, and "the trouble" could be anyone's. "Never" has nothing under it. The last sentence asks Claire for something while she is still paying for the night.

**Better.**
"Claire, last night our software showed the wrong doors at site one for four hours, and your supervisor had to find it for us. You then had to explain our failure to your chief executive. I'm sorry.

An agent wrote the change, its tests passed, and nobody here read it. Saying who reads such changes was Mateo's job and mine. It was on our list in August, and we didn't do it.

I don't know yet how many trucks went to a wrong door. You'll have the count by Friday noon.

Since this morning, nothing an agent wrote goes to your live sites. Every Friday you'll get a list of what did go live, with the name of the person who read it. I'm not asking you for anything today."

**Why it works.** It opens with what happened to Claire, and "I'm sorry" comes after something to be sorry for. It names whose job it was, and the engineer isn't in it. The promise is smaller than "never", and it can be seen on Friday.

**The second question.** Claire answered in one line: "Why would I believe the list?" Lucía wrote back, "You shouldn't yet. Pick any line and ask to speak to the person named beside it." On Friday the count came before noon. From the list, Claire picked the second line, Thursday night's repair to site one's labels. The name was the engineer from Tyler's call. She explained the change in two sentences, because this time she had opened it. Claire didn't say it was all right. She kept the list.

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Write the apology you owe: what you did, what it cost them, what you don't know yet, what has already changed and where they can see it. Cross out every "if", every "but" and every request. **Done means** a colleague who reads it can say what you did, what it cost, and how the other person would check the change.

## Practice: the conversation you're putting off

**If you do only one, do this one.** **With a person.** Fifteen minutes to prepare, then the real conversation, this week.

1. Answer the test's questions as Tyler did. Open the record behind each fact.
2. Find out what isn't at stake and who can promise it. If nobody can, leave it out.
3. Send the subject with the invitation.
4. When you meet, say your first two sentences, your facts, your part, your limit and your question. Then stop.
5. Say back the first thing they tell you that you didn't know. Watch what they do with it: correct it, add to it, or ask about your part.
6. Write down together who does what, by when.

**Done means** you both have that line in writing, and you can name one thing you believed beforehand that they confirmed or corrected.

**Spar with an AI.**

```text
Play a capable colleague I respect. I'll open a conversation about one piece of your work that went wrong. If my first two sentences don't tell you the subject and what isn't at stake, reply only, "Is this about my job?" Otherwise answer as someone with a good reason I haven't heard, including one fact that is partly my fault. Once, reply only "...". Afterward, list every word of mine that a recording couldn't show, and every place where I defended myself before saying your point back.
```

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## Words to use

- **To invite.** "Could we talk at [time], about [subject]? [What isn't at stake.]"
- **To open.** "This is about [subject]. [What isn't at stake.]"
- **To leave room.** "The record shows [what]. I don't know what came before."
- **Your part.** "My part was [what]. By [day] I'll have changed it."
- **When it's aimed at you.** "Which day should I look at?" "You're right about [part]. I need until [day] for the rest."
- **To end on the change.** "What would make the next one different?"
- **To pause.** "I want to get this right. Could we stop, and finish at [time]?"
- **When they apologize to you.** "Thank you. What I need to see is [what]."

### Phrases you will hear

- "Can I have a quick word?" (A private talk, often about a problem. Ask what it's about.)
- "No hard feelings." (I'm not angry with you.)

## When it backfires

**When you hold the power.** "What happened?" from the person who sets salaries can sound like a trap. Say your own part first, and let them answer tomorrow.

**When they do.** Ask before you give feedback upward: "I noticed something about our Monday calls. Would it help to hear it?" Keep it private and about the work, with the subject in writing first. A no is an answer.

**When it's already decided.** If a role is ending, don't ask for an account that can change nothing. Say the decision, stop, and stay for the questions.

**When an apology can cost you.** Facts in writing can be quoted in a claim. If money, an injury or a contract may follow, find out first who at your company may write it. Rules differ by country, and this isn't legal advice.

**When it isn't a wording problem.** Harassment, discrimination, threats, danger, anything unlawful: there is no first sentence for you to prepare. Keep dated notes, use a formal channel and get help. *Difficult people* ends there.

**When directness costs more than you think.** In some teams a criticism said aloud, even in private, can't be taken back. Ask how this person likes to hear a problem, and let the route change while the facts stay.

**In a second language.** Softeners shrink the subject: "a small issue" may be heard as small. Write your first two sentences word for word. And because "sorry" in English also means "I didn't hear you", make yours a full sentence: "I'm sorry we didn't read it."
