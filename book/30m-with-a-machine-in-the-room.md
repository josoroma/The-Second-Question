# With a machine in the room

*In a dispute: check what it promises*

"Did you mean the last sentence?"

Otávio asked by phone, on Wednesday 21 October, and Mateo had to open his own message to find it.

That morning Otávio, who heads information security at Tolva's client Veldhaven, had questioned an agent's change to site two's doors. Mateo, who runs technology at Tolva, had asked an approved assistant for a polite reply that kept Tolva's position. He checked its eight facts, and all were right. The courteous ending had no fact to check: "Naturally, nothing will reach site two without your team's approval."

Mateo had read good manners. Otávio had read a new rule. The rule agreed on Monday's call with Claire, who runs operations there, was narrower. A Tolva engineer signs each agent's change, and Otávio's reviewers have one working day to stop it. Approval takes a reviewer's time on every change. Otávio has two reviewers, and one is off every Friday. Because of Mateo's sentence, Otávio had asked that one to come in.

Mateo could have said the assistant wrote it. Otávio hadn't asked that.

"No," Mateo said. "I checked eight sentences and sent nine. I can't promise your reviewers' time or change Monday's rule alone."

"Then what can you promise?"

"You have one working day, so we'll sign nothing on a Thursday, starting tomorrow. Then your review day is never a Friday. The signing is ours."

Otávio said it back, then phoned his reviewer, who got the Friday off again. Mateo wrote to Otávio and Claire himself: "This morning I promised an approval I had never decided on. I take it back. Monday's rule stands, and from tomorrow we sign nothing on a Thursday."

## What a fact check lets through

In a hard moment, the sentences that count most aren't facts. A no, an offer, a deadline, the next step in an apology: each commits somebody to something, on a day. A machine can word them all, as firmly or gently as you ask. A fact check lets every one through: a promise can only go wrong later.

> **Line to keep:** A promise can't be proofread, only kept.

Keeping one takes somebody who knows what was promised before and will be there when it falls due. The assistant wasn't on Monday's call, and nobody will phone it on Friday. They'll phone you, with a second question.

Decide before it drafts. Write what you've promised this person, what you'll do by when, and what you won't do. Hand that over with one instruction: `If this needs a deadline, an offer, a promise or a consequence I haven't given you, leave a blank and ask me.`

Then read the draft twice, once for facts and once for what it commits somebody to. There, ask the last question of the second-question test, *Who answers for it?*, and write a name and a day. If the name isn't yours, ask that person first.

## Practice: what the draft commits you to

**If you do only one, do this one.** **With a person.**

**Situation.** You're a data scientist. Twice this month, a change in another team's table made your forecast wrong. You ask an assistant for a firm message to their lead.

**First draft (what an assistant writes).** "The region codes changed again on Friday, and Monday's forecast left out the north. We require two weeks' notice of any change. Otherwise we will have to build our own copy and involve senior management."

**What's wrong.** After the facts, it commits you to a build and a complaint, and you've decided on neither. If the answer is "Go ahead," you have no next sentence.

**Better (by phone).**

You: "The region codes changed on Friday, and Monday's forecast left out the north. It's the second time this month. If there was a notice, I missed it. Could someone tell me two days before a code changes?"

Lead: "I can't always give you two days."

You: "Then tell me the same day, and I'll hold the forecast until I've checked it. Can you keep that one?"

**Your turn (10 minutes, then a conversation).** Get an assistant's draft of a message you're putting off. Underline whatever commits somebody. Cross out what you wouldn't do if they answered "Go ahead." Say the rest to the person, ask "What will you do now?" and watch for a promise you didn't make. **Done means** they can say what each of you will do, and by when.

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## When it backfires

If they've already acted on a sentence nobody decided, it may still bind your employer. Ask whoever signs before withdrawing it. If they hold the power, phone first.

Don't ask who wrote theirs. They sent it, so ask which point to answer first.

Don't cross out everything. A hard message that commits nobody is a complaint.

If you'd be uneasy telling them how an apology was made, write it yourself.

In a second language, a draft's courtesy can promise more than you hear: *we'd be happy to*, *rest assured*. Ask for a list of its promises in your first language.

## Record again

Write your point, then take sixty seconds on the same prompt: what you are working on, and why it matters. (Written form: three minutes without stopping.) Run the five checks from *Start here*, with *said back* done by someone who argued with you this month.

Last, list what the recording commits you to, and by when. Compare it only with your own earlier takes.

*Safe practice: record only yourself, or others only with their consent and within your employer's rules.*
