# With a machine in the room

*After you listen: keep their words*

Hugo put it this way: "On a bad morning my phone rings before I've had coffee." The assistant put it this way: "Peak-hour dwell at inbound sites drives avoidable escalations."

Hugo runs operations at Sarnik, a chilled-food distributor near Lyon. In July, at a trade fair, he said the first one to Lucía, co-founder of Tolva, a dock-scheduling company. She had promised him a page by Friday on how Tolva measures a truck's wait.

On Thursday night she gave her notes, without his name or Sarnik's, to the assistant Tolva had approved for client work. The page came back finished. Its headline was the twenty minutes every stand had promised to save him. His phone had become an escalation. A driver shivered through a frozen dawn, and the last line asked for half an hour to discuss a pilot.

Then she asked the question Hugo would ask next: which driver? There wasn't one. Nor had she said what Hugo had to decide, so the assistant had assumed he was choosing whether to buy. Before that, he had to find out how long his trucks wait. She closed the page and wrote her own:

"You told me a truck without a door waits in your yard with the cooling running, and on a bad morning your phone rings before you've had coffee. You said nobody writes down how long it waits. Two clock times per truck, at the gate and at the door, would show it. Which warehouse could write both down for a week?"

Tolva's method went underneath, with what a price would depend on. The assistant corrected her spelling and nothing else. Hugo replied on Friday evening: "Gate times we have. Door times from Monday, one warehouse." He had corrected her, and there would be a count.

## Whose words, whose morning

You were there; the assistant wasn't. Unless you name what they said, assume it will trade it for something smoother and guess whose decision the page serves. So put it in your request:

`[Reader] has to decide [what]. Keep these words exactly: [theirs]. Use only this moment: [what happened, who saw it]. The count is [number, or "none yet"]. Before you draft, tell me what you understood and what you're unsure of.`

Lucía's, filled in: "An operations director has to decide how to count his trucks' wait. Keep these words exactly: my phone rings before I've had coffee. Use only this moment: the bad morning he described. The count is none yet." Correct the read-back before it drafts. Then ask each sentence of the draft where it came from: them, you or nobody. What came from nobody goes, however good it sounds.

> **Line to keep:** Keep what they said, even when the machine says it better.

## Practice: the note after the call

**With a person.** **If you do only one, do this one.**

**Situation.** You manage a product. A customer's finance manager told you, "At month end two of us stay until nine, matching invoices by hand." You promised a note.

**First draft (what an assistant writes from your notes).** "Thanks for a productive call. We covered invoice formats, user access, export timing, month-end workload and training."

**What's wrong.** Five topics, none chosen. "Month-end workload" hides two people staying until nine, and nothing is asked.

**Better.** "You said that at month end two of you stay until nine, matching invoices by hand. I don't know yet whether that's every month, or how many invoices it takes. Before I propose anything, can I sit with you on the 30th and count them?"

**Why it works.** Her words, so she can correct them; an honest gap where the count will go.

**Your turn (10 minutes).** After your next conversation about someone's problem, write one sentence in their words before an assistant sees your notes. Fill in the request and get a draft. Beside each sentence write *them*, *me* or *nobody*, and delete every *nobody*. Send it, and watch what the reply corrects or adds. **Done means** every sentence you sent has *them* or *me* beside it, and one of theirs matches your notes word for word.

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## When it backfires

**When they didn't expect to be quoted.** A sentence said at a fair may not be meant for someone's boss. If others will read your note, ask first.

**When an assistant takes the notes.** Even where everyone has agreed, people may say less. Read its summary for the words it traded away.

**In a second language.** Keep a phrase said in another language as they said it, beside the English. A translation is a draft too.

## Record again

Record sixty seconds on what you are working on and why it matters, for one listener you can name. Make your example one moment that happened, with its count. Then check:

1. **Sentences before the point.** How many come before the first one that says why it matters? If none does, write *never*.
2. **Hedges with no basis.** How many *probably*, *I think*, *should* or *hopefully* have no reason after them?
3. **One example.** Is there one case that shows why it matters? Background doesn't count.
4. **The ending.** Is there an ask or a next step?
5. **Said back.** Before anyone hears it, write the point you meant. This time, find a person, who hears it once, writes the point in one sentence and asks you a question. Their sentence should name your point and your ask.

The filler count is optional; skip it if you stutter, work in a second language or pause to think. In writing, take three minutes without stopping. Keep it with your earlier takes.

*Safe practice: record only yourself, or others only with their consent and within your employer's rules.*
