# Say less

Sarnik's operations director asked his question in the first minute of the video call, and Naomi answered it in the fifth.

The question was "What does it cost?" Naomi is a data consultant who works alone from Seattle. In March, Veldhaven, the Rotterdam food wholesaler, hired her to predict when trucks would reach its three warehouses. Early on this June morning, already afternoon in Lyon, she was offering the same kind of forecast to Sarnik, which distributes chilled and frozen food from four warehouses near Lyon. Its operations director had her one-page description open, and a budget that had just been cut.

"Good question," Naomi said. "Before the number, let me explain what goes into it, because otherwise it will sound like a lot."

She explained that every warehouse needed six weeks of its own gate logs before she trusted a forecast there. Then she said it again: "So basically, it's not one model for everyone." She described how she checked each forecast against the log herself, every week. She said what a truck that arrived an hour before or after its slot cost a dock, and what larger firms charged for less. She said she had no office and no staff, so none of that was in her price. "What I mean is," she said, reaching the same point a third time, "your warehouse gets its own forecast." Once more she said she knew the number would sound like a lot.

Four minutes after his question, she said it: "So it would be three thousand euros a month per warehouse."

The line went quiet. She could hear him typing. She filled the silence: "Roughly. But I'm flexible, so tell me what works for your budget."

The typing stopped. "Flexible how much?"

## A defense nobody asked for

Of everything Naomi said in four minutes, the director kept one word, and it came in her last sentence.

Most of those sentences would have served her well later, once he asked how the forecast worked. Said before the number, they became a defense, and a defense told him there was something to defend. The two warnings agreed with a complaint he had not made. "Roughly" turned a price she sets into a guess. And "tell me what works for your budget" handed him the job of pricing her work. Leaving the price open sounded generous, and it left him to finish her proposal.

The four minutes also pushed out the one fact he most needed. At the one cold warehouse Naomi had measured, her forecast was less accurate than at the others, and she did not yet know why. Every one of Sarnik's warehouses is cold.

## Where to stop

Say your point once, at the size the facts allow, and then stop. The end comes when the first four questions of the second-question test have their answers: *What is the point? What does it rest on? Where does it stop? What do you want from me?* Aloud, that is about fifty words, a target this book sets. Any sentence after your ask repeats an answer or argues with one. Of all the skills in this book, this is the easiest to start, because you start it by deleting.

Naomi's answers fit on four lines:

- **The point.** Three thousand euros a month per warehouse, starting with one.
- **What it rests on.** A check he can run on his own gate logs.
- **Where it stops.** The one cold warehouse she has measured, where it is less accurate.
- **What she wants.** A start date.

The first line comes from her point sentence, built as in *Know what you think*. "The director should start with one warehouse at three thousand euros a month, because his own logs will show him within a month whether it works." When the point is a price, the price goes in the sentence.

"Roughly" is gone from the four lines, because a price you set is a decision, and a decision has nothing to size. The cold-warehouse limit is in, because it rests on her own measurements and he can't decide without it. That is the sized claim from *How sure are you? Say that*, used for cutting: a hedge stays only when it has a basis.

In the first minute, she could have said: "It's three thousand euros a month per warehouse. I'd start with one: in the first month, your own gate logs show you how often it was within thirty minutes, before you pay for a second month. At the one cold warehouse I've measured, it's less accurate, and I don't know why yet. When could we start?"

> **Line to keep:** Stopping tells the listener you meant it.

## Leave out the apology and the repeat

An apology in front of your point tells the listener how you expect it to be heard. "I know this will sound like a lot" asks them to treat the number as a problem before they have weighed it. Save apologies for real faults; *The hard conversation* covers how to make one.

Then the repeat. Naomi made her per-warehouse point three times without being asked, and the second and third each opened with a filler: "So basically," "What I mean is." A second try is fair when the first one missed, as long as it is shorter and different. Before a third, stop and ask which part didn't arrive, and say only that part.

Fillers are saying more at the smallest scale: words that add nothing, such as *um* and *basically*. As in *Start here*, the count is optional. If you want fewer, start with those that open a repeat, like Naomi's "So basically": catch one and you catch the repeat behind it.

| Instead of | Say | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Three thousand. Roughly." | "Three thousand." | A hedge on a number you set makes it sound open |
| "So basically, what I mean is..." | "Which part should I say again?" | A question shows you where the gap is |
| "There are a few options, each with trade-offs, so it depends, but..." | "I recommend the second option." | The trade-offs are your answer to their "Why?" |

## The pause you chose

Decide before you speak that your ask will be your last sentence, then wait after it until the other person replies.

Naomi got that pause after her number and filled it. The quiet after "three thousand euros a month per warehouse" was the director typing the number into his budget sheet. To her it sounded like a verdict, so she ended it with "roughly" and an open offer.

For the listener, a quiet like that may be working time: the number against the budget, and against what they will say when someone asks about it. Leave them that time.

> **From practice:** Make your ask the last line of your notes, written as a question. Your notes then end where your answer should, and the question hands the turn to the other person.

## Practice: the rate, written once

**Situation.** Sarnik's operations director had asked "Flexible how much?", and Naomi had promised him a figure in writing that evening.

**First draft (what a careful consultant writes).**
"Thank you for your time today, and sorry again for the long answer. Before I confirm a figure, I'd like to explain a little more about how the forecast is built, as I realize the number may seem high at first. Each warehouse needs its own fitting, so I start with six weeks of your gate logs, and I check every forecast against the log myself. As a one-person business, I have no overhead to pass on. All in, the cost would be around three thousand euros a month per warehouse, but I'm happy to discuss whatever works for your budget."

**What's wrong.** It is the call again, on paper: two apologies in front, the defense before the answer, a hedge on her own price and an open offer to finish. It still has no limit and no date, and it never answers his question.

**Better.**
"The forecast is three thousand euros a month per warehouse. Where I can be flexible is the start: one warehouse, for three months. In the first month I fit it to six weeks of your gate logs, and you see how often it was within thirty minutes before paying for a second month. It is less accurate at the one cold warehouse I've measured, for reasons I don't know yet; your first month will show whether that holds for you. Can we start on 1 July?"

She sent it that night. His reply came the next morning: "One warehouse from 1 July. Who on my team do you need in the first month?"

**For Veldhaven's finance lead.** The same week, Naomi sent the same rate to Veldhaven. Its finance lead approves her next contract; Claire, who runs operations there, decides when site three joins.

"From July to September, keeping the forecast running at sites one and two is three thousand euros a month per site, eighteen thousand euros for the quarter. Claire's go-live plan includes it at both sites. Site three joins at the same rate once its numbers catch up; Claire will decide when, from my weekly note. Could you approve the eighteen thousand by 30 June, when my current contract ends?"

**Why it works.** Each message answers the first four questions once, then stops. The listener sketch from *Who is listening* shows why they differ. The director decides whether to try the forecast at all, so he gets the rate first and a start date last. The finance lead decides how much to approve and for how long, so that message opens with the total and the months. The message to Sarnik answers "Flexible how much?" by moving the flexibility from the price to the start.

**Your turn (5 minutes).** Take a message you must send this week that carries a price or a date. Draft it, then mark the four answers in it, one sentence each, and delete everything before the first and after the last. If one is missing, *Words to use* below gives you a start. Then look for an apology in front of the number, a hedge on a number you set and an open offer at the end. **Done means** a colleague who reads only your first sentence can tell you the number and what it covers, and nothing follows your ask.

## Practice: hold the stop

**If you do only one, do this one.**

**Timed drill (30 seconds).** Ask a colleague to put to you the question your work gets most often: what it costs, when it will be ready, or what you recommend. Give your answers in 30 seconds or less, then stop; the silence after them is not timed. Constraint: the number, the date or the recommendation goes in your first sentence. Run three rounds. In each, your colleague makes one of these moves, in an order you don't know:

1. Say nothing after your ask, for as long as they like. Wait until they speak.
2. Cut in once before you finish: "That's more (or later) than we planned." Answer in one sentence, finish, and stop.
3. Wait until you stop, then say: "Sorry, I don't follow." Ask which part, and say only that part again.

**Untimed form.** Write your answers and a one-sentence reply to each move, then read it all aloud once.

**Done means** your colleague can repeat your number and its one reason, and you said nothing after your ask until they spoke.

## Words to use

- **To give the price.** "It's [amount] a month for [what it covers]." "The rate is the same for every customer." (Only if it is.)
- **To give what it rests on.** "You'll see [the result they can check] before you pay for [the next step]."
- **To give the limit.** "What I can't promise yet is [the limit]."
- **To ask.** "Can we start on [date]?" "Could you approve it by [date]?"
- **When they push back.** "I understand it's more than you planned. The rate stays the same, and the start can be smaller."
- **When they don't follow.** "Is it the price that's unclear, or what it covers?"
- **To take back a "roughly" or an open offer.** "Let me correct one thing I said earlier: [the correction]."

### Phrases you will hear

- "That's a big number." The price surprised them. It is not yet a no.
- "Walk me through the number." They want the reasons now.
- "Is there any wiggle room?" They are asking whether the price can move.

## When it backfires

**When they ask for the reasons.** Then the reasons are the answer: give them in order, and stop when the questions do. Most of Naomi's reasons were answers to questions he had not asked yet; they did harm only because they came first.

**When the other person holds the power.** A buyer with other options, or a client who can end your contract, may hear a bare number as abrupt. Put the warmth around the number and keep the number itself bare: no apology, no "roughly". If they push, hold the rate and the stop: one sentence, then quiet.

**When money comes up another way.** In some companies nobody names a price until the work is agreed; in others the first price is only a starting point. Ask someone who works there how prices are usually raised. If they expect yours to move, say once that it is the same for every customer, but only if it is.

**When you cut too much.** Cutting the limit to sound sure is saying less of the wrong thing: where it stops is one of your answers. And with someone thinking aloud, one clipped line per question can sound unwilling. Think aloud with them: give the point, then your reasons as their questions come.

**When the silence becomes pressure.** Held to make the other person uncomfortable, the pause you chose becomes a tactic. The check is whether you could say aloud what you are doing: "I'll stop there so you can think." If someone uses silence on you, your number was complete when you stopped. A silence before you answer "Is that your best price?" is different: it can sound as if the price is still moving, so answer at once. *Thinking on your feet* covers the pause before an answer.

**In a second language.** A short sentence leaves less to go wrong when you are nervous, so write the number sentence before the call and say it as written. Leave out the apology for your English. It is one more apology in front.
