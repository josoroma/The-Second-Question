# Saying no and pushing back

Before ten on a Monday in October, Bianca, Tolva's product manager, had three requests to answer.

Site three's manager: "Site two's drivers get their door by text from 9 November. Please add site three for the 23rd. It's the same thing."

Claire: "After last Tuesday night, I need the door override on my supervisors' phones before site two goes live on the 9th."

Lucía: "Nothing new at site one before the peak, so your rule for containers waits until January. Can you tell the team and site one's manager today?"

The first two came from Veldhaven, Tolva's client, where Claire runs operations. Lucía is Bianca's own chief executive. On "last Tuesday night", 6 October, a change written by an agent had mislabeled doors at Veldhaven's first warehouse for four hours.

One reply would fit all three. "Thanks, it's on the list." The list was real. It had a hundred and forty lines, and nothing on it had ever been refused.

What would a manager do with those words, seven weeks before the peak?

## Nothing refused, nothing promised

People plan on whatever you haven't refused, so a vague answer costs them a plan. A clean no gives them time: the first sentence says it won't happen, and the rest helps them act on it. It answers the questions of the second-question test from *Start here*, in order:

- **What is the point?** No, to what, by when.
- **What does it rest on?** One reason they can check.
- **Where does it stop?** What you are not refusing, and when asking again makes sense.
- **What do you want from me?** What to do now, without it.
- **Who answers for it?** The name of whoever decided.

> **Line to keep:** Say the no first, and leave them something they can use.

Bianca wrote to site three's manager:

"No, site three won't have driver texts on the 23rd. They have never run on a real morning; site two's first is the 9th. The no covers this peak only. With site two's numbers, I can give you a yes or a no for spring by 15 January. Please plan December without them. I decided this, so bring me the questions."

The answer came within the hour: "Not what I hoped. But I can still find a second person for the gate."

That second person is what a clean no is for. Bianca offered no regret and no compliment, and her message is still gracious. Grace at work is mostly this: an early answer, the true reason, and something they can still use. It came the morning she was asked, while there was time to act, and it said when to ask again. Soften what the no costs them, never the no. And give one reason. Three can sound like a search for one they'll accept.

## Eleven minutes later

Bianca built her answer to Claire the same way:

"No, not before the 9th. Phones need their own login, and your security review allows ten working days a round. August's needed two. If it started tomorrow, a second round would end on the 9th itself. After the peak, I'd say yes. Shall I book the review for January? This is my decision."

Eleven minutes later Claire replied, with Lucía and Veldhaven's chief executive copied: "I asked how my supervisors get through the next bad night. I was sent a policy I already know."

Bianca read her message again and found nothing to correct. What failed was around it. She had refused the phones without asking what they were for. And she had done it in writing, to someone who had spent a week answering upward for a night Tolva caused. A senior person may have to forward your no, and nothing in this one was any use to Claire.

It was also Claire's second no to phones. In August Lucía had promised them on a Monday and taken them back on the Wednesday.

Bianca sent Lucía one line before she did anything else: "The no is right, and I sent it badly. I'm phoning her."

"Good. If she asks me, she'll hear the same no."

Bianca phoned. "Phones are still a no for the 9th. I should have called before I wrote that. What should a supervisor be able to do on the next bad night?"

"Find it in ten minutes, instead of four hours."

"Then phones wouldn't have helped. They'd have shown the same wrong labels. What if a supervisor compares the screen with the signs, every door, at the start of each shift?"

"Send me that and the no, in one message. That one I can pass on."

She passed it on before noon. That night Diego, the engineer Tolva had placed at Veldhaven, walked the first check with the supervisors. The no never moved.

With a senior client, or with anyone in a bad week, the answers stay and the route changes. Ask what the yes was for. Tell your own boss. Say the no aloud, with what they can have. Write it last, in one message they can forward. Asking which problem needs solving is the habit of the framer, the thinker type Bianca is growing toward. That morning it came one message late.

## What you can trade

When someone moves your date or grows your scope, offer a choice. Say what can't all happen, show two versions that can, and name the part that stays in both. Trading scope against time is as old as projects. The part to protect is the check that catches a mistake before customers do.

### Practice: all of it by the 1st

**Situation.** You run a two-person software company. A new ordering page is agreed for the 15th. Domestic orders are built and tested; export orders wait for the customer's product codes; the load test takes two days. On Wednesday the customer's head of sales writes: "We've announced the new page for the 1st. I'm sure you can make it work!"

**First draft (what a careful founder writes).**
"Thanks for letting me know. That's tight, to be honest. We still have export orders, and we're waiting on the product codes, and the load test is two days, so it's a lot. But we'll do our best to get there."

**What's wrong.** The defense comes before the answer, and the answer never comes. "Do our best" will be read as the 1st. The product codes, the one thing they can act on, hide in the middle.

**Better.**
"All of it on the 1st, no. Domestic orders on the 1st, yes, if Monday's load test passes. Export orders need your product codes; if they're here by Friday, the 15th holds for everything. The load test stays in both versions. Which should the announcement say: domestic on the 1st, or everything on the 15th? I need your choice by noon tomorrow."

Their reply: "I need all of it. Can't you skip the load test?"

"No. Without it, your customers would be the test. What I can add from the 1st is export orders by email, which we'd enter by hand. Who decides about the announcement? I'd like to show them both versions."

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Take a date or a scope that someone has changed on you. Write what can't all happen, two versions that can, the part that stays in both, and the choice with its day. Then write your answer to the second push. **Done means** someone outside your work reads it once and can tell you what won't happen and what there is to choose.

## Decided, or still open?

The container rule gives containers packed without pallets a longer slot at the door. Two people unload them by hand in about two hours, against the forty minutes booked, and the next truck waits. Bianca wanted the rule live for the peak. Lucía's message said January.

She asked before she argued: "Is this decided, or still open?"

"Open until four," Lucía wrote.

Then she used sort first, from *Thinking on your feet*. A disagreement is about what is true (agree the test before anyone argues the answer) or what to do (agree the goal). Or it is about who gets something limited (agree a fair rule and name who decides). Bianca phoned to find out which this was.

"An agent wrote that rule's tests," Lucía said. "An agent wrote last Tuesday's."

"If a person reads every test by Thursday and finds nothing, would you ship it?"

"No."

So no test would settle it. Bianca left the tests alone and argued the goal, with a sized claim: "Then this is about December. Diego counted three of those containers a week in September, and each one costs the next truck about an hour. Nobody has counted a December. I'd ship it. I'd change my mind if the planners could book two hours for them by hand."

"Maybe they can. I believe your number," Lucía said. "I still want Claire to say, any day until January, that nothing changed at site one, and be right. It waits."

"Then it waits. I'll tell them."

Lucía answers to Claire for site one, so the decision was hers. What Bianca owed her was one honest view, at its size, while it could still count.

If you are the senior one, less of this may reach you than you need. "Open until four" got Lucía the best argument against her plan before the plan was fixed. Say what is open, and until when.

## "Did you agree with this?"

Many companies ask people to disagree first and commit afterward. It is good advice, and it stops before the hard part: facing the people who will live with the decision. Bianca wrote the no she had argued against twice, for two readers.

To site one's manager, who has a December to run:

"The container rule won't reach site one before the peak. After last week we are changing nothing there until January, so what your supervisors know stays true. I know the cost: about three trucks a week that wait an hour. Could your planners book two hours when the packing list says no pallets? If they can't, tell me, and I'll take that to Lucía, who decided this. You'll have a date from me by 1 December."

To the team, who have work to put down:

"The container rule stays on the test copy. Nothing changes at site one until January, so Claire can say that and be right. The rule itself isn't in question. By Thursday, please leave a note that says what is tested and what isn't, so whoever picks it up in January can start there. Questions to me."

The engineer who had built it asked one: "Did you agree with this?"

"I argued for shipping it, and Lucía heard me before she decided. Her reason is a good one, and it's our plan now. If the planners can't do it by hand, that's a new fact, and I'll take it to her."

"Yes" would have been false. "Lucía decided", with nothing after it, would have handed the team Bianca's objection to carry. Backing a decision means you stop arguing in the rooms that can't change it, and bring new facts to the one that can.

## Practice: one no this week

**If you do only one, do this one.** **With a person.** Find the oldest request you know you won't do. Put the test's answers on paper. If the person is senior or in a bad week, ask what it was for, and say the no aloud before you write it. Then listen to the first thing that comes back:

- "Why not?" Your reason wasn't one they could check.
- "So what do I do?" You left them nothing to use.
- "But you'll try?" They never heard the no. Say your first sentence again, more slowly.

**Done means** they have told you what they will do without it.

**Spar with an AI.**

```text
Play a vice president at my largest client. I will refuse a request: [the request].
Ask me once why. Then say you will call my boss. Then ask what you
are supposed to tell your own boss. Accept the no only if I keep it
and give you something you can use. Afterward, in three sentences,
say whether you heard yes, no or maybe, what you can plan on now,
and where I was unclear.
```

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## Words to use

- **To find what it's for.** "If you had it on [day], what would you do with it?"
- **The no.** "No, not by [day]. [The one reason.]" "Ask me again on [day], when I'll have [what]."
- **The trade.** "[This] or [that] by then. [The check] stays in both."
- **When you can't refuse.** "I can start today. Which of these two waits?"
- **To someone senior.** "Is this decided, or still open?" "Could I tell you why I see it differently, before you decide?"
- **To back it.** "We're doing [this], because [the reason]. Questions to me."
- **The graceful exit.** "Thank you for asking me first. I'd like to know what you decide."

### Phrases you will hear

- "Can you squeeze it in?" (Do this too, and move nothing. Ask what should move.)
- "That ship has sailed." (It's decided. Ask what can still change.)
- "Let's agree to disagree." (They want to stop. Ask who decides, and when.)

## When it backfires

**When they hold the power.** You may not be free to refuse. Turn the no into an order of work, and write it down: "I'm starting the report. The migration moves to the 20th. Tell me today if you want it the other way."

**When you shouldn't back it.** Carry a choice you would have made differently. Don't carry one that puts your name to something false or unsafe, or that hides a risk from the people who would pay for it. Tell the person deciding, keep dated notes, and use the formal route; *Difficult people* says where wording stops helping.

**When a first-sentence no is rude.** In some rooms it costs the other person their standing. Keep the no and change the route: alone, beforehand, or through someone they trust.

**When everything gets a no.** A two-minute favor doesn't need the whole test, and people find a way around whoever refuses everything. If you can't remember your last yes, ask what your list is for.

**In a second language.** On a call, *can't* is easily heard as *can*. Start with the word no, or say *cannot*, then write the sentence in the chat.
