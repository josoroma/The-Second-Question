# The meeting

Tyler: "Good morning, everyone. Two weeks of work, forty-five minutes, two decisions. The first one is..."

Lucía: "Sorry, Tyler, one thing before that, because it changes the plan. On Monday I told Claire her supervisors will have the override on their phones from the first night. So phones go to the top, and the rest moves down. Any problem with that?"

Tyler: "Okay. Let's see what we can move."

By the end of minute one, the plan had changed. This was planning at Tolva, a video call on the last Wednesday in August, run by Tyler, the delivery lead. Bianca, the product manager, decides the order of the work, and four engineers do it. Lucía, co-founder and chief executive, doesn't usually come.

The week before, she had told Veldhaven, Tolva's largest client, that its first warehouse, site one, would go live two weeks late, on 28 September. On Monday Claire, who runs operations there, asked what her supervisors would get for the wait, and phones were an easy yes. The override lets a supervisor move a truck to another door. For now it runs on a screen they have to walk back to.

In the silence after "any problem with that?", one engineer unmuted, and muted again.

## The plan that changed by silence

Tyler's answer kept the call calm, and it let a plan change without anyone deciding it. He didn't ask whether the override was ready for phones, what would wait, or whose choice it was. One engineer had the answer to Lucía's question and did not say it.

Sort each item before anyone argues it, hear the person who knows before the person who decides, and close with each owner saying the decision back, with a date. Each takes one sentence, and you can say it without running the meeting. Learn the three, and you are the person people want in the room when something has to be settled.

> **Line to keep:** A decision is only as real as the name beside it.

## Minute four: what kind of item is this?

Tyler had opened the list to see what could move. Nothing on it was his to move. He closed it.

"Lucía, if phones go first, I'd like us to decide it. Bianca, is the order still yours?"

"The order is mine," Bianca said. "The promise is Lucía's."

Tyler typed three questions into the shared notes, where everyone could see them. Is the override ready for phones? What does the first night need? Two engineers: phones, or what was on top before?

This is *sort first*, from *Thinking on your feet*, used on an agenda. What sounded like one item was three, and each is settled by something different.

| An item about | Is settled by | In Tyler's notes |
|---|---|---|
| What is true | A test, agreed before anyone argues the answer | Try it the way site one will use it |
| What to do | The goal, said aloud | What the first night needs |
| Who gets something limited | A fair rule, and the name of who decides | Two engineers; Bianca decides |

Take them in that order, because a choice made on a wrong fact has to be made again. An item that fits no row is news: send it beforehand and drop it from the list. For the rest, write each item as a question with the decider's name after it. "Phones" is a topic. "Do phones go first? Bianca decides" is something a room can finish.

## Minute nine: the person who knows, then the person who decides

"Question one," Tyler said. "First anyone who worked on the override this week, then everyone else. Bianca and Lucía last."

The newest person on the call spoke, an engineer on contract in her second week. "This may be a basic question. What happens if two supervisors move the same truck?"

"It gets two doors," said the engineer who had unmuted in minute one. "I did it on Monday, on the test copy, from two screens. I've seen it once. With phones, everybody has a screen."

"Why didn't you say so when I asked?" said Lucía.

"You had already told Claire."

Lucía's question had come after the promise, from the person who made it. Any reply had to begin by telling the chief executive, in front of her own team, that she was wrong. That is how the most senior person in a room can be the last to hear what it knows. If that person is you, ask before you announce, ask for facts instead of objections, and speak last, which is an old practice in many teams.

Hear, too, the engineer's "everybody has a screen". It says what the change would set off next, and it came from someone who had run it.

The contract engineer could ask because nobody expected her to know. While you are new, the basic question is yours, so use it. Once you aren't, invite it from whoever is.

> **Research says:** One paper reports two studies of diverse groups in which higher-status members tended to have more influence on the group's decision. That gap narrowed when the leader looked more at lower-status members; with influence more even, the groups worked through their information better and did better on the task. No independent repeat was found, and the finding is about where a leader looks, so calling on people first by name is our extension.

On a video call nobody can tell where you are looking. Use names, and tell people before the meeting that you will.

## Minute twenty: a rule, a decider, a sentence

By minute sixteen two engineers were deep in how to lock a record. "Do we need that to choose, or to do it?" Tyler asked. "To do it," one of them said, and they took it to the chat.

"Question two. What does the first night need?" Lucía made her case: a supervisor with a truck at the wrong door shouldn't have to walk to a screen to fix it. She was right, and Bianca said so. Bianca added one thing: no truck with two doors. The goal they agreed on included both.

That left the limited thing, two engineers for two weeks. Tyler asked for the rule before the choice. "Whatever the first night can't run without goes first?" Bianca and Lucía both said yes. Then Bianca decided, in a point sentence: "We fix the double move first, because a truck with two doors is worse than a supervisor who has to walk."

"I'd have tried to do both," said Lucía. "But the order is yours, and the promise is mine to move."

The group didn't vote, and it didn't wait until everyone agreed. One person decided, named before the discussion, by a rule agreed before the answer. Lucía could repeat the reason to Claire without sharing the view. In June Tyler would have smoothed all this over; now the disagreement had a kind, a rule and a name, which is the problem-solver's habit taking hold.

Choose that way when one person has to answer for the result. Ask for everyone's agreement when everyone has to keep the decision, as with a team's working rules. And keep out of the meeting whatever one person can decide and undo alone: say in advance that whoever sees it first decides, then tells the rest.

## Minute forty-one: the owners, one by one

Tyler didn't summarize. He asked each owner for a *read-back*, from *Listening people can feel*: what I understood, what I will do, what I am unsure of.

"Phones are not in this plan," Lucía said. "I'll call Claire today, before six her time. I'm not sure what date to give her."

"None yet," said Bianca. "Ask me at the next planning, on 9 September. By then the fix will have run for a few days."

"I'll fix the double move and show it from two screens by 4 September," said the engineer. "I'm not sure who reads the tests if an agent writes them."

That was Tyler's other decision: who reads what the coding agents write before it merges. Two minutes were left; Lucía's item had used the rest. "Mateo and I will settle that," said Lucía. Mateo, her co-founder, was not on the call. Tyler typed both names. He could see that the line had no date, and he could see the clock, and he let it go.

A chair's summary, or an assistant's, would have said "Lucía to update Claire", and nobody would have known she had no date to give. The unsure part is where an owner tells you what the plan still lacks. In the shared notes, every line with one name and a day was done by that day. The line with two names wasn't, because each of them could wait for the other.

## The other three meetings

The same moves run the rest of an agile team's calendar. Give each meeting one question, and end it when the question has its answer.

| Meeting | The question | Done when |
|---|---|---|
| Stand-up | Who needs whom before tomorrow? | Everyone who is stuck has a name and a time |
| Review | Does it work for the person it was built for? | That person has used it while you watched, and said what they'd change |
| Retrospective | Which one thing do we change? | It has an owner and a day to check whether it helped |

In a stand-up, yesterday matters only where it changes someone's today. In a review, hand over the screen and stay quiet, because applause settles nothing. In a retrospective, start from *the observable version*. A room can argue all afternoon about "the handover was a mess". It can fix "the ticket waited from Monday to Thursday".

## Practice: the message that gets you the floor

**Situation.** Minute two of a planning call. The chief executive has just moved an item to the top. On Monday you saw it fail. Nobody is leaving a gap, so you type in the call's chat.

**First draft (what a careful engineer types).**
"Sorry to jump in, and it may just be the test copy, but I was trying a few things with the override on Monday and saw something a bit odd. Happy to go through it later if useful!"

**What's wrong.** What happened is missing. "Later" gives the room permission to go on.

**Better.**
"On the override: Monday, on the test copy, I moved one truck from two screens and it got two doors. I've seen it once, so I don't know how often it happens. Can we look at that before we add phones?"

**Why it works.** It names the item the room is on and gives one checked fact with its limit. Then it asks for a place in the order, which the chief executive can grant without having been wrong.

**Your turn (5 minutes).** Write yours for a meeting this week: `On [the item]: [what I saw, and when]. [Its limit]. Can we [what you want] before we [the next step]?` Keep it under forty-five words, a target this book sets. Send it in the chat, or say it aloud, item first. **Done means** a colleague who reads it once can say what you saw and what you want the room to do first.

## Practice: one item, said back

**If you do only one, do this one.** Choose one item in a real meeting this week. If you don't run the meeting, tell whoever does what you plan to ask.

**With a person.** Ask three questions, each at its moment:

1. Before the discussion: "Who decides this one?"
2. Before that person speaks, to whoever is closest to the work: "What have you seen that we should know first?"
3. Before the next item, to the owner: "What will you do, by when, and what are you unsure of?"

Watch the third part of the reply. If the owner is unsure of nothing, ask what they will do first tomorrow. **Done means** an hour later you and the owner each write down the decision, the name and the date, separately, and the two match. **Written form.** Send the owner question three after the meeting, and compare their answer with your notes.

## Words to use

- **To sort an item.** "Are we deciding this, or hearing about it?"
- **To hear whoever knows.** "Who worked on this last?" "[Name], you ran it on Monday. What did you see?"
- **To ask what everyone else skipped.** "This may be a basic question. What happens if [it goes wrong]?"
- **When you are the most senior.** "I haven't promised this yet. What should I know before I do?" "I'll go last."
- **To bring the talk back.** "Do we need that to choose, or to do it?"
- **To decide.** "Before we choose: is [rule] a fair way to choose?"
- **To leave it open.** "We can't decide this today. [Name] will bring [the missing fact] by [day]."

### Phrases you will hear

"Let's take this offline" means talking later, without the group: ask who, and by when. "Let's park it" means leaving it for now: ask what would bring it back. "We're aligned" may mean agreement, or only that nobody objected: ask who does what.

## When it backfires

**When they hold the power.** Tyler's questions worked because Lucía let them. Had she said "It goes first", sorting her item aloud would have been a correction in public. Ask once: "Before it goes in, may I tell you what we saw on Monday?" If the answer is no, write the decision down with her name, put the fact beside it, and send both to everyone as the notes. Better, reach her before the meeting, while nothing has been announced.

**When going first is a risk.** Being called on before your manager can feel like a trap, and in some teams it is rude. Ask people privately beforehand, let them write it, or carry the fact yourself with their permission.

**When a decider comes too early.** If the people who must keep a decision haven't been heard, each will decide again, alone, after the meeting. A quick decision the team doesn't follow is slower than another half hour.

**When everything gets sorted.** A choice you could undo by lunch needs no rule and no round. Do it every time and a meeting sounds like a courtroom. Save the read-back for owners: twelve people reciting is a ceremony.

**In a second language.** A fast meeting can close an item while you are still building your sentence. Write it before the call, and ask for each decision to be typed where everyone can see it. If you run the meeting, read the chat aloud before you move on.
