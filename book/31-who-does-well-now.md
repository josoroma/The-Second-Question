# Who does well now

Six habits gain value as drafts get cheap.

In November an assistant wrote Tolva, a small software company in Montevideo, three pages of rules for work done by agents. Seven people then met to turn them into one page they could keep. One person named the question. One asked who had looked. One followed a change to whoever would feel it next. One chose a path before anyone could know. One settled a quarrel. One replaced two words that would have been misread.

You'll hear that meeting; then each habit gets its name. All six are things people do, so you can start any of them this week.

## What a draft leaves out

Ask an assistant for a plan and it arrives whole: headings, reasons, a confident last line. Now look for what didn't arrive. Nothing in the text says whether this was the question worth answering. Nothing says who checked it, or what it sets off once it's running. It doesn't choose when the facts can't be known yet. It hasn't settled anything between the people who must live with it, and it can't tell whether they understood.

A colleague's draft can leave out the same six. What changed is the price. Spend a week on a draft and you'd meet some of them on the way. When it takes a minute, you meet them in the meeting.

You'll hear versions of this elsewhere, so ask what they rest on. People who work with these tools say that producing matters less than it did, and that directing and judging matter more (*reported*: testimony). Many of those who say so build the tools, sell them or teach them. I have an interest too: this book trains judgment. So set the testimony aside and test the reasoning. While people decide, sign and answer for results, somebody has to bring those six things to the table. I'd call that a *reasonable bet*, good reasoning on thin evidence, and I'd give it up if customers stopped asking for a name.

Can't an assistant supply them too? Ask, and it will often hand you ten questions, a page of side effects and two paths with their costs. Take them, because some will be ones you missed. They're drafts as well. Somebody still picks one question, phones the person the change will reach, and hears the engineer say yes.

> **Line to keep:** Answering for a draft means supplying what it left out.

## One meeting, one page

In October, a change written by an agent passed its tests, went in unread and put the wrong labels on warehouse doors for four hours. It changed Tolva's dock scheduler. The doors belonged to Tolva's largest client, Veldhaven, a food wholesaler run from Rotterdam. By November, Tolva's promises about agents sat in four places: the new agreement with Veldhaven, a written apology, a message of Mateo's, and Veldhaven's own access policy. An engineer joining that week would have found none of them.

On Wednesday 4 November, five days before Veldhaven's second site went live, seven people met by video to put them on one page. Tyler, who leads delivery, had given the notes to an assistant Tolva approved. It returned three pages and eleven headings. The first read: "All agent-generated work undergoes rigorous review by qualified personnel."

With Tyler were Tolva's founders: Lucía, the chief executive, and Mateo, who runs technology. Bianca, the product manager, was there, and so was an engineer who reads what the agents write. Diego, the engineer Tolva placed with Veldhaven, joined from Rotterdam. Naomi, the independent data consultant whose forecast feeds the scheduler, had been invited to find whatever the page couldn't support.

Two people who weren't there mattered as much: Claire, who runs operations at Veldhaven, and Otávio, who heads its information security. As you read, mark the line where each habit begins.

Tyler: "It's tidy, and I can't tell what it would have stopped in October. We have an hour. I'd like to leave with one page and one name."

Bianca: "Nobody has ever asked us how we work with agents. Claire asked why she'd believe our Friday list. Yesterday Hugo, who may buy from us, asked who picks up the phone. They both want to know what they can hold us to when an agent's work reaches their doors. Three of these headings answer that. I'd delete eight."

Lucía: "Delete them."

Naomi: "The first one left says every change is checked before it goes live. Did somebody look, or is that what we expect?"

Tyler: "I looked this morning. Seven changes since the rule began, seven names."

Naomi: "Then you've checked the names. What shows that the checking works?"

Mateo: "One test. In October one of Otávio's reviewers hid wrong-door faults in three of six changes. Our reader found two. The third needed tests she'd written herself, from the brief."

Naomi: "I'd print that: one test, one reader, two by reading and the third by her own tests. And the month you'll run it again. 'Rigorous' has to be right every night. This only has to be right about October."

Mateo: "January. Now follow one change through the page. The agents write faster than we can read, so everything waits at the reading. We sign nothing on a Thursday, so on Wednesday evening somebody will want the queue empty and will read faster. A fast read misses more. What it misses comes back as new changes, and the queue grows. The next people to feel it aren't on this call. Otávio's two reviewers would get our whole week on one Thursday."

Bianca: "What breaks it?"

Mateo: "When more is waiting than one day's reading, the agents stop. The reading never speeds up. I have four more effects. This is the one that hurts somebody."

The engineer: "So my name stops the agents. And when something gets through at four in the morning, it's my name again."

Nobody spoke. Tyler didn't move on.

Tyler: "What would your name be promising?"

The engineer: "That it won't break."

Tyler: "Does anybody here need it to promise that?"

Lucía: "No. I need to tell a customer what you read, what you ran and what you couldn't cover."

Tyler: "Then those three lines go under every name, and the phone at night stays where it is today. Could you put your name to that on Monday?"

The engineer: "If it goes on before the agent starts. I don't want to meet it when the work's done."

Tyler: "Mateo, is that yours to say yes to?"

Mateo: "It is. Yes."

Lucía: "Only Veldhaven has this in writing. We could keep it for them and move faster for everybody else, and nobody here knows which one the next customer will want. I choose one page for every customer, small changes included, until the December peak is over. It costs us speed. Two things would change it: a dock waiting on a change that's finished and unread, or a fault that gets past the reading and the tests. After either one, we meet the next morning."

Diego: "Then two words need work. On a dock, 'signed' is what happens to a delivery note: somebody counted the pallets and they were all there. The supervisors I work with would read 'signed' and stop looking. So the docks get 'read by', 'run against' and 'not covered'. Otávio's reviewers know what a signature covers, so they keep the word. And unless we say what a 'change' is, an agent will usually guess. Its first instruction should be: 'Put the reader's name in your first line. If you have no name, stop and ask.'"

Mateo said the page back, and Bianca corrected one word. The assistant had needed less than a minute for its three pages. The one page took fifty-two.

### The page they kept

- Before an agent starts, one engineer's name goes on the work. That engineer reads every change the agent writes, small ones included, runs it against tests a person wrote, and signs it.
- Under the signature: what was read, what was run, what wasn't covered. The docks see those three lines without the word "signed".
- The agent's first instruction: "Put the reader's name in your first line. If you have no name, stop and ask."
- At Veldhaven, the signature reaches the two reviewers one working day ahead, and one objection stops the change. We sign nothing on a Thursday, and changes run by day.
- A change that needs new access waits ten working days.
- When more is waiting than one day's reading, the agents stop.
- Every Friday, Claire gets the list of what went live, with the name of who read it.
- This rests on one test: six changes, three hidden faults. Reading found two; the reader's own tests found the third. We run it again in January.
- It holds until the December peak is over. A dock waiting on a finished, unread change, or a fault that gets past both, reopens it the next morning.
- Mateo answers for this page.

The next six sections take the habits one at a time: the line where each begins, how it sounds in other rooms, its trap and where you trained it. Each ends with a practice small enough for this week.

## The framer: what is the question?

The framer decides what the question is before anyone answers it. On the call, the habit begins at "Nobody has ever asked us". Bianca found one question under two real ones, and eight headings had nothing left to do. In April she copied the team's board into a report and let each reader pick a meaning. Hugo does it from the buyer's side: he read a polished proposal twice, and what he asked about was the phone.

**In a meeting.** "What will be different once we've answered this?" "I think the question is [this one]. Is that the one you asked?" On a sales call: "Before I price it, what happens on the day it works?"

**The trap.** Framing that never ends. You'll recognize it by "But is that really the question?", asked a third time while somebody waits. Frame once, then help answer.

**Built from** the point sentence and the listener sketch. Go back to *Know what you think*, *Who is listening*, *Questions and common ground*, *The brief* and *Saying no and pushing back*. Repeat *one no this week*.

### Practice: the question behind a request

Take a request you've already started on. Write the question it answers, then two others the same person might have meant. Send all three: "Which of these should I answer?" **Done means** the reply picks one, or gives you a fourth.

## The calibrator: how sure are we?

The calibrator says how sure we are, and tests a claim before the team acts on it. It begins at "Did somebody look?" Naomi didn't doubt Tyler. She separated what he'd checked, the names, from what nobody had shown yet, and gave the test its real size. In June her first report to Veldhaven held four hedges and no number. Claire's "Why would I believe the list?" is the same habit on the client's side.

**In a meeting.** "Who looked, and when?" "I'd act on this for [one thing]. For [the other], not yet." In a code review: "The tests pass. Who wrote them, and from what?"

**The trap.** Sizing everything, until the room leaves with five limits and no answer. The sign is "It depends" with nothing after it. Give the claim you'd act on first, then its limit.

**Built from** the sized claim and the second-question test. Go back to *How sure are you? Say that*, *The explanation*, *The update* and *Arguing well*. Repeat *one claim at its real size*.

### Practice: a number and its source

Find a number your team repeats without saying where it came from. Give ten minutes to three facts: who measured it, when, and on how many cases. Put them beside the number, where the team will see them. **Done means** somebody who wasn't there can say what it doesn't cover.

## The system reader: what does it set off?

The system reader asks what a change sets off after its first effect, and who feels it next. It begins at "Now follow one change". Mateo's first effect was the one everybody wanted: changes get read. He followed it to the constraint, around a loop, and out to two people who weren't on the call. In May he wrote three pages of true facts, all the same size. Now he had five effects and spoke about one. His remedy, to stop starting work while the queue is full, is an old one in software teams. In August, Otávio asked the same of a login: "What it can change. At which sites. How fast I can cut it off."

**In a meeting.** "Where will this wait?" "Who gets more work on the day we get less?" In planning: "If this goes first, who stands still?"

**The trap.** The map of everything, where the tenth arrow is as thick as the first and nothing ships. It sounds like "It's more complicated than that", and then stops there. Bring one effect, one person it reaches and one thing that breaks the loop.

**Built from** next-question order, asked of a change. Go back to *Give it a shape*, *The explanation*, *The meeting* and *The brief*. *Seeing the system* takes it further. Repeat *hear the plan first*.

### Practice: the next person

Pick one change that ships this week. Write its first effect, then the name of whoever feels it next. Ask that person, "What will you have to do differently on the day?" **Done means** one line in the change's note came from them.

## The strategist: which path?

The strategist chooses among paths when nobody knows the answer, and says what would change the choice. It begins at "Only Veldhaven has this in writing". Lucía named the faster path, said nobody could know, and chose. Then she said what the choice costs and which two events would reopen it. In May, her word for a date was "on track". Renata, Tolva's first investor, asked for the habit's second half in August: "How will I know, before the 28th, if the 28th is moving?"

**In a meeting.** "Nobody knows this yet. Until [day], I'd do [this]." "If we're wrong, what will we see first?" To an investor: "Here's the path we turned down, at its best."

**The trap.** Loving the decision. The sign arrives and the choice stays, because it has your name on it. The quieter form is calling every guess a bet, so that nothing you say can be wrong. Ask somebody else to watch for the sign.

**Built from** the preparation sheet and the sized claim. Go back to *How sure are you? Say that*, *Steady under pressure*, *The pitch*, *Negotiation* and *Pressure and how to resist it*. *Leading when nobody knows the answer* takes it further. Repeat *a page and a person*.

### Practice: a choice with an end date

Take a decision that's waiting for a fact nobody will have in time, and fill in: `Until [day], we [choice]. It costs us [cost]. If we see [sign], we [next step].` One filled in: "Until Friday the 27th, we answer every support message by hand. It costs us two mornings a week. If we see the same question five times, we write the page." Send yours to whoever has to live with it. **Done means** they can tell a third person what would change it.

## The problem-solver: what will hold?

The problem-solver takes a mess with people in it to a decision that lasts. It begins at "What would your name be promising?" Somebody kind could have soothed that engineer and moved on. Tyler asked, and the quarrel turned out to be about what a signature means. He took the yes from the person with the most to do, then checked whose condition it was to grant. He also left one subject off the page: how much to hand to agents at all. Lucía and Mateo had put that conversation off since July, and no page could hold it for them. In August, Tyler let a line about reading go into the notes with two names and no date.

Wesley, who runs procurement at Veldhaven, named a missing part the same way in October: "Your contract has a price for everything except being late."

**In a meeting.** "Who has to do the most if we agree to this?" "Is that yours to say yes to?" In a retrospective: "We've decided this before. What didn't we write down?"

**The trap.** Peace before the decision. Everybody nods, and the person with the work hasn't spoken. A second trap waits behind it: every quarrel gets sent to you, and people stop settling their own.

**Built from** sort first and the read-back. Go back to *Thinking on your feet*, *The meeting*, *Difficult people*, *Arguing well* and *Negotiation*. Repeat *one item, said back*.

### Practice: the decision that came back

Find something your team has decided twice. Ask the person with the most to do, "What did we leave out the first time?" Rewrite the decision with one name, a day and the missing part. **Done means** that person says it back, and a colleague who wasn't there can tell you what's new in it.

## The translator: what will they act on?

The translator carries meaning across a boundary intact: between disciplines, between a client and a team, between people and machines. It begins at "Then two words need work". Diego took the page's words to a dock, a security team and an agent, and asked what each would hear. Two of the three would have heard something else. In June he answered "It should be fine" and left the listener to supply the rest.

**In a meeting.** "When they read that, what will they do?" "What do you call it on your side?" On a client call: "I'll say it in your words, and you tell me if it's still true."

**The trap.** Becoming the only way across, so both sides bring you everything and stop asking each other. Or softening a sentence until it's easy to hear and no longer true. Leave them a word they share, and get out of the way.

**Built from** the observable version, the read-back and the working story. Go back to *The exact word*, *Listening people can feel*, *Story for people who think in systems*, *The brief*, *On the page* and *Across borders*. Repeat *one sentence you misheard*.

### Practice: the word they act on

Choose a message you sent this week to somebody outside your field. Ask them which word they'd act on, and what they'd do. Rewrite the sentence around their answer. **Done means** they tell you their next step, and it's the one you meant.

## What each owes the other five

Any one of the six, alone for an hour, would have ruined that meeting. Each habit has a debt to the rest.

- **The framer** owes them a question they can start on, and an hour when the framing stops.
- **The calibrator** owes them a claim they can act on today, limit included.
- **The system reader** owes them the one effect that matters, with a name beside it.
- **The strategist** owes them the best case for the path not taken.
- **The problem-solver** owes them the disagreement, out loud, before the agreement.
- **The translator** owes each side the other's real words, including the unwelcome ones.

When a meeting stalls, look for the debt nobody has paid. It may be yours.

## Practice: the last two weeks

**If you do only one, do this one.** It takes fifteen minutes, your calendar and your sent messages from the last two weeks. Nothing here is scored, and no row gives you a type.

| Habit | Look for |

| The framer | A question you named that changed what got worked on |
| The calibrator | A claim you tested, or sent with its basis and its limit |
| The system reader | A person you warned, because a change would reach them next |
| The strategist | A choice made without knowing, with the sign that would change it |
| The problem-solver | A disagreement that ended in a name and a day, and still holds |
| The translator | A sentence you changed for one listener, who then did what you meant |

1. For each row, write one dated thing you said or wrote that somebody else could confirm. If you can't find one, leave it blank.
2. Ask one colleague: "In the last two weeks, when did something I said change what you did?" Put the answer in its row. If it surprises you, trust it over your own list: I'd call that row the habit you're growing into.
3. Read the blanks. Some belong to a teammate, and some weeks don't call for them. Circle the one whose absence cost somebody something, and put its practice in next week's calendar.

**Done means** your colleague could check every entry against a day, and one practice from this chapter has a date.

## When it backfires

- **The names become labels.** "He's a system reader" excuses him from deciding, and "I'm not a strategist" excuses you. Use the names for acts: "Who's framing this?"
- **Somebody senior holds the question.** Reframing a chief executive's question in public can sound like a refusal to answer. Answer first, then ask: "Is it this you need, or [the other question]?" Size their number in private, or in writing. If the choice is theirs, bring two paths and what would change each.
- **You work alone.** Six habits are a lot to hold about your own draft on a Tuesday night. Borrow one: a customer who'll tell you what they heard, or a friend who asks what would change your mind.
- **A room that reads questions as doubt.** Say what the question is for: "I ask so we can ship it."
- **In a second language.** Each habit fits in one short question, so prepare the one this meeting needs and write it at the top of your notes. If the talk moves too fast, put it in the chat.
