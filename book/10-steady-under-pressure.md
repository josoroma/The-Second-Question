# Steady under pressure

What do you do in the last ten minutes before a call you dread?

Lucía had three files open and was reaching for a fourth. In ten minutes, at eleven in Montevideo and four in the afternoon in Rotterdam, Veldhaven's chief executive would call. He had asked for twenty minutes on a Thursday in June to hear the date from her, not from a summary.

She is co-founder and chief executive of Tolva, a company of fourteen people in Montevideo whose scheduler tells each arriving truck which warehouse door to use. Tolva's largest client, Veldhaven, is a food wholesaler with three distribution centers run from Rotterdam. The first of them, site one, was due to go live in September.

The files were the contract, her notes from the last board call and Mateo's design note from May. Mateo, her co-founder and Tolva's chief technology officer, had written there what the date rested on. She closed all three. Anything new she read now, she would have no time to fit in.

She took a yellow note and wrote two lines on it:

"He should plan site one for September, because either result of his security review still lands in that month."

"Not the 14th without the 'if': the review decides it."

She stuck it under her camera, where reading it would look like looking at him. On her pad, for herself only, she wrote one more line: "This call decides what he tells his board about site one. It does not decide whether Veldhaven stays."

Then she stood at the window and said her first sentence aloud, twice. The first time it came out fast. The second time it came out whole. She also said aloud her answer to the push she expected. If he wanted the 14th with no condition, he would get the 28th as the date and the 14th as a chance. At a minute to eleven she drank some water, let one slow breath out and joined.

He came straight to it. "Claire tells me you have a date for me."

"Site one goes live on 14 September, if your security review passes in one round. If it needs a second round, the 28th. I'll know by 20 August."

"I can't take an 'if' to my board. Is it the 14th?"

The 14th was one word away, and saying it would end the silence. Her eyes went to the second line under the camera.

"Then take the 28th to your board," Lucía said. "If the review passes in one round, you get two weeks back, and you'll hear it from me first."

"In May your company told Claire the integration was on track," he said, "and nobody on your side could tell her what the date depended on. Why should my board believe this one?"

It was a fair question, and it stung. The May summary had been Lucía's, and Claire, who runs operations at Veldhaven, had waited a day for the answer to what the date depended on. Lucía heard herself begin, "That summary went out before Mateo had...", and the rest of the sentence was gone. For two seconds, perhaps three, she said nothing. Then she looked at the first line of the note.

"You're right about May," she said. "I sent that summary before I knew what the date rested on. This time I know. It rests mostly on your security review, and either result still gives you site one in September."

"And the exact day?"

"On 20 August. I'll call you myself."

"Then my board hears September, and I hear the day on the twentieth. I'll hold you to that."

The call took eleven of his twenty minutes, and that afternoon she sent him the date in writing, condition included. He had no way to check the review that day. What he could check was whether her date changed when he pushed, and it did not. Choosing a path while nobody knows the answer, and saying what would change it, is the strategist's habit, the thinker type Lucía is growing toward.

## Decide before anyone pushes

A steady call is mostly decided before it starts. Lucía lost no facts that morning. She nearly lost the size of her claim, and for a few seconds her place. Those were the two things the push went after, and you can protect both while nobody is pushing. In the last ten minutes, write down the sentence you will come back to, the one thing you will not agree to, and what the call can and cannot decide. Then say your first sentence, the answer they came to hear, aloud until it comes out whole. Ten minutes is a target this book sets: enough for three lines and one sentence said twice.

The point sentence, from *Know what you think*, is the way back when you lose the thread, the moment your next sentence is simply gone. The second line, what you will not agree to, is a decision made while you are calm, so that when the push comes there is nothing left to decide. The stakes line gives the call what a sized claim has in *How sure are you? Say that*, a limit: what the call decides, and where that stops. Lucía feared the call could cost Tolva its largest client. Written down, the stakes shrank to what one man would tell his board about one site.

None of this will steady your hands, and it doesn't need to. A voice that shakes can still carry a sentence it has already said twice. And if your attention keeps running to the worst version of the call, three short lines can give it somewhere better to go.

> **Line to keep:** Write it down while you are calm, and you can find it again when you are not.

## Practice: your last ten minutes

Run these steps before your next hard moment this week. It might be a price you must say aloud, a date someone will lean on, a no to someone senior or a review of your own design. **If you do only one, do this one.**

1. Close everything you were reading. There is no time left to fit anything new in.
2. Write your point sentence where your eyes will be during the call: on a note under the camera, or at the top of the page in front of you.
3. Under it, write the one thing you will not agree to, and why.
4. For yourself, one line on the stakes: `This call decides [one thing]. It does not decide [what I fear].`
5. Say your first sentence, the answer they came for (Lucía's was her date and its condition), aloud twice. If a colleague is near, ask them to say the push you fear most, at a moment you don't choose, and answer it. If you lose your place, look at the note and say the point again in plain words.
6. In the last minute, drink some water, let one slow breath out and read the note once more.

**Done means** a colleague who reads only your note can say what you are asking for and what you will not agree to.

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| The fear written as the stakes | Put the fear after "It does not decide" |
| A rehearsal of the whole call | Rehearse the first sentence and the hardest push |

If you won't have the ten minutes, write the note the evening before, stop reading early enough to sleep, and keep the last step for the minute before you join.

## When the next sentence is gone

The thread can go even with the note in place: a sharp line, or a word that won't come when you reach for it. When it goes, stop, look at the note and say the point again in plain words, as if for the first time. Then carry on from there, or ask what they need.

You can say what is happening while you look: "Let me come back to the point."

If your point sentence no longer answers what they just asked, don't force it back in. Say that you didn't prepare for that question, and say when you will have an answer.

## The sting can wait

A line that stings can pull you toward a reply about yourself. Lucía's began as one: the history of the summary, everything that would make May sound less bad. It answered a question he had not asked: whose fault was May? He had asked why this date should be believed. Her answer admitted May in four words, because he was right about it, and spent the rest on what the date depended on.

If a sting is unfair, you don't have to settle that on the call. Say "I see that differently, and I'll answer it in writing," and answer what was asked.

**Spar with an AI.** Rehearse the push and the sting before they reach you.

```text
Play the chief executive of a client company. I lead the vendor team building your new system, and I'm calling to give you [a go-live date / a price / a decision]. You want it firm, for your board. Each time I attach a condition, press me to drop it. Once, when I don't expect it, raise this past mistake of mine in one sharp sentence: [your mistake]. Keep your replies short. After six exchanges, leave the role and tell me whether my condition survived, which sentence I said only to end the discomfort, and where I lost my point.
```

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## Practice: a sharp comment, answered in the morning

**Situation.** 23:40 on a Tuesday, two days before a client's release. Their architect comments on your team's pull request: "Second broken build this week. Does anyone on your side run the tests before you merge?"

**First draft (written at 23:46).**
"To be fair, both builds broke because your team upgraded the date library on Monday and nobody told us. We run every test before we merge. I'll look into it tomorrow."

**What's wrong.** It answers the sting first: before he learns whether his release is blocked, he learns whose fault the week was. "We run every test before we merge" is bigger than the facts, because none of those tests ran against the upgraded library. "I'll look into it tomorrow" gives him nothing to plan around.

**Better (sent at 7:50).**
"Your release isn't blocked: I reverted our merge last night, and main has passed every build since. Both breaks had one cause: our branches still used the date library from before Monday's upgrade. So yes, we run every test before we merge, but only on our own branch. From today we also run them on the latest main. The fixed change passes there and can be merged once you approve it."

**Why it works.** It opens with his release. The revert went in that night; the reply went out in the morning. The cause takes one sentence and blames nobody. His question gets a true answer at its real size: every test, but only on the branch. Who upgraded what can wait for the review of both breaks. The sting gets no reply at all.

**Your turn (5 minutes).** Take the last work message that stung you. Write the reply you wanted to send. Cross out every sentence that defends you or answers the sting, then add the answer to the question inside the message, or to the need behind it. Read what is left the next morning before you send anything like it. **Done means** a colleague who reads your new reply can say what happened and what happens next, and can find no sentence whose only job is to defend you.

## Words to use

**To hold a condition.** "I'd rather give you the condition now than an apology in August." "The date and the condition come together."

**When the thread goes.** "I've lost my place. The point is this: [your point sentence]."

**When a line stings.** "You're right about [what was true]. Here is what's different this time." "I see that differently, and I'll answer it in writing. On your question: [the answer]."

**To close.** "If you hear anything new about [the review], tell me, and I'll tell you what it does to the date." "I'll send you the date and its condition in writing today."

### Phrases you will hear

"Put a stake in the ground" (commit to one date or one position). "I'll hold you to that" (I expect you to keep this promise, and I will check). "Don't sugarcoat it" (tell me the bad part plainly).

## When it backfires

**When the note becomes a script.** Read aloud word for word, the point sentence can sound read, and coming back to it again and again can start to sound like dodging. Use the note to find your place, then say the point in your own words.

**When they can overrule you.** Veldhaven held the contract, and Lucía could still keep her condition, because the date was hers to give. If someone above you says "Tell them the 14th," say the condition once, in writing, to the person who decides, and let them decide with it in view. *Saying no and pushing back* has the rest of that route.

**When saying you lost your place costs more than it should.** In some rooms "Let me come back to the point" is ordinary; in others it can sound unprepared. If you can't tell which room you're in, skip the announcement and say the point again.

**When there is no time at all.** A senior person stops you in the corridor with the hard question. The routine shrinks to one step: say your point sentence to yourself once, then answer.

**When it is more than nerves.** Nerves that stop you working, before every hard call or for days ahead of one, are beyond what a book can fix. Take them to a professional you trust.

**In a second language.** Pressure may thin out a second language first, and numbers, dates and names are the words you can least afford to build on the spot. Put the ones you will need on the note, written the way you will say them, so that under the push you read them instead of building them.
