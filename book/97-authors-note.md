# Author's note

## What I got wrong

I've been an engineer long enough to be called senior. Much of that time my desk has been inside a client's building, near the people who use what I ship. The room that mattered was a review there one April. I brought two pages on splitting the billing service in three. An assistant had drafted them from my notes, and they read better than anything I would have written that fast.

The client's head of operations asked one thing: "What will my team be able to do in June that it can't do today?" I answered with the architecture. They asked again, more slowly. I didn't know. I had read every line without deciding what the work was for.

The money went elsewhere. The design was sound, and it came back in September with that team's problem in its first sentence. I wanted a book that trains you for that question, so I had one made.

## What I use, and what I still find hard

I use *The brief*. For this book's art I gave the agents three examples and said which one wins when they disagree. Only I could choose that, and an agent left without it usually guesses. With code, I ask for the plan first and look for a step I didn't expect.

*Say less* is still hard. Engineers are paid to see the exception, and I say it aloud. I once told a director the release was safe, heard the quiet, and filled it with how we would roll back. They spent the rest of the call on the rollback.

## What would change my mind

The central claim is that smooth text costs little now, so people test your understanding with their second question. I hold it at medium. It is reasoning, and nobody has tested it where you work.

Two records would move me. In the first, the people I work with act on polished pages for a year without asking what they rest on, and the work goes no worse. In the second, a machine answers the second question live, for whoever is on the call, and the decisions come out as good. Either one would mean this book trains a skill the room has stopped needing.

## About the examples

The life examples in this book are a compilation of hypothetical situations, put together to show a technique. Any likeness to a real person or company is unintended. The stories in this note are hypothetical too, and so is the engineer in them. The instruction about the art is real, and so are the published studies.
