# With a machine in the room

*On the page: decide before it drafts*

An assistant will hand you a finished message before you have decided what it is for. This part trained you to decide first.

In June, Bianca, Tolva's product manager, pasted a month of requests from Veldhaven's site managers into an assistant Tolva had approved for client work. She asked it for a message to Claire, who runs operations at Veldhaven, Tolva's largest client. Seconds later she had seventeen requests under five tidy headings. It was the message Bianca used to build by hand, every request forwarded and none chosen, only faster. Claire had one decision to make, and the list never said which. The assistant had no way to know what Tolva's engineers could finish before September. Bianca did. She deleted the list and wrote the point sentence first (what one named person should do, and the one reason):

"Claire should pick one of the two changes her site managers asked for most, because before September we can finish only one of them at site one."

Claire chose the same afternoon.

> **Line to keep:** The less you decide before the draft, the more the draft decides for you.

## Before the draft, and after it

**Before.** Write the point sentence with its limit. Add the listener sketch (what this person knows, has to decide and stands to lose) and the questions they will ask next, in order. Do it on paper or in your own file, and keep that page. Only you know what you mean, and only one question to your listener can check the sketch. When you ask for the draft, give it the page and tell it to ask you for any fact it lacks. If you don't, assume it will guess.

**After.** Put every label it chose through the observable version (what would a recording show?), and check that each assumption you gave it still sounds like one. Any claim you didn't give it is *told*, at best, until you check it. Then put the draft through the second-question test, with the page you kept beside it. The fifth question, *Who answers for it?*, is already settled: you answer for what goes out under your name.

> **Research says:** In one experiment, two-person crews were as likely as people working alone to miss a problem their automated system failed to flag. A review of such studies found this kind of miss in novices and experts, and training alone did not remove it. These studies tested automated aids, not assistants that write, so applying them to drafts is the book's inference, and so is the safeguard of writing your page first.

## Practice: two drafts, one point

**If you do only one, do this one.**

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Pick a message you must send this week that asks someone to decide or act.

1. On paper, write its point sentence with its limit, then the listener sketch, and turn the page over.
2. Give an assistant only your notes and ask it for the message.
3. Write the point its draft chose in one sentence: to whom, asking what, and why.
4. Turn your page back and mark each difference: listener, ask, reason, a dropped limit, an added fact.
5. Ask again with your page, or write the message yourself.

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

**Done means** a colleague can see both points side by side with every difference marked, and the message you send opens with your point sentence.

## When it backfires

Sometimes the draft's point is better than yours. Take it once you can say why and it passes the second-question test; then it is your decision.

When someone senior wants the message in ten minutes, skip the comparison and keep the point sentence. It is the decision you will be asked about.

In a second language, an assistant can help with the English once the point is decided. Then check that the polished English kept your ask and your limits.

## Record again

This part ends where the book began: sixty seconds, out loud, on what you are working on and why it matters. Write your point sentence first, then put the paper away and record. No assistant can say this minute for you.

Score it as you scored your first two takes:

1. **Sentences before the point.** How many came before the first one that says why the work matters? If none does, write "never".
2. **Hedges with no basis.** Each *probably*, *I think*, *should* or *hopefully* with no reason after it.
3. **One example.** One case that shows why it matters: a person, a number or a thing that happened. Background doesn't count.
4. **The ending.** An ask or a next step: yes or no.
5. **Said back.** One listener hears it once and writes your point in one sentence; an assistant given the transcript can stand in. Compare their sentence with your point sentence.

The filler count is optional; pauses, restarts and searching for a word never count, and if you stutter, work in a second language or pause to think, skip it. You can also write for three minutes without stopping, on the same prompt, and run the same checks. Put the results beside your two takes from *Start here*: your before and after, not anyone's norm.

*Safe practice: record only yourself, or others only with their consent and within your employer's rules.*
