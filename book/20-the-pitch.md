# The pitch

"So, what does your company do?"

The candidate had read the job ad, and the question was fair. Investors and customers ask it too. An assistant had drafted the ad, and it could have described almost any company. One Tuesday in the middle of August, Mateo, who runs technology at Tolva, a small company in Montevideo, was asking her to become its first support engineer.

He had a sentence ready, the one his co-founder, Lucía, used with investors. "We're a dock-scheduling platform for food distribution," he said, "and before the December peak we go live at three warehouses with our largest client."

She took a moment. "So three warehouses start in three months, and I'd be all of support."

"Yes," said Mateo. Then he explained, for eleven minutes, how the scheduler re-plans when a truck is late.

"Thank you," she said. "I should tell you that I have another offer, in a team of nine. I promised them an answer on Friday."

Nothing Mateo said was wrong. He had given her the news investors like best, and for her it was the worst news.

## A sentence that stays, a reason that moves

A pitch has two jobs. It lets a stranger picture what you do, and it gives this listener a reason to decide. Build the two separately. The sentence that says what you do stays the same for everyone. The reason after it is the one this listener needs, so it changes with each person.

> **Line to keep:** Of all your reasons, lead with theirs.

Start with the sentence that stays. "Dock-scheduling platform" shows nothing, so the candidate pictured the only thing she could: three start dates. Write what a recording would show (the observable version, from *The exact word*). Tolva's recording shows trucks, doors and a clock: "We decide which truck goes to which warehouse door, and when."

| Instead of | Say | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "A patient-engagement solution" | "We text clinic patients the day before, and refill the slots they cancel." | A company: a message, a slot, a day |
| "Predictive arrival insights" | "I forecast when a warehouse's trucks will reach its gate." | One person's work, and who uses it |
| "An onboarding initiative" | "A new engineer will release a change in their first week." | A project, as someone will see it |

Saying what a customer can see is old advice. The harder part is leaving the sentence alone once it works: learn it, and use it in the ad, on the site and on the call. If your company does three things, choose the one a customer would miss first. The other two can wait for a question.

Then find the reason that moves. Fill in the listener sketch for one person: what they know, what they have to decide, what they stand to lose. Write your point sentence, `[this listener] should [believe or do this], because [the one reason]`, and take the reason from the last two lines of the sketch.

Don't take it from what convinced you. Mateo has stayed with Tolva's problem for three years because one late truck can undo a whole morning's plan, and he finds that beautiful. It explains why he is there. It gives a support engineer no reason to turn down a team of nine.

## What a bad week looks like

That evening Mateo could fill in two lines. She knew the ad, three dates and a team of one. She had to choose between that and a team of nine, by Friday. The third line he left blank. He might have written "her evenings". He sent her a question instead: "Before we talk again: what does a bad week look like where you are now?"

"I open a ticket," she wrote back. "The customer asks every day, and every day I apologize. I've been apologizing for one export since March."

Ask about the work, and listen for the loss. She could carry three warehouses; she was tired of apologizing for faults she wasn't allowed to fix. So the reason that was hers, *you can fix what you hear about*, was easy to write, and at Tolva it was false. The ad said support would "triage and escalate". Mateo had to make it true before he could say it, so he gave the job the same access to the code that every engineer has, under the same review. When the reason they need is false, you have two honest choices: change the work, or leave the reason out.

On Thursday he called. "We decide which truck goes to which warehouse door, and when. You'd be the first to hear when we decide wrong, and you'd have the access to fix it."

Then a working story, built backward from her decision:

"In July, a shift lead at our client wrote to me: 'Your nightly email says the flour comes at one in the morning. It comes at six.' The email showed Montevideo time. The fix took me ten minutes. She waited four days for it, and by then her shift had stopped opening the email. That month the client's sites sent me twenty-six messages. Nine needed a change in the code, and those would be yours. The other seventeen needed a clear answer. No site is live yet, so I can't tell you what a live month looks like. Would you want those nine?"

"What happens when two sites go wrong on the same night?" she asked. It was her second question, and no ad had an answer to it.

"You take one and I take the other, until we hire a second person. I don't know when that will be, so I can't give you a date. My nights I can put in the offer."

"Put the access in the offer too," she said. On Friday she signed it.

Mateo used one proof, his strongest, with its limit attached. A second, weaker one can feel like more support. Cut it.

> **Research says:** In two experiments about everyday causes, people given weak evidence for an outcome judged it less likely than people given none. In a later online experiment, this happened to listeners who expected the speaker's strongest evidence, and not to those who expected hedging. That a pitch audience expects your strongest, and that a weak proof hurts beside a good one, is our reasoning, untested here.

## Words Renata can carry

Late in August, Renata, Tolva's first investor, offered Lucía an introduction to a partner at another fund: "Send me something I can forward."

Renata couldn't say yes for that fund. She could only carry the words. Before you pitch, ask whether this listener can decide. If they can't, get to the person who can, or write lines that hold up when someone else delivers them. And size the request: before a first meeting, an investor decides only whether an hour is worth it.

"Tolva's software decides which truck goes to which warehouse door, and when. Three warehouses start on it between September and November, so if the dates hold, their December logs will show whether it works at full size. An hour now lets you say what you'd need to see in them. So far: one pilot dock, six weeks, average wait per truck down from about fifty minutes to about thirty, by the client's gate log. No whole warehouse has run on it yet. The first starts on 28 September, if a second security review passes."

The partner wrote back: "The three peak weeks, by site, from the client's own log. When could I have those?" She had started deciding.

Change the reason for each listener as often as you like, as long as they could meet and agree on the facts.

## What to admit, and when

You have been told to be open about what is hard, because people trust someone who admits things. You have also been told never to show doubt to a person who is judging you.

The first is right about anyone who will live with the flaw. Mateo's four days would have met her in her first week, and hearing it from him let her weigh it. The second is right about someone with few facts and someone else to convince: one confession can become most of what they know, and all of what they repeat.

So choose by who is listening. With people who will work beside you, say something of your own early, the flaw included. With investors, clients and bosses, show competence first, then the admission, and make it a limit of what you know. Lucía gave the gate log, then "No whole warehouse has run on it yet." A partner can check that, and watch for the day it changes. "We're honestly a little overwhelmed" gives her a worry about Lucía, with nothing to check.

## Practice: the request to finance

**Situation.** You lead a platform team. On Friday the head of finance closes next quarter's budget, and she has asked every team for one short message: what, how much, why this.

**First draft (what a careful engineer writes).**
"We'd like a read replica for reporting. Analytics queries currently hit the primary database, which isn't how anyone would design it today. A replica is the standard pattern, it's low risk and easy to reverse, the team knows it well, and it sets us up for growth. The cost is modest, about 1,400 euros a month. Glad to go through the design."

**What's wrong.** Every line is accurate, and it answers a question she didn't ask: is this good engineering? Hers was: why this, out of everything on my list? Six reasons, and none of them touches her month. "Modest" is hers to judge.

**Better.**
"On the last day of each month, the finance reports and our customers' orders share one database, and the orders wait. At the last three month-ends, checkout took more than ten seconds for two to four hours; I read that from our logs. I'd like a second copy of the database, for reports only: 1,400 euros a month, from October. It would take the reports out of checkout's way. Whether your reports would also run faster, I haven't tested. Can you give me a yes or no by Friday?"

She replies: "And if it's no?"

"Then I'd run the reports overnight. That costs nothing, and your team would have them at seven instead of on request. If you can live with that on the last day of the month, no is a good answer."

**Why it works.** It opens with something anyone at the checkout could see. It gives the one reason that falls in her month, with one proof and its limit. The price has no adjective. A budget holder is always choosing against something else, so the second answer tells her what a no would cost and who would pay it.

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Take something you need money, people or time for. List your reasons and cross out each one you could give only to a colleague. Write the message with one reason, its proof and its limit, then your answer to "And if it's no?" **Done means** someone outside your field reads it once and can say what is being bought, what the claim rests on and what a no would cost.

## Practice: one sentence, one listener

**With a person.** **If you do only one, do this one.** Say the sentence that stays to someone outside your field and ask: "If you stood there, what would you see?" Change it until their answer is right.

Then choose one person whose yes you need this month. Write their three lines, leave the last one blank, and ask them one question about their own work to fill it in. Say your sentence and their reason in under thirty seconds, a target this book sets. **Untimed form.** Send both in a message.

Now listen for the next question. "What is it, exactly?" means the sentence failed. "Why would I?" means the reason was still yours. A question about cost, dates, proof or limits means they have started deciding. **Done means** you have their next question written down, word for word, and it is one of that last kind.

## Words to use

- **What you do.** "If you stood in [place], you'd see [what]."
- **To find their reason.** "What does a bad week look like where you are now?" "What else are you choosing between?"
- **To find who decides.** "Who else has to agree, and what will they ask you?"
- **The proof.** "The strongest thing I can show you is [proof]. It doesn't cover [limit]."
- **An admission, upward.** "We haven't shown [what] yet. We'll know by [when]."
- **The graceful exit.** "If this isn't yours to take on, who should I ask?"

### Phrases you will hear

- "What's your moat?" (What would stop someone from copying you?)
- "What's the traction?" (What shows that people want it: contracts, use, renewals?)
- "Give me the elevator pitch." (Say it in half a minute.)

## When it backfires

**When you have no proof.** Say so, and offer the test: "Nobody uses it yet. Here is what we'd measure in the first month, and what would make us stop." A weak proof dressed as a strong one fails at the second question.

**When two listeners share the room.** Use the reason of the person who decides, and tell the other one afterward what it means for them. Two reasons at once sound like a list.

**When they hold the power.** If a senior person cuts in, give the request and stop; the reason can wait for their question. If the answer is no, a second reason sounds like pressure. Ask what would have to change, and when you may ask again.

**When asking is read as haste.** In some companies a request in the first meeting is welcome; in others it costs you the second one. Ask whoever introduced you how decisions get made there, and put the request in writing afterward.

**In a second language.** Learn the sentence that stays word for word, and say it until it needs no attention. Then all of your attention is free for the question that follows it.
