# Negotiation

Wesley's agenda had three lines, and Tolva would pay for every one of them.

- Site one, two weeks late: 36,000 euros.
- Night of 6 October: 10,000 euros.
- Monthly fee from January: minus 10 percent.

Wesley runs procurement at Veldhaven, the Rotterdam food wholesaler that is Tolva's largest client. His agenda reached Montevideo on a Monday late in October, for a call on Thursday. Lucía, Tolva's chief executive, could not dispute the events. Tolva's scheduler, which sends trucks to their doors, had started at site one on 28 September instead of the 14th. On 6 October, a change that a coding agent wrote mislabeled doors there for four hours.

Her first answer was ready. "Thanks, Wesley. We want to put this right. Would five percent help?"

Then she read it as Wesley would. Five percent of what, and why five? She had picked it because it was smaller than ten. It would cost Tolva 13,500 euros every year, it rested on nothing, and it asked for nothing. He hadn't even pushed yet.

She took out a sheet of paper.

## Judge it in December

Five percent would have bought Lucía a calmer Monday. The agreement gets judged later, by the people who have to carry it out, in December, when Veldhaven's peak starts.

So decide on one page, before anyone pushes, what you will do without a deal, what you want, and what each thing is worth to both of you. Use the conversation to find trades. Then agree only to terms that both sides can still carry out once the work has started.

> **Line to keep:** A good deal leaves each side enough to keep its word.

That holds in both directions. Give away what you need in order to deliver, and you have promised something you can't pay for. Take what they need, and you are depending on someone who can't pay for it either.

You don't have to be the hardest person on the call, or the fastest in English. You have to know your page. Write it well, and the hardest decisions of the call are made before it starts.

What follows is the page, then one negotiation from its first line to its last, then five you are likely to meet. The headings name moments. Go to yours.

## One page, before anyone pushes

The tool is **the preparation sheet**, one page before any negotiation:

- what I do with no deal;
- where I would walk, and why;
- what I aim for, and why it is defensible;
- my guess at their three;
- the issues and what each is worth to each side;
- who decides on their side;
- what I will not do;
- and the test: will both sides still keep this once the work has started?

Much of it is standard in negotiation teaching, where the first three lines are called the alternative, the walk-away point and the target. The lines to protect when you are in a hurry are the last two. They are the ones that still matter after everyone has signed.

Here is Lucía's page from Monday, with the basis words from *How sure are you? Say that*.

- **No deal.** The March contract stands: 90,000 euros as each of sites two and three goes live, then 7,500 a month per site from January. It puts no price on a delay, so we owe nothing (checked). It ends next March unless they renew.
- **Where I would walk.** More than 40,000 in all. Past that I can't hire a second support person, and the nights I'd be promising have nobody on them.
- **Aim.** One payment, this year, of what the two weeks and the night cost them: about 20,000 (assumed). A bill can be checked.
- **Their three.** No deal: nothing to show finance, and no protection if November slips (reasoned). Where he would walk: I don't know. Aim: a saving he can report, probably this year (assumed).
- **Who decides.** Wesley on money; on dates, Claire, who runs operations (told).
- **What I will not do.** Cut the monthly fee, which pays the people on the nights. Use the missing clause to pay nothing. Blame their security review. Promise a date without its condition. Hint at a better client.
- **The test.** In December, can we still staff the nights, and can they still do their part?

| The issue | Worth to Tolva | Worth to Veldhaven (my guess) |
|---|---|---|
| Ten percent off the monthly fee | 27,000 euros a year, every year | Nothing before January |
| One credit this year | Its amount, once | All of it, this year |
| A price on being late at sites two and three | Small, if our dates hold | Large: December, and Claire's word to her board |
| The same fee to the end of next year | Large: a client we can plan on | Some: no increase |
| Two of their engineers on the phone, go-live nights | Large: it protects the dates | Small: a few nights |

Compare the two columns, row by row. Whatever is worth more to them than it costs you is yours to offer. Whatever is worth more to you than it costs them is yours to ask for. And every guess marked *assumed* is a question for Thursday.

> **Research says:** In three experiments, negotiators who kept their mind on the price they wanted got better deals than those who kept it on their limit, and were less satisfied with them. Reminding them of that limit afterward raised their satisfaction. Our step: keep your aim in view as you talk, and check your limit before a yes and afterward; experiments on price say little about long relationships.

## What to say, and what to keep

Two pieces of advice pull against each other. *Be open*: nobody can offer you what they don't know you need. *Give nothing away*: every fact you share can be used on your price.

The first is right about needs. Wesley won't offer his engineers' phone numbers unless Lucía says that the go-live nights worry her. The second is right about limits. Once he knows she could pay 40,000, the conversation is about 40,000.

So say what you need and what matters most, because trades are made of nothing else. Give one and ask for one, and leave your ranking out of an opening offer. Keep where you would walk and what you would do with no deal. What you won't do can be said aloud, with its reason. Mention another offer only if it is real and you would take it, because they may tell you to. And lie about nothing. "I'd rather not say" is always available, and a false limit is exposed the day you move past it.

## Thursday, line by line

A video call, Rotterdam and Montevideo, with Lucía's page beside her screen.

### They open first

Wesley: "You've seen the agenda. I'd like to leave today with all three."

Lucía: "I've read it. Before I answer, what does the thirty-six rest on?"

Wesley: "Eighteen is invoices. Overtime for the planners, temporary staff. I doubled it, because nobody sends me an invoice for a site manager's weekend."

Lucía: "And the ten?"

Wesley: "Five is what the carriers billed us for waiting. The same idea."

A smaller number from her would have made thirty-six the place they both started from. She asks the second question instead, and thirty-six comes apart into two numbers. Sort first, the tool from *Thinking on your feet*, tells her what she has. The eighteen is about what is true, and invoices settle it. The doubling is about who carries a cost that nobody billed, and that needs a fair rule and a named decider.

> **Research says:** In three experiments, whichever side made the first offer did better; the advantage disappeared when the other side thought about the opener's alternatives or limit, or their own target. Pooled studies agree, less strongly for complex negotiations, and it can reverse when one side knows far more or the opener reveals priorities. We found no independent repeat of the defense, so take it as a cheap habit: before you answer their number, look at your own aim.

Should you speak first? If you know as much as they do, yes: your number, with what it rests on. If they know more, as Wesley knew what those two weeks had cost, ask first.

### They are right about something

Lucía: "Both of those happened. The review that moved the date found a fault of ours, and the night of the sixth was ours. Send me the bills, and I'll credit what they say, once, this year. That's twenty-three."

Wesley: "And the rest? Your contract has a price for everything except being late."

Lucía: "It does. We both signed it, and it's a gap. I'd like to close it before November. First, may I ask about your third line?"

She could have cleared the first two lines with the contract and lost the client next March. She concedes the facts at once and stops where the bills stop; the half that nobody invoiced is still open. And the twenty-three carries no "if", because she has already agreed to it.

### You find out what they need

Lucía: "The ten percent starts in January. Does it help you this year?"

Wesley: "No." He looked at something off the screen. "Finance gave me a savings target. It closes on 31 December, and your contract is on the list. In January they'll give me another one."

Lucía: "So what's booked before the 31st counts, and a cheaper January doesn't."

Wesley: "Not for this one. Any cut counts for something."

Lucía: "Understood. Here is mine, then. The monthly fee pays the people who answer at night, so I won't cut it. What I need most is to keep your two November dates."

One question turns *assumed* into *told*, and her read-back gets corrected, which is how she knows she has it. Only then does she give a priority of her own. Wesley has a target with a closing date. The ten percent was one way to reach it, and not a good one.

### You give ground

Lucía: "If the agreement runs to the end of next year at the same fee, I can put a price on being late. Four thousand euros for each week, up to twelve in all, when the cause is ours."

Wesley: "And when the cause is ours?"

Lucía: "The date moves by the same number of days. For that I'd need two of your warehouse-system engineers available on the go-live nights."

Wesley: "Who decides whose cause it was?"

Lucía: "Mateo, my co-founder, and Claire, in writing, within two working days. If they can't agree, we each carry half."

Attach a return to everything else you give: `If you [what I need], I can [what it costs me]. If not, [what stays as it is].` The "if" is old advice; the last slot is easy to leave out. A gift with no "if" can invite a second request, because it suggests that the first figure was loose. Before you say a number, add up its worst case. Lucía has: twenty-three and twelve make 35,000, inside the limit she hasn't mentioned.

### They push once more

Wesley: "Twenty-three isn't forty-six. Make it thirty and we're done."

Lucía: "Thirty would close it, I can see that. Twenty-three has bills behind it, and the other seven doesn't. But the weekends were real. Both sites start on a Monday. Diego and a second engineer can be on your dock from the Sunday evening, for three nights, at each."

Wesley: "On the Sunday."

Lucía: "From the Sunday."

Wesley: "That one the site managers will want in writing."

She neither splits the difference nor repeats herself; she goes back to what the doubling stood for and finds something that answers it. Tolva has already placed Diego, its engineer, in Rotterdam, so three nights cost little. To people who lost their weekends, they are worth more than seven thousand euros in somebody else's budget.

### You close

Lucía: "Let me say it back. Twenty-three off site two's invoice, against the bills. Four thousand a week for being late, up to twelve, or the date moves with you. Two of ours on the dock from each Sunday, two of yours available at night. The same fee to the end of next year. What's wrong, or missing?"

Wesley: "Missing. Who reads what your agents write? Our security people won't let me forget it."

Lucía: "Then it goes in, with a name beside it. Last question. It's the second week of December. Which of these has one of us stopped keeping?"

Wesley thought about it. "'Available.' At four in the morning those engineers are asleep, and their manager asks who pays. Give me names and hours, and I'll get it agreed."

The sheet's last line, asked aloud, found the one term that would have failed, and Wesley was the one who found it. Two names, ten at night to six, three nights, paid by Veldhaven. The agreement was signed on 2 November, once a lawyer on each side had read it.

## Five you are likely to meet

### Scope

The client adds a data export to work you priced without it. Offer the whole choice, with each side's cost: "The export is eight days. We can add it and move the date to the 20th, add it and leave out the audit screens, or plan it for the next release. Which is worth most to you?" Silence about the eight days is also an offer. You have just given them away.

### A deadline

Ask what happens on the day: "What has to be true on 1 June?" The date often stands for something smaller than the plan, such as one screen that a chief executive can show. Then trade contents for time: "The booking screen on 1 June, if the test accounts arrive by the 10th of May. The reports on the 22nd." Never sell a date your own estimate denies. You will pay for it in public.

### Price

Raise a rate in one sentence, with a date and with what doesn't change: "From 1 February my rate is 640 euros a day. Days you've already booked stay at 580." If the budget is fixed, change the quantity and leave the rate alone: "Then 5,800 a month buys nine days instead of ten." For saying the number and stopping, see *Say less*; for the buyer's objection, *The sale*.

### Salary

Write down more than pay before you talk: title, start date, hours, days at home, the date of the first review. Find the range from two sources you could name aloud. If you're asked what you earn now, the rules differ by country; where you may decline, try "I'd rather talk about what this job needs." The first practice below is the rest of the conversation.

### Equity

A number of shares tells you nothing until you know the total. Ask for facts before you discuss amounts: "How many shares exist, counting those already promised? What do I pay for mine, and when? What happens to them if I leave, or if the company is sold? Who is paid first?" If you are the one offering, answer those before they are asked, and stay inside what you know: "I can't tell you what they'll be worth. I can show you how many there are." Equity, tax and contract terms vary by country, so take the papers to a professional before you sign. Dividing a company between founders is a conversation of its own, in *Founders and the AI-era startup*.

## Practice: the salary answer

**Situation.** A small company wants you as its first data engineer. On Friday's call the founder asks, "What are you looking for?"

**First draft (what a careful engineer says).**
"I'm on fifty-two now, and my rent has just gone up, so I'd need at least fifty-six to move. I've run a pipeline alone in my last two jobs, so I think that would be reasonable?"

**What's wrong.** Two numbers, both about the speaker's life, neither about the job. "At least" gives away where the speaker would walk. The one fact the founder could repeat to whoever signs comes last, as a question.

**Better.**
"Sixty-four thousand. Two published ranges for this role run from fifty-eight to sixty-six, and you need someone who can run the pipeline alone from the first month. I've done that twice. Does it fit what you've planned?"

"No. We planned fifty-eight."

"Is fifty-eight a rule for the role, or this year's budget?"

"The budget. It resets in July."

"Then I could start at sixty, if the offer says sixty-four from July, once the morning load has finished by seven for a full quarter. If the letter can't say that, I'd need the weekend to think."

**Why it works.** The number comes first, with a basis that travels. One question finds out why fifty-eight is a limit, and the answer turns an argument about money into a trade about time. The condition is one that both of them can check in July.

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Write your figure, two facts behind it that the other side could check, and one question to ask when they say it's too much. Say all of it to a colleague who answers, "We planned less." Reply with the question before any new number. **Done means** the colleague can repeat your figure and one fact behind it, and can't say the least you would take.

## Practice: one page, one question

**With a person.** **If you do only one, do this one.** Choose something you will really negotiate this month: a date, a rate, a piece of scope, a day off. Fill in the sheet (10 minutes). Put a basis word beside every guess, choose the *assumed* line that matters most, and turn it into a question. Ask it before any number is discussed.

Watch for a reply that contains a date, a rule or a name: that is what their number is for. If another number comes back instead, ask what it has to achieve. Afterward, send them what each of you will do and by when, and ask which line would be hardest to keep. **Done means** that guess now says *told*, and they have confirmed your message in writing or corrected one line of it.

**Spar with an AI.**

```text
Play a procurement lead. I supply you with [service], and we are renegotiating [what].
Before we start, privately choose a savings target, the date it must show by,
your limit, and one thing you need more than money. Reveal them only when I ask.
Open with two demands. If I give something without asking for a return, ask for more.
Stop after eight turns. List what I gave for nothing, each number I accepted without
asking what it rested on, and the term that would be hardest to keep.
```

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## Words to use

- **To ask about a number.** "What does that figure rest on?" "When does the saving have to show?"
- **To give one.** "[Amount]. It rests on [what they can check]."
- **To trade.** "If you can [what I need], I can [what it costs me]."
- **When they ask for your limit.** "I won't start from my limit. Here is what I'm aiming for, and why."
- **To take time.** "I want to check that before I say yes. You'll have my answer by [time]."
- **To find who decides.** "If we agree today, who else could change it?"
- **To close.** "In [month], which of these will be hardest for you to keep?"
- **To leave.** "I don't see terms we'd both keep. If [one thing] changes, please call me."

### Phrases you will hear

- "Let's split the difference" (agree on the midpoint). Ask first what each end rests on.
- "Best and final" (the last offer they plan to make). Ask whether it's final on price, or on everything.
- "Take it or leave it" (no more discussion). It may be true; compare it with your no-deal line.
- "You're leaving money on the table" (you could have had more). Perhaps, and check what it would have cost them to keep.

## When it backfires

**They hold the power.** With one client, a fixed pay scale, or a boss who writes your review, the no-deal line is weak, and it's better to learn that on paper. Ask privately for things that cost them little: a review date, a shorter notice, a payment term. Use the time before the next talk to strengthen that line with a second client or a second offer.

**There is nothing to trade.** Some tenders compare one number, and some pay bands don't move. Ask once whether anything else can. If it can't, decide yes or no and say so plainly.

**The page makes you deaf.** Your guess at their three is still a guess. When Wesley said "Any cut counts for something," Lucía had to change a line. If nothing on your page moves during the talk, you may have been reading it instead of listening.

**"What does it rest on?" at every number.** Asked of the figure that matters, it is careful; asked of every figure, it sounds like an audit. Give the basis of your own number first, and the question reads as a rule for both of you.

**A first number that is meant to move.** In some rooms everyone expects a long way between the opening and the end; in others, a moved number looks like a bluff. Ask someone who has dealt with this team how far first numbers usually travel.

**It isn't yours to trade.** What the law already settles, such as wages owed, safety or discrimination, doesn't go on the issues line. Get outside help. For a deadline or a threat built to stop you thinking, go to *Pressure and how to resist it*.

**In a second language.** "If" is a small word, and your return depends on it. Say it first and slowly, put every figure and condition in the chat as you say it, and ask for five minutes whenever terms change. Nobody owes a fast answer to a written agenda.
