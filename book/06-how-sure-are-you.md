# How sure are you? Say that

"Are we fine for labels?" Mateo asked in the team chat at 16:42. "It should be fine," Diego answered at 16:44.

In June, Mateo, Tolva's co-founder and chief technology officer, read the reply in Montevideo and moved on. Diego was the engineer Tolva had placed in Rotterdam with Veldhaven, its largest client. He had a reason for his "should": that morning he had printed two hundred labels through the new interface at site one, and every one came out readable. He had not tested the printers at Veldhaven's other two distribution centers, sites two and three. He assumed they matched site one's.

They didn't. Sites two and three ran older firmware, and the label work slipped three weeks.

Diego wasn't careless, and he wasn't hiding anything. His reply held a fact he had checked and a guess he hadn't, together inside "should", and Mateo had no way to pull them apart.

## Guesses in plain sight

Behind every claim people act on is how much you know, how you know it and what you haven't checked. This book calls that the claim's size. Leave it out and the listener supplies one. You find out which size they chose when the date passes.

Say the size, and your word becomes something people can build a plan on, guesses included, because they know which parts are guesses.

## The sized claim

The sized claim has three parts.

- **The claim.** One sentence: what is true, or what will happen.
- **The basis.** How you know it, in one of four words, strongest first:
  - **Checked.** I ran it or read the record myself.
  - **Told.** A person, a document or a machine told me.
  - **Reasoned.** I worked it out from things checked or told.
  - **Assumed.** I am taking it as given.
- **The limit.** Where the claim stops, what has not been checked, and what would change it.

Here is Diego's reply at its real size:

"Site one, yes: I printed two hundred labels through the new interface this morning, and every one was readable. Sites two and three, I haven't checked. I'm assuming their printers match site one's. I can test site two on Thursday."

The firmware would still have been old. What would have changed is when Tolva found out, and whether anyone planned on a guess without knowing it was one. Every doubt that hid inside "should" is still there, now attached to two named sites and a Thursday.

This book's bet is that when any draft can sound sure, people will check with the person who can say how sure, and on what.

> **Line to keep:** Put the doubt in the claim, where people can use it.

## From hedge to limit

Naomi is a solo data consultant in Seattle. Veldhaven hired her in March to forecast when each truck would really arrive. Her forecast will feed Tolva's scheduler, which gives each truck a door and a time. By June she had six weeks of Veldhaven's gate logs to score it against. She is growing toward the calibrator: the habit of saying how sure we are, and testing a claim before the team acts on it.

### Practice: one claim at its real size

**Situation.** June. Claire, Veldhaven's vice president of operations, has asked Naomi in writing how the forecast is doing.

**First draft (what a careful consultant writes).** "Early results are encouraging overall, though they may vary somewhat between sites. It's probably too soon to draw firm conclusions, so I'd treat the current numbers as indicative for now."

**What's wrong.** Four hedges and no number. Every phrase protects the writer, and the fact Claire most needs, that site three is far behind, hides inside "may vary somewhat". Nothing here can turn out wrong, so nothing here can be planned on.

**Better.** "Over six weeks of your gate logs, the forecast was within thirty minutes for about eight arrivals in ten at sites one and two. At site three it was about five in ten. I checked each forecast against the log myself. I don't know yet why site three is worse, and none of the six weeks was a peak week, so I can't yet say how it will do in December. The first peak week's logs could move every number here."

**Why it works.** The first two sentences are the claim, the third gives its basis, and the last two are the limit. Naomi got there by asking what each hedge was unsure about. "May vary somewhat" became site three's number, and "probably too soon" became the missing peak week. "Encouraging" and "indicative" named nothing, so she cut them.

> **Research says:** Across eleven studies of advice about forecasts, advisers who said they were "not sure" were rated below confident ones. Advisers who gave a range, a probability or "more likely than" were not marked down, and given a choice, people more often picked the adviser whose advice carried its uncertainty. The advisers were strangers, so carrying this to your own colleagues is the book's reasoning: give the range, and drop the "not sure".

**Your turn (5 minutes).** Open the last status message you sent and label every claim in it with one of the four words, in the margin, for yourself. Then rewrite the claim that matters most as three sentences: the claim, its basis word, and its limit, including what would change it. Read it aloud once. **If you do only one, do this one.** **Done means** a colleague who hears it once can tell you which part you checked yourself and what would change your answer.

## What Claire had to decide

Naomi sent the rewritten report. Claire answered within the hour: "This is clear, and I still can't use it. On Tuesday I approve the go-live plan for all three sites. It has to name the sites where your forecast goes into the scheduler. Can you call me?"

Claire had asked in writing how the forecast was doing, and the report answered that. Her plan asked something else: which sites?

Naomi called and began with the sites. "Sites one and two. Site three, not yet. I'd put the forecast in the plan for the first two, and add site three when I can show you its numbers have caught up."

"Why is site three behind?"

"I don't know yet. What I do know is that it's behind in every one of the six weeks, so it isn't one bad week. I'll have a first answer by the end of the month."

"That I can put in the plan," Claire said.

A sized claim answers a question, and the question that counts is the one the listener has to decide. The listener sketch in *Who is listening* asks for that decision; if you can't name it, ask before you size anything. Then lead with the claim that answers the decision, with its limit in the same breath, and let the basis follow as far as the listener wants.

**Spar with an AI.** Rehearse asking for the decision first.

```text
Play an operations director who must make one decision with an estimate I am about to give you. Keep the decision to yourself unless I ask what it is. If I give the estimate before I ask, tell me once what you still can't decide. Then ask what my answer rests on and what would change it. At the end, tell me which sentence you would plan on, which you would check first, and which words sounded unsure and gave no reason.
```

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## Sound sure, or say how sure?

Sound confident, says one piece of advice. A listener who must act needs something to act on, and a speaker who wraps every sentence in "maybe" hands the listener the work of guessing. Its best case is a room that has to decide today, where a speaker who won't commit leaves everyone stuck.

Admit what you don't know, says the other. Nobody is right every time, and when the miss comes, the person who claimed too much pays more for it. In two experiments with mock witnesses, a mistake cost a witness more credibility when that witness had been confident about the very point they got wrong. Its best case is a forecast scored against the record every week, as Naomi's is, where every miss is seen.

The sized claim takes the true half of each. Be firm in your voice and exact in your claim. "About five in ten" can be said as steadily as "yes", and it should be. Give a number only when you have something under it.

## Dates, numbers and "I don't know"

Dates and numbers are claims too. For a date:

`[date], if [the one thing it depends on]. If not, [the other date]. I will know by [when].`

In June, Lucía, Tolva's co-founder and chief executive, filled it in for Veldhaven's chief executive: "Site one goes live on 14 September, if your security review passes in one round. If it needs a second round, the 28th. I'll know by 20 August."

Before you give a date for your own work, find the last job like it and how long it took. That is your basis, reasoned from something that happened.

> **Research says:** In a series of studies, people predicting when they would finish their own tasks were too optimistic, planning forward and setting aside how similar jobs had gone. Asked to connect the new task to those past jobs, they lost the bias, while people forecasting someone else's finish erred the other way. In these studies that link helped; they don't show every job runs late.

For a number, give a range and say what moves it. Once the old firmware was found, Diego's next message to Mateo had both: "Two to four weeks to fix. Four if Veldhaven can't update the printers at both sites in the same week."

"I don't know" is a limit said first. Follow it with what you do know and when you will know more.

## When the claim comes to you

Claims reach you every day without their size, from colleagues and from machines. Use the second-question test from *Start here*, all five, in order: *What is the point? What does it rest on? Where does it stop? What do you want from me? Who answers for it?* The first one without an answer is the next thing to find out.

Run it on Diego's reply. The point is clear: the labels are fine. What does it rest on? No answer, and that was Mateo's next thing to find out. One line would have found it: "Is that all three sites, or site one?"

Now run it on a machine. The week after the call, Naomi asked an assistant, one her contract with Veldhaven allows, to draft her weekly note from her results table. The draft was fluent, and its arithmetic was right: "Across Veldhaven's sites, about seven arrivals in ten land within thirty minutes of the forecast." The point: the forecast mostly works. What it rests on: her own table. Where it stops, and what would show it wrong: the draft doesn't say, so that is where she looked. Seven in ten was the average of all three sites, and site three's five in ten had disappeared inside it. The claim was the wrong size.

Whatever a machine tells you carries the basis word *told*, however fluent it sounds, until you check it. The fifth question has a standing answer: if nobody else answers for a claim, you do, the moment you pass it on. Naomi sent the note with three sites in it.

### Practice: test a claim that reached you

**Your turn (3 minutes).** Take the last summary an assistant drafted for you, or the last status claim a colleague sent. Put it through the second-question test, in order, and stop at the first question it can't answer. **Done means** you have written down the one thing you will check before you forward it.

## Words to use

- **The basis.** "I checked this myself." "I was told this by [who or what], and I haven't checked it." "I worked it out from [what]." "I'm assuming [what]. Nobody has checked it."
- **The limit.** "This covers [what]. It doesn't cover [what] yet." "What would change this is [one thing]."
- **A number.** "Between [low] and [high]. It's [high] if [one thing] happens."
- **"I don't know."** "I don't know yet. What I do know is [what]. I'll know by [when]."
- **When they need one answer.** "If you need one date for your plan, use [the later one]. I'll tell you by [when] if [the earlier one] holds."
- **A claim that reaches you.** "What is that based on?" "Is that checked, or were you told?" "Is that all three sites, or one?" "Who signs off on this before it goes out?"

### Phrases you will hear

- "Back of the envelope": a quick, rough calculation.
- "Off the top of my head": from memory, without checking.
- "Don't quote me on that": I'm not sure, so don't repeat it as fact.

## When it backfires

**On every sentence.** The sized claim is for claims someone will act on. Put a basis word on every line of a status message and the one assumption that matters gets lost among twenty that don't. Size what carries a decision, and say the rest plainly.

**Upward.** A senior person who asked for one date may hear your condition as an excuse. Give the date and its condition in one breath; if they still want one date, give the later one and say when you'll know about the earlier. Don't drop the limit to sound surer. Repeat the condition in writing afterward, so that if the date moves, the record shows what it depended on.

**Against a sure voice.** A sized claim can lose, at first, to someone who simply sounds certain. Researchers tested what happens when that person is shown wrong. A confident manner still helped, unless it was tied to a claim someone could check. Confidence stated in words, then shown wrong, was judged worse than caution.

**Culture and rank.** In some teams, "I haven't checked" said to a senior person sounds like skipping the work; in others, leaving it out sounds like hiding something. Ask how this team wants to hear a limit before the meeting where it matters.

**In a second language.** The four basis words are plain, and may carry your meaning more reliably than *should*, *might* and *could*, whose strength can shift from one speaker to the next. "I checked" is hard to misread.
