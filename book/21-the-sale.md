# The sale

Hugo: "Thirty-four minutes. Gate to door, between five and seven, over three weeks. After seven it's nine."

Lucía: "Then five to seven is where we start. The scheduler re-plans the doors every time a truck checks in."

Hugo: "Can it show the temperature in the trailer?"

Lucía: "Yes. We can add that."

Hugo: "Does it know where each truck is standing in the yard?"

Lucía: "It can. We'd build you a map."

Hugo: "Texts to the drivers?"

Lucía: "Yes, those too."

Hugo: "Good. Send me a proposal."

The video call, on a Tuesday late in August, stayed friendly to the end. Lucía is co-founder of Tolva, which makes dock-scheduling software in Montevideo. Hugo runs operations at Sarnik, a distributor of chilled and frozen food near Lyon. In July he had told her that Sarnik didn't know how long a truck waited for a door. He had gone and counted, and she had answered his count with three yeses.

That evening an assistant turned her notes into eleven pages: a temperature module, a yard map, a messaging service, four warehouses, 8,400 euros a month for each. Hugo replied the next morning. "I asked if it could. I did not ask for it. Those questions were my engineers' list. What does five to seven cost?"

## Questions, heard as orders

Hugo was right: the proposal priced features he didn't need. Each of his questions had a short, true answer ("It could. We haven't built it."). Lucía answered three questions with three promises, because a yes kept the call pleasant. The questions that would have set a price went unasked: what he was deciding, who would sign, and what thirty-four minutes cost them.

Selling well is mostly finding out what is true and writing it down so someone else can check it.

A sale is a decision another person has to make and then explain. The second question may come from someone you never meet, and your buyer will have to answer it for you. So fill in their side of the decision with them, and say it back before you price it. Write a proposal whose every line they can check, say the price as something you have decided, and sort the objection before you answer it.

> **Line to keep:** Sell a decision they can explain without you.

## The call that should have come first

On Thursday Lucía phoned. "You're right. I priced your engineers' list. May I ask three things before I write another line?"

Her questions were the listener sketch from *Who is listening*, filled in aloud: what Hugo knew, had to decide and stood to lose. Asking before offering is among the oldest advice in selling. The sketch tells you what to ask, and when you have asked enough.

| The sketch | Lucía asked | Hugo said |
|---|---|---|
| **Know** | "What have you tried for five to seven?" | "Earlier slots. Carriers ignore them. Now I'm getting another quote." |
| **Decide** | "What do you decide, by when, and who signs?" | "Next year's budget, in October. I recommend. Our finance director signs." |
| **Lose** | "When you show her the number, what will she ask first?" | "What she asks me now. Why we pay carriers to wait." |

The third question never mentions losing. It asks what happens to your answer next, and the loss comes back in the reply: every month, Hugo explains a cost he can't yet bring down.

Look at her second question. Hugo could stop this purchase alone; he could not start it alone. Whoever signs may care about something your contact has never had to count, so ask to meet them before you price anything. Lucía asked: "Could I have ten minutes with her first?"

> **Research says:** In three experimental and field studies, people underestimated by as much as half how likely others were to agree to a direct request for help. A separate lab later found the same kind of underestimate. The requests were for help, so asking for the introduction, in person where you can, is our step; they say nothing about the order.

The finance director gave them ten minutes on Monday. Hugo had counted minutes; she had been paying for them. At the warehouse he had timed, carriers had billed 4,100 euros for waiting in July.

Then Lucía used the read-back: what she understood, what she would do, what she was unsure of. "At your busiest warehouse, trucks wait thirty-four minutes from five to seven, and you're billed for it. I'll write one page about that and leave everything else out. I'm not sure whether you'd start with one warehouse or four."

"One," said Hugo. "And it's not the busiest. It's the one with the fewest doors."

Tolva's price depends on doors and trucks. One wrong word would have put a wrong number on the page.

## A page they can check

Every claim in a proposal is a sized claim, from *How sure are you? Say that*: the claim, its basis, its limit. On a call you can add the limit when someone asks. On paper nobody can ask, so print it.

### Practice: the proposal

**Situation.** On the last day of August Lucía writes again, for Hugo and for the finance director who signs.

**First draft (what went out on Tuesday, shortened).**
"We are pleased to propose a complete dock-management platform for Sarnik's four warehouses, combining real-time scheduling, trailer-temperature monitoring, a live yard map and driver messaging. Clients report significant reductions in waiting time. The investment is 8,400 euros per warehouse per month, all modules included. We look forward to a long partnership."

**What's wrong.** It answers questions asked in passing and skips the one he came with. "Significant" has no count behind it. Nothing says what Tolva hasn't shown, and the finance director can't tell which part of 8,400 euros would shorten any wait.

**Better.**
"To decide in October: should next year's budget include our scheduler at the one warehouse you timed?

"The problem, as you corrected it: between five and seven, a truck there waits thirty-four minutes for a door (Hugo's count, three weeks). In July, carriers billed 4,100 euros for waiting (your invoices).

"What we have shown: at one client's dock, the average wait fell from about fifty minutes to about thirty, over six weeks, by their own gate log. What we haven't shown: a whole warehouse, cold or not. Our first is due to start in late September, if a security review passes.

"The price: 5,000 euros a month for that warehouse. July's waiting charges wouldn't cover it, even if every one disappeared. Whether the rest is worth it (the cooling units running in your yard, the calls before seven) is yours to judge.

"Left out, because you didn't ask for them: trailer temperature, the yard map, driver texts.

"To check us: send your three weeks, and we'll replay them truck by truck before you sign anything. Questions about any line come to me. Lucía"

**Why it works.** It opens on their decision and uses their numbers, each with its source. It prints the limit and does the subtraction the finance director was going to do anyway. Either of them can repeat the case from this page.

**Your turn (12 minutes).** Take a proposal, a quote or a budget request you've sent. Rewrite it on one page: the decision, the signer, the problem in their numbers, what you've shown and haven't, the price, what you left out, one check they can run. **Done means** a colleague who reads it once can tell you the price, what it buys and the one thing you haven't shown.

## Say the price

Two pieces of advice about saying a price each have a good case. Soften it: a number can threaten someone's budget and their standing, and a person who feels pushed may stop weighing it. Project it: say it flat, because "only" and "roughly" announce that it can move.

Use both, on different things. Soften what the price costs the person, and leave the content alone. Tell them before their boss hears it, give them the page that explains it, and let them say no without embarrassment. Then say the number at full strength. The same goes for a limit, and for any point the room keeps passing over.

Lucía said it on the Monday call: "It's five thousand euros a month, for that warehouse." She stopped there, as *Say less* teaches. She had a lower number in her head and left it there. If four thousand was possible, Hugo would have been right to ask what the other thousand had been for. A price should move when what it covers moves: a shorter term, a narrower scope, less support.

## The objection, and a no

On 10 September Hugo called. "Your page is clear. The other company quotes two and a half thousand. And my engineers say they can build it themselves in six weeks, with a coding assistant. So tell me your number again."

Treat an objection as a disagreement and sort first: say to yourself which kind it is, and you know what would settle it.

| You hear | It may be about | What could settle it |
|---|---|---|
| "You're twice their price." | What is true: is it the same job? | One test, agreed first, run on both |
| "My team could build it." | What to do | The goal: what must still work in two years |
| "There's no budget." | Who gets something limited | A fair rule, and who decides |

Lucía took the price first. "Five thousand euros a month, for that warehouse. I haven't seen theirs run, so I can't tell you it's worse. Give us both the same three weeks and count the trucks that would still have waited past thirty minutes. If theirs does as well, buy theirs."

Hugo wrote that down. "And the build?"

"They can build a first version. Ask them who answers for it at five in the morning, in its second winter."

"That I will ask." He read something on his screen. "Five thousand is clear. And it's no. One dock, six weeks: your own page says it. I can't put that in front of her."

Everything in this chapter was done well, and it failed. The price was clean, the objections were sorted, and the reason he gave was the limit she had printed. A rounder claim might have got past him that afternoon. It would have left Hugo to explain the first bad night to the person who signed.

Lucía didn't cut the price or argue with her own sentence. She asked, "What would you need to see?"

"A warehouse running on it, nights included. My budget closes in October."

"Then I'll call you when I have one, and I'll tell you how the nights went. The price will be the same."

"Call me, then."

### Practice: three lines before the price

**With a person.** **If you do only one, do this one.** Before you next send a price (a quote, a day rate, a request for budget), send the person three lines. First, the problem in their words, with their number. Second, what they decide, by when, and who signs. Third, the one thing you're unsure of.

End with "What did I get wrong?" and hold the price back for their answer. Watch for the word they change: it may be the one you were about to price. **Done means** their reply corrects or confirms each line and names whoever signs.

**Spar with an AI.** Paste this in, and expect a no.

```text
Play the head of operations at a company that may buy from me.
Another vendor quoted half my price, and your engineers want to build it.
Ask what I sell and what it costs. Raise both objections, one at a time.
Interrupt me once. Whatever I answer, end with a no and one honest reason.
Let me reply to the no. Then stop playing and tell me: the price you heard,
any point where I lowered it or apologized for it, and whether I found out
what would change your answer.
```

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## Words to use

- **When they ask "Can it...?"** "It could. We haven't built it. What would you use it for?"
- **To find whoever signs.** "Who else will read this?" "What will they ask first?"
- **The price beside their cost.** "It's [amount] a month. [What they counted] alone doesn't cover it. The rest is yours to judge."
- **A lower quote.** "I haven't seen theirs run. What test would you trust for both of us?"
- **To hold the price.** "I can change what's included. For what's on this page, the price is the same."
- **A no.** "That's clear, thank you. What would you need to see?"
- **To leave.** "Then I won't follow up before [the thing they need]. You'll hear from me when it happens."

### Phrases you will hear

"What's the ballpark?" (roughly how much?) "Can you sharpen your pencil?" (can you charge less? Ask what they would take out.) "That's above my pay grade." (someone else decides. Ask who.)

## When it backfires

**When they only want a number.** Someone who has asked your price twice and received questions has a reason to leave. Give the number, or the range and the one fact it depends on, and then ask.

**When they hold the power.** A large buyer's process may forbid calls or ask for a figure in a box. Send your questions by the route it names, and put your limit in the one free-text field.

**When "who signs?" embarrasses.** In front of a team, or where rank is sensitive, it can sound like "so you don't decide". Ask in private, and ask who else will read the page.

**When you need this sale to pay salaries.** Holding a price is harder with nothing behind it. Decide your lowest number, and what comes out at that number, before the call (*Negotiation* has the preparation).

**When the count says they don't need you.** Say so and stop. You will have lost an order and kept someone who believes your next number.

**In a second language.** Type the price in the chat as you say it. If an objection arrives as an idiom, ask for its figure: "What number did they give you?"
