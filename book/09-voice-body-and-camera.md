# Voice, body and camera

Diego has just asked for the paper plan when Claire's face changes. Her eyebrows pull together and her mouth goes flat. Her eyes leave the camera, move to something on her left and stay there.

It is June. Diego works for Tolva, a software company in Montevideo. Tolva has placed him in Rotterdam with its largest client, the food wholesaler Veldhaven. Tolva's scheduler, which sends each arriving truck to a door, is due to go live at Veldhaven's site one on 14 September. Claire, who runs operations at all three of Veldhaven's distribution centers, has joined the call from site three, the cold store. On his laptop her face fills a window smaller than his hand. He can't see her hands, or whatever her eyes have gone to.

His request is small, and it costs her something. For the first two weeks after go-live, he wants her planners to keep printing the old paper plan every evening. If the scheduler stops in the night, the dock supervisors will still know where each truck goes.

He reads her face in under a second: no.

So he argues with it. "I know it's extra work for your planners," he says, leaning toward the lens. "But the first nights are when a new system is most likely to stop. If it stops at three in the morning, your supervisors need a plan in their hands, not a phone number in Montevideo." His voice has climbed and quickened. Between sentences his eyes drop to his notes.

"Diego." Claire is looking at the camera again. "Sorry. That was an alarm from one of the cold rooms here. Someone is going to check it." She pauses. "I haven't said no. Two weeks is fine. One of the planners is on leave the week of the 14th, so put it in writing for the site manager, with the day it stops."

He has spent his best argument on an objection nobody made. He has also told her, without meaning to, that he expected a refusal.

## The no that nobody said

Everything Diego saw was there. Claire's eyebrows did come down, and her eyes did leave the camera. The no was his. He took one change in one face, turned it into her answer and argued with the answer. In a window that size, a frown at an alarm and a frown at a request look the same.

A change in someone's face tells you that something happened on their side, and very little about what. Let it tell you which sentence to ask about, then ask in words, about the work. Your own face, voice and camera get read the same way, in a room or on a call. Most of what they send is yours to set, and the rest you can put words beside. Get this right and you stop arguing with faces.

Two tools from Part I do the reading. The observable version, from *The exact word*, asks what a recording would show: eyebrows down, eyes to the left for a few seconds, no words. Could two people who disagree about what the frown meant agree that this happened? Easily. Could they agree that Claire said no? They couldn't, because she hadn't.

The sized claim, from *How sure are you? Say that*, asks what a reading rests on. A face reaches you the way a message does, as something told. The frown told Diego that something was going on at Claire's end. What it was, the frown couldn't say, so Diego supplied it himself, and his basis word was *assumed*. The limit of any reading is everything else a frown can mean. Here it was an alarm on another screen. It might also be a picture running a moment behind the sound, arithmetic, glare from a window or a long sentence in a second language. Several signals that agree give you more to reason from, and still only a reading.

Then trade the reading for the one source that has the answer: her words. Her answer is still *told*, but by the only person who knows. Finish what you planned to say, at the size you planned it, and stop talking. Ask one question about what you just asked for, rather than about their face: "Does two weeks cause a problem for your planners?" She can answer that in a sentence. "You look concerned" asks a client's vice president to explain her face, and whatever is behind it, to a supplier.

> **Line to keep:** Only words can tell you what a face meant.

## The other side of the screen

When Claire looked back from her other screen, Diego was leaning into his camera, talking faster about trouble at three in the morning, his eyes dropping away from hers. She read him the way he had read her. That week she asked Lucía, Tolva's co-founder and chief executive, whether Diego expected the scheduler to fail in its first nights. Lucía passed the question on, and Diego wrote to Claire: "On the call I argued as if you had said no, and you hadn't. Nothing in our tests says the scheduler will stop. I want the paper plan so that if it does, nobody on the dock has to guess." Claire replied in one line, copied to site one's manager: "Agreed. Send her the plan with the day it stops."

However your words were prepared, the face and voice that carry them arrive live. None of this means your face matters more than your words.

> **Myth check:** The claim that 93 percent of a message lies outside the words is usually traced to two 1967 studies in which people judged the attitude behind one spoken word. Tone outweighed the word, a negative tone turned a positive word negative, and a face counted about one and a half times as much as a voice of matched strength. Nobody explained a plan or argued a case, so this book's advice is to let words carry the content, and keep your tone from contradicting them.

Each of your signals can look like something you didn't mean:

- **Where you look.** In a room, people can see what you are looking at; on a call, they see only that you have looked away. On a laptop the lens sits above the screen, so watching their faces makes you look slightly down, and looking into the lens means you can't see them. Neither is wrong. One way to choose: the lens for the sentence you most need them to take in, their faces while they answer.
- **In a room.** Sit or stand where the person who decides can see your face without turning when you reach the ask. Turning toward whoever is speaking can look like listening. A laptop open while they talk can look like email: close it, or say what it's for.
- **Your face while you read or work something out.** It can look like Claire's frown, aimed at whoever is speaking. You don't need to change it. When it matters, put words beside it. Claire did, but only after Diego had argued.
- **Your voice at the ask.** Diego's climbed and sped up the moment he thought he had to defend his request. Keep the ask, and whatever follows it, at the pace of the sentence before it. A sentence that rises at the end can sound like a question to some listeners. If yours does and you mean a request, say so in words: "That's what I'm asking for."
- **When you're muted.** Your face is all the speaker has. A nod, or unmuting to say "Go on," tells them you are still with them.

> **From practice:** Before a call that matters, drag the window of the person who decides to the top of your screen, as close to the camera as it will go. Looking at them and looking into the lens become almost the same thing.

## Practice: hold the claim when the face changes

**Situation.** A video call. You lead the move of a client's order history to a new system, and their finance director has asked when it will be finished. At the words "second weekend", her eyebrows come down and she looks off to one side.

**First draft (once the face changes).**
"It needs a second weekend. Well, possibly. It depends a bit on the invoices, and we could maybe run them on a weeknight instead, so it might not come to that."

**What's wrong.** Nothing about the move changed between the first sentence and the second. Her face did. The claim shrank to fit a guess about a frown: "possibly", "maybe" and a weeknight the engineer doesn't believe in. If she was only checking her calendar, she now has a weaker answer and a promise nobody can keep.

**Better.**
"It needs a second weekend. The first one moved every order. The invoices are the rest, and they can't move while your month-end close is running. They're smaller than the orders, so one weekend after the close should finish them. Before I go on: does that weekend cause a problem on your side?"

**Why it works.** The claim keeps the size the facts give it, with its reason attached. The question is about the work, so whatever the frown meant can arrive in words, whether it was her calendar or the cost. She never has to explain her face.

**Your turn (5 minutes, with a colleague).** Take something you will ask for this week, such as more time, a budget or a decision. Say it to a colleague, on a call or across a desk. Ask them to change their face once, at a moment you don't know in advance: for example, a frown or a glance at a phone. When it happens, finish what you planned to say, stop, and ask one question about the work. **Done means** your colleague can say your claim was the same size after their face changed as before, and could answer your question in one sentence.

**If you do only one, do this one.**

**Written form.** Write the answer. Under the sentence that asks for something, write what you would add if the listener frowned there. If it changes the size of the claim, cross it out and write a question about the work.

**Mistakes and fixes.**

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Arguing with a face | Stop and ask about the work |
| A claim shrunk to fit a frown | Keep its size and its reason |
| "You look concerned" | A question about what you asked for |
| A nod written down as a yes | Get the yes in words |

## Practice: the frame at your ask

**Your turn (5 minutes).** Sit or stand where you usually join calls or present. Record thirty seconds of a request you will make this week, with its reason. Play it to the moment you make the request and pause there. In one line, write what a stranger would read in that frame: eyes that left the screen while you thought, a frown as you asked. Then play the request itself once more and listen for a single thing: did your voice climb or speed up at the ask, compared with the sentence before it? Change one thing and record once more. **Done means** someone shown both takes can point to what changed.

**Without recording.** On your next call, look at your own window once while someone else is talking, and write down what a stranger would read in it.

*Safe practice: record only yourself, or others only with their consent and within your employer's rules.*

## Words to use

- **When a face changes at your ask.** "Before I go on: does [the ask] cause a problem on your side?" "What would you need to know before you decide?"
- **When your own face or eyes could be misread.** "I'm looking at your message on my other screen." "I'm writing that down." "Sorry, a message just came in. Please go on."
- **When you can't see faces.** "I can't see anyone, so I'll ask each of you: [name], does [date] work?" "Your picture froze just then. Does [the ask] work for you?"
- **To turn a nod into a decision.** "Can I write that down as a yes to [the ask]?"

### Phrases you will hear

- "That raised a few eyebrows": it surprised people, or made them doubt it.
- "I can't read her": I can't tell what she thinks.
- "She's putting on a brave face": she looks calm, but may not feel calm.
- "He gave me a look": his face showed he didn't like something, without a word.

## When it backfires

**When the frown is about you.** Sometimes it follows a remark that could hurt, such as a joke about their old system. Then a question about the work sounds as if you didn't notice. Repair the remark first: "That came out wrong. What I meant was [what you meant]." If it wasn't about you, the repair cost one sentence.

**Overuse.** A question after every flicker can sound unsure, and it breaks into their thinking. Keep it for a face that changes at a sentence that asks something of them.

**Who holds the power.** A client or a boss may move on before you can ask, so ask afterward, in a message about the work. Expect your own face to be read closely too, and put words beside it early: "I'm working out the order of the steps." With a peer you know well, naming what you saw can be quicker: "You frowned when I said Thursday. Is that a problem?" When the power is yours, your frown can shrink someone's estimate the way the practice's first draft shrank. Say what it means.

**Culture and habit.** How much eye contact or nodding feels polite differs between people and teams, and nobody's habit is the correct one. In some teams a nod means "I'm following", not "I agree". Get the decision in words before you write it down.

**Cameras off.** A dark window says nothing about attention. The connection may be weak, or the room shared. Ask for cameras only when faces would change what happens, and say why. Otherwise, ask each person by name: silence from a dark window is easy to write down as a yes.

**Looking away.** Some people think better without a face in front of them, or find holding someone's gaze tiring. Don't read honesty into where anyone's eyes go.

**In a second language.** Following a long sentence in English can look like disagreeing, on their face and on yours. Expect some frowns to mean "working", and when the frown is yours, say what it means: "I understand so far. I'm just checking the numbers."
