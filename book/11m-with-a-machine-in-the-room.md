# With a machine in the room

*On a call: put your own lines first*

The script was a hundred and forty words long, and Diego had written none of them.

Diego is the engineer Tolva placed with Veldhaven, its largest client. Late in June he needed one thing from Veldhaven's systems team: the label printers at sites two and three updated in the same week.

Earlier that month he had taken a frown from Claire, who runs operations at Veldhaven, for a no. So he asked Tolva's approved assistant how to ask politely. Its draft thanked, apologized, told the labels' story in order and ended hoping the team "could potentially consider" both sites, "ideally within the same week". Read aloud, one sentence took two breaths, and the fact that mattered came last, as a wish.

He kept the thanks and wrote his own three lines on a card under the camera. On the video call he told the systems lead: "Thanks for making time. I need the printer updates at sites two and three in the same week. Then our label work fits the three weeks we announced. A week apart, it takes four. Can you do that?" He put the weight on "same" and stopped.

She looked away. He didn't guess why. He waited.

"Why does the week matter? It's half a day per site."

The script had no answer to that. Diego did. "We test our labels again on the new firmware. Same week, one round of tests for both sites. A week apart, two rounds, one after the other."

"Then both in the same week, before mid-July. You'll have the day by Friday."

## From the page to the call

An assistant can prepare what you will say. Everything this part trained comes after: saying less, being easy to hear, your face and camera, staying steady, and the next question.

Give it the jobs that end before you speak. Ask for speech; unless you do, assume it writes for a reader. Speech means short sentences, numbers as you will say them and your ask last. Put your own lines where your eyes will be, and keep the script off the screen. Then let it play your listener: one interruption, then the two hardest questions it can find. The question you get may be a third, and you will answer it from what you know of your own work.

> **Line to keep:** There is no script for the second answer.

## Practice: a draft for the ear

**If you do only one, do this one.**

**Situation.** Tomorrow, on a video call, you tell a prospective customer when their pilot can start.

**First draft (what an assistant writes).** "Following our kickoff on 3 June, we resolved two formatting issues in your sample data. Based on current capacity, we anticipate commencing the pilot as early as 15/07, contingent upon receipt of the signed data agreement by 01/07."

**What's wrong.** It reads well and is hard to say: history first, a date in figures, and the customer's one task hidden in "receipt of", never asked for.

**Better.** "The earliest we can start your pilot is the fifteenth of July. That date needs your signed data agreement by the first of July. Can your team sign it by then?"

**Why it works.** The date comes first, in words; the condition and the ask get a breath each.

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Get an assistant's draft for your next call that needs a decision. Read it aloud, cut every word you wouldn't say in conversation, and say your version from three lines on a card. Then have the assistant interrupt you once and ask the question your listener would ask next, and answer it. **Done means** a colleague who hears your version once can repeat your number and your ask, and you answer the question without notes.

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## When it backfires

**When the words aren't yours to change.** Read an approved incident statement as written, and say so. Answer questions in your own words, or say who will.

**When you rehearse every call.** You may answer the question you practiced instead of the one asked, so sort it first, as in *Thinking on your feet*.

**In a second language.** Keep the assistant's sentence if you can say it whole; swap any word you trip on for an everyday one.

## Record again

Record sixty seconds on what you are working on and why it matters, with only your point sentence in view. Slow your first sentence and stop after your ask. Score it:

1. **Sentences before the point.** Count the sentences before the first one that says why your work matters, or write "never".
2. **Hedges with no basis.** Each *probably*, *I think*, *should* or *hopefully* with no reason after it.
3. **One example.** One case that shows why it matters; background doesn't count. Yes or no.
4. **The ending.** An ask or a next step: yes or no.
5. **Said back.** Before anyone hears it, write your point in one sentence. A colleague or a friend hears it once and writes theirs, or an assistant given the transcript stands in. Theirs should name your point and your ask.

The filler count is optional. Pauses, restarts and searching for a word never count; if you stutter, work in a second language or pause to think, skip it. The written form: three minutes without stopping. Put it beside your earlier takes.

*Safe practice: record only yourself, or others only with their consent and within your employer's rules.*
