# Across borders

Five people were discussing Diego's request, and the only word he understood was his own name.

It was the second Tuesday in September. In twenty days the dock scheduler made by Tolva, a company in Montevideo, would go live at the first of Veldhaven's warehouses. Diego, the engineer Tolva had placed with Veldhaven in Rotterdam, needed one favor from its systems team. Every Sunday night the test copy of their warehouse system wiped itself clean. He had spent two days loading a week of real arrivals into it, ahead of a full-day trial on Friday the 18th.

"Can you skip this Sunday's reset?" he asked.

The team's lead looked at the engineer beside her. He said something short, someone answered, and for a minute and a half the room spoke Dutch. After six months in the city, Diego had enough Dutch to buy lunch. Somebody laughed.

The lead turned back to him. "We'll see what we can do."

"Thank you," Diego said. That afternoon he wrote to Mateo, Tolva's co-founder and chief technology officer: "No reset this Sunday."

On Monday morning the test copy was empty. Loading the week again would take until Wednesday. Diego typed a second message to Mateo, "They say yes here and then do nothing," and opened his notes to quote the yes.

There wasn't one.

## A no in other words

He walked to the lead's desk with her sentence instead of his conclusion. "On Tuesday you said, 'We'll see what we can do.' What should I have heard?"

"That you shouldn't plan on it," she said. "If I can, I give you a day. Finance tests on the same copy. I asked them on Wednesday and they said no. I thought you knew."

"And the laugh?"

"Last time we skipped it, the disk was full by Wednesday and nobody could test. We were arguing about whether that would still happen. It goes faster in Dutch."

Then she had a question for him. On Thursday she had sent Tolva her changes to site one's messages, and nobody had answered. "Four days. What's wrong with them?"

Nothing was wrong with them. At Tolva, when nobody answers a list like that, it means go ahead. She had been waiting for a yes that Tolva thought it had given.

Diego says no with the word "no," so that was the word he had listened for. The lead says yes with a day, and Tolva's silence had no day in it. Each side had been clear in its own way.

> **Research says:** In one laboratory's study, first-language speakers of Mandarin Chinese overrated how well Americans with English as their first language understood their Chinese, even when told the listeners knew none. The listeners, in turn, overrated how well they had grasped what the speakers meant. One pair of languages in one laboratory can't measure your meetings, but it is a reason to check on both sides.

The sentence that cost him two days needed no translation. It was already in English, six common words. A tool could have translated every word of the Dutch and still not told him what the lead had promised.

When you work across a border, find out how *this* team says yes, says no, disagrees and goes quiet. Watch what their words turn into, ask about one sentence, and agree in writing what you will both mean. You can start this week, and soon a yes from five time zones away is a yes you can plan on.

> **Line to keep:** Their yes is your guess unless you know how they say no.

## What differs, and what to ask

Two good teams can differ on every row.

| What differs | Watch | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| How a no sounds | What "we'll see" turned into last time | "When I ask for something you can't do, how will you tell me?" |
| Who may disagree with whom | Who speaks after the senior person has spoken | "If I think a number is wrong, should I say so here, or to you first?" |
| What silence means | The last question nobody answered: what happened next? | "If I hear nothing by Thursday, what should I assume?" |
| What interrupting means | Whether the person cut off carries on or stops for good | "Should I cut in, or write it in the chat?" |
| What goes in writing | Which decisions come back as a message, and who sends it | "Do you want this in writing before we talk, or after?" |

Don't fill in the answers from a guidebook, or from a chart that scores countries. The engineer beside the lead grew up in the same city as she did. In that minute and a half he had said a flat no to Diego's request, twice. Their country can't tell you which of the two will answer you.

"They say yes here and then do nothing" is a label with a whole company inside it. Use the observable version from *The exact word*: what would a recording show? One sentence on a Tuesday, and a reset on a Sunday. Diego had an older moment, too. In June the same lead had agreed to update two sites' label printers, and she had ended with "You'll have the day by Friday." Her yes had come with a day. He had had half the answer since June.

Two moments are enough to ask with. Quote the words and ask what you should have heard. Keep the question on the work: "What is it like in your culture?" asks one person to speak for millions.

If you work for the client from another country, offshore or nearshore, you won't overhear how Tuesday's sentence turned out. Ask the first row's question in your first week, before an answer matters.

### Practice: one sentence you misheard

**With a person.** **If you do only one, do this one.** Choose one answer from a client or another team that turned out to mean something else. Write down their words and what happened next, with no adjective and no country. This week, ask one person from that team, alone: "When you said [their words], what should I have heard?" Then offer one from your side: "When we [say or don't say something], it means [what]. Is that how you read it?" Write one line that you will both follow, and send it.

Watch the reply. If they explain the moment, the question was safe to answer. "It's fine" may mean the place or the person was wrong, so try in writing, or ask someone with less to lose. **Done means** they have confirmed or corrected your line in writing, and a person who joined this morning could tell whether it was kept.

## Half a page, from both sides

On Wednesday both teams gave Diego half an hour on a call. The lead wrote her team's habits, Mateo wrote Tolva's, and they agreed this:

- A yes has a name and a day. "We'll see" means not yet, so don't plan on it.
- No reply is not a yes, from either side. After one working day, ask again; after two, phone.
- Either team may use its own language when that's faster. Say so first. Whoever switched gives the result in English, and the other side reads it back.
- Anyone may cut in to correct a number.
- Same-day questions come between two and five in Rotterdam, nine and noon in Montevideo. Anything sent outside those hours waits for the next ones. If it can't wait, phone.
- Any line that fails twice gets rewritten.

Many software teams keep a working agreement; this is one with two companies on it. It binds both sides: Tolva had to give up its silent yes, and its engineers now announce it when they switch to Spanish. And anyone could see whether a line was kept, so nobody has to argue about what "responsive" means.

Before anyone left, each side gave the other's lines a read-back. Mateo went first: "So 'we'll see' is a no." "It's a not yet," said the lead. "When it turns into a no, I'll tell you. Last week I didn't." Without that correction, Tolva would have dropped requests she was still working on.

A question Mateo sends at five in the afternoon reaches Rotterdam at ten at night, and without a rule, somebody has to wonder whether it's urgent. Put both clocks on every time you write. And watch the gap itself: late in October, Rotterdam's clocks change and Montevideo's don't, so five hours becomes four.

By the end of the month Mateo was asking Diego what a sentence from Rotterdam meant, and the lead was asking him about sentences from Montevideo. He gave both of them what the other side had told him, and when. That is the habit of the translator, the thinker type he is growing toward: carrying meaning across a boundary intact.

## Straight, or their way?

You will hear two pieces of advice. One is to be direct everywhere, because careful wrapping gets lost in a second language. It has a strong case: when a truck is heading for the wrong door, "Door seven is closed" has to come at once, from whoever sees it. The other is to follow the local way, and raise things through the people the hierarchy expects. Diego's Tuesday is the case for that one.

Try the listener sketch from *Who is listening* on the lead. She knew that finance shared the copy. She had to decide in front of her own engineers, with Diego waiting. And she stood to lose either way. A yes was a promise finance could break; a no, in public, landed on the project of Claire, who runs operations at Veldhaven. "We'll see" was the one answer that cost her nothing that day.

So find out the local way by asking and watching, agree how you will work, be as direct as they allow, and put the substance in writing. The message doesn't bend; the route does. "If Sunday's reset runs, I lose two days" is the same sentence in a full room, in a message or at her desk on Monday. At her desk, she could have said, "Ask finance."

### Practice: the yes that never came

**Situation.** You build software for a client five hours ahead of you. On Thursday's call their architect said of your plan to put orders on a queue, "Interesting. Let's keep it in mind." Work is due to start on Monday.

**First draft (what a careful engineer writes that evening).** "Thanks for the call! As discussed, we'll go ahead with the queue unless we hear otherwise by end of day tomorrow. Happy to jump on a quick call if there are any concerns."

**What's wrong.** "As discussed" claims an agreement, and "unless we hear otherwise" turns silence into a yes the architect never gave. "End of day tomorrow" falls at two different hours on two desks. "Any concerns" leaves the architect to write the whole objection.

**Better.** "On Thursday's call you said of the queue, 'Interesting. Let's keep it in mind.' I'm not sure whether to plan on it. May we start on Monday?

"With the queue, orders wait when your system is slow, instead of failing. I checked that with your March traffic. I haven't checked it at a year-end peak. It takes two weeks, and during the first we can still go back.

"One number is enough: 1 to start Monday, 2 for not yet and what's missing, 3 for no. If I have no answer by Friday, 16:00 your time (11:00 mine), we won't start. I'll phone you on Monday at 15:00 yours."

**Why it works.** It quotes words instead of a reading, sizes the claim, puts both clocks on the deadline and starts nothing on a silence. A no is private and costs one keystroke.

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Find an answer you are treating as a yes, though it came with no name and no day. Write their words, your one question, what you have and haven't checked, three answers they can give as a number, and what you will do without one. **Done means** a colleague who wasn't there can sort the reply into yes, not yet or no.

## Words to use

- **When the room changes language.** "Take the time you need. Could I have the result in English when you're there?"
- **When your own side does.** "We need two minutes in Spanish to get this right. Then I'll tell you what we said."
- **When you can't tell what the answer was.** "Should I plan on that, or not yet?"
- **To make a no easy to give.** "If it's a no, please tell me by Friday. I can plan around a no."
- **When nobody answers.** "I haven't heard from you, so this is still open. From [day], waiting costs us [what]."
- **Before you disagree with someone senior.** "I have a different number. Would you like it now, or after the call?"

### Phrases you will hear

- "Let's table this." (In some countries, discuss it now; in others, put it off. Ask: "Now, or later?")
- "We'll take it from here." (They have it. Ask: "Who, and by when?")
- "That could work." (It can be a yes or a maybe. Ask: "Is that a yes for Monday?")

## When it backfires

**When the question sounds like a complaint.** "What does 'we'll see' mean here?" can feel like an accusation, above all in front of others. Ask alone, about your own mistake, with your own team's habit to offer in return.

**When they hold the power.** A client may have no wish to agree habits with a supplier. Then follow theirs, and state yours as information: "A yes from us always has a day in it." Confirm what you understood in writing, so that a correction costs them one line.

**When someone quotes the page to win.** "Line two says no reply isn't a yes" wins the point and costs goodwill. Use the page to settle what a sentence meant. And it describes these people this autumn, so reopen it when someone new joins.

**When the habit isn't the problem.** Someone who keeps breaking a line they agreed, or a room that uses its language to leave you out, is a different case. So is an accent treated as a joke. Those belong to *Difficult people*.

**In a second language.** Ask for the sentence in the chat before you interpret it, because what sounds cold aloud may be plain in writing. If you lose the thread, name the last part you have: "I followed until finance. What came after that?"
