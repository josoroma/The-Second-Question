# Difficult people

Behavior you can see is all you can work with.

So this guide goes by what people do. They interrupt, correct you in public, dismiss your work, make a dig, write yes and mean no, go silent, present your work as theirs, or keep moving the date. Go to yours. For harassment, threats or danger, go to the last section.

By a Friday in mid-October, Veldhaven's word for Naomi was *unreliable*. She is the consultant in Seattle who forecasts when trucks will reach the food wholesaler's three warehouses. She can't stop being "unreliable", so the word leaves you nothing to try.

The same week, on a recording: her trial forecast for site three missed most of Tuesday afternoon's trucks. That evening Claire, who runs operations there, wrote to ask what had happened. By Friday morning Naomi hadn't replied. Those facts give you plenty to try. Did the message arrive? Is she waiting for something? Claire sent a smaller question and had an answer within minutes (*They go silent*).

Say what happened in words they would accept. Start from the kindest cause that fits the facts. Then choose your response by how much power you hold and how much you need them. If the right response fails, stop rewording it and change the route: another person, another channel.

> **Line to keep:** A label changes nothing because nobody can stop being one.

Work this way and you can get something done with almost anyone whose behavior can change. You'll also know sooner when it can't.

In six of the eight entries the difficult person is one of this book's leads: Lucía, Mateo, Diego, Bianca, Tyler and Naomi, once each. So read every entry twice, the second time for yourself.

## Before you answer

1. **Write what happened.** Who said or did what, and when, in words a recording would back. Cut every word for what they are or what they meant: *rude*, *careless*, *lazy*, *on purpose*. This is the observable version from *The exact word*: could two people who disagree about the label agree that this happened?
2. **Write your part, then theirs.** Yours first: the late draft, the vague request, the decision they heard about last. Then two innocent explanations you haven't ruled out. Some fit every entry: a norm from another team or country, a second language, a neurotype unlike yours, pressure you can't see.
3. **Sort first.** If you disagree about what is true, agree the test. If it's about what to do, agree the goal. If it's about something limited (the floor, the credit, the hours), agree a fair rule and name who decides.

Describing the act and looking for your own part are old advice. The order is my choice: yours first, while you can still see it. If you are angry, write all three now and answer later. You may hear that anger never works. Sometimes it moves a date, and then the subject is you. *Steady under pressure* shows how to hold a reply overnight.

## Confront it, or acknowledge first?

You will hear two pieces of advice about disrespect. One says confront it at once: what passes in silence can look accepted, and everyone watching is learning what is allowed. The other says acknowledge the person first: someone who feels accused may defend their standing before they hear a single fact.

Each is right about a different moment, so choose by power and by whether you need the relationship. The first time, assume an innocent cause and check. The second time, describe what happened and say what you need. A pattern, or harm to other people, goes to a formal route: someone with the authority to act. The more power they hold, the more private and written every step becomes.

| Where you stand | First time | Second time | A pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| They hold the power, and you need them | Alone, one question about the work | In writing: what happened, what you need | Dated notes to your manager, or to your sponsor on their side |
| They hold the power, and you could leave | Ask once, or let it pass | One written line, and a plan that works without them | Leave cleanly, with the work handed over |
| Peers who will keep working together | Alone, aloud, the same day | What happened, what you need, a rule for both | The person you both answer to |
| You hold the power | Ask what you did first | Alone: what happened, and what changes now | You start the formal route, and say so |

In each entry, **Upward too** is the default: written to be said to a client, a boss or an investor as well as to a peer. **Peers only** can cost you with anyone who has power over you.

## In front of others

### They interrupt

A client's director cuts in at your second sentence, on every call.

Your second sentence may be history, and the director may want the answer your first one should have held (*Give it a shape*). Or the line lags, or the thought will be gone if it waits, or talking over each other is how that team shows interest.

- **Upward too.** Put the answer first, so that a cut costs you only the reasons. Then: "That's the answer. May I add the one risk before we leave it?"
- **If you need them.** Alone, later: "When we change subject early, the risk gets lost. Should I put it first, or send it the day before?"
- **Peers only.** Once, at the same volume: "I'd like to finish this sentence."
- **When it's you.** Write your thought down, and begin with theirs.
- **When it repeats.** Ask whoever runs the call to keep a speaking order. And when someone else is cut off, speak up for them: "Could we hear the rest of that?"

### They correct you in public

Mid-October, a meeting on site two. Claire asked an engineer from Veldhaven's systems team how many door changes had failed in the night's test. "Two," he said. "It was nine," said Diego, the engineer placed at Veldhaven by Tolva, its dock-scheduling vendor. "I have the log open." Diego was right; two was the night before. The engineer didn't speak again that afternoon, and his next warning reached Diego two days late, as a ticket. "He used to walk over," the systems lead said.

Diego had cover. The two teams' page of working rules, agreed in *Across borders*, let anyone cut in to correct a number and said nothing about doing it in front of Claire. Another corrector may fear that someone will act on the wrong figure, or come from a team where a quick correction is a courtesy.

- **Upward too.** "Thank you. I'll check that and send you both the right figure by four."
- **If you need them.** Alone. The engineer's version, when Diego asked what he should have done: "Send it to me while I'm talking. I'll correct it myself."
- **Peers only.** "Nine, then. Let me finish what it means."
- **When it's you.** Cut in only if someone is about to act on the wrong figure. Then give the floor straight back: "The log says nine. Please go on."
- **When it repeats.** Agree a line. The two teams added one to their page: with somebody's boss in the room, the number goes to the speaker first.

### They dismiss your work

"No. That won't work. Next one." Mateo, Tolva's co-founder and chief technology officer, needed less than a minute. Six days earlier, on the night of 6 October, a migration that a coding agent wrote and nobody read had mislabeled site one's doors for four hours. The engineer on contract had brought a design to undo a change like that.

Mateo had seen a real fault and said only the conclusion, in his second language, after a week of short nights. Your part may be that the review was the first anyone saw of the design.

- **Upward too.** Treat the verdict as a claim and ask, alone or in writing, what it rests on. Hers, that afternoon: "You said the undo won't work. I'll fix it or drop it by Thursday. Which step fails?" His: "Step four. It wipes out every door the supervisors moved by hand. The other five are good, and I should have said so."
- **If you need them.** Before the next review: "What do you look at first in a design like this?"
- **Peers only.** In the room: "Which step? I'd rather hear it now."
- **When it's you.** Give the reason with the verdict, or the day you will have it.
- **When it repeats.** Ask for one rule: every rejection names what fails, in the notes. If reasons still don't come, ask who else can review.

### They make a dig

Tyler, Tolva's delivery lead, ran October's retrospective. "In August I wrote 'Mateo and Lucía' beside who reads the agents' work," he said. "I should have written 'whoever's awake.'" People laughed. Mateo didn't.

A joke can be a complaint that never had its own place on an agenda. Tyler had let that line go in August without asking for a day, because asking meant an argument. Mateo's part was plain: he and Lucía, his co-founder, had never set one. Elsewhere, teasing may be how a team shows affection, or a joke may have changed in translation.

- **Upward too.** Answer the true part and move the rest: "That's right, we never set a day. If there's more, I'd like it after this." Mateo said it, and Tyler came. "There is more. I should have asked you in August." Twenty minutes later they had a rule: a line in the notes gets one name, or it isn't a decision. If none of the joke is true, carry on, and ask afterward.
- **If you need them.** Alone, the same day: "Is there something about August we should put on the agenda?"
- **Peers only.** The same question aloud, with time to answer.
- **When it's you.** Say the complaint as one plain sentence, where it can be answered.
- **When it repeats.** Give both dates and say what you need: "Tell me directly, or put it on the agenda." After that, go to *When the right words fail*.

## Between meetings

### They write yes and mean no

In the week after the failed migration of 6 October, Tyler, Tolva's delivery lead, moved the engineer building site two's driver texts to other work for two days. Bianca, the product manager, replied: "Of course. The drivers have waited this long."

Writing carries no voice, and in a second language, as English is for Bianca, a flat sentence may be only flat. Tyler's part: she heard after it was decided.

- **Upward too.** Offer both readings and let them choose. Tyler wrote: "I can read this as 'two days is fine' or as 'you should have asked me first.' Which is it?" Bianca: "The second. Two days is fine. Finding out afterward isn't."
- **If you need them.** Talk, then send the read-back: what you understood, what you will do, what you are unsure of.
- **Peers only.** "That reads like a no. Is it?"
- **When it's you.** Write the no, and what you would accept.
- **When it repeats.** Ask before you decide: "What would you choose?" Then take the words as written, and say so: "I'm reading 'of course' as a yes, and I'll start at noon."

### They go silent

In mid-October Naomi's forecast missed an afternoon at site three. Claire, who runs operations at Veldhaven, asked why, heard nothing for three days, and wrote again: "I don't need the cause today. Is 23 October still good for your yes or no on site three? One line is enough." Naomi wrote back: "The 23rd holds. I don't know yet why Tuesday missed. On Monday I'll tell you what I've ruled out." She had spent three nights in the gate log, row by row, and hadn't wanted to write before she knew.

Silence can mean a message that never arrived, an answer that needs someone's approval, or a team where no reply means yes (*Across borders*). Your part may be the question. "What happened?" asked for a finished investigation.

- **Upward too.** Ask for something smaller that can be answered truthfully today: a date, a yes or no, the name of whoever knows.
- **If you need them.** Change the channel once. Phone, and ask how they are before you ask where it is.
- **Peers only.** "I've asked twice. Should I take it as a no?"
- **When it's you.** Send the smallest true line you have.
- **When it repeats.** Agree in writing what silence means and what you will do without them. Then do it.

### They present your work as theirs

Early in October, Veldhaven's finance lead asked Naomi, who sells Veldhaven its arrival forecast, why they were paying for it twice. A page from Tolva listed "our arrival forecast." Lucía, Tolva's chief executive, had written four pages and let an assistant cut them to two. The words "from Naomi" went with the rest, and Lucía signed what was left.

Shortening can lose names. "Our" can mean the project's, and in some teams work goes out under whoever presents it. Check your part: is your name on the work itself, where the next person to shorten it would see it?

- **Upward too.** Offer a cause they can accept and ask for one line. Naomi wrote: "Your page says 'our arrival forecast.' I think my name went in the shortening. Could you send finance one line today to put it back?" Lucía's, within the hour, copied to Naomi: "The forecast is Naomi's, and our scheduler reads it. The page was mine, and so is the mistake."
- **If you need them.** Before the next one: "Who presents this, and how will my part be named?"
- **Peers only.** In the room: "I ran those numbers, so send the questions to me."
- **When it's you.** Before you sign a shorter version, check whose names are gone.
- **When it repeats.** Put your name in the first line and send the work yourself to the person who decides. Where credit decides your pay or your contract, take dated examples to whoever sets it.

### They keep moving the date

Renata, Tolva's first investor, wanted the plan for sites two and three by Friday 30 October. Then by Wednesday. At nine on Tuesday evening she wrote: "Tonight?" Lucía had typed "Sure" before she stopped. She had already said it twice, at no visible cost, so why would the date hold?

Renata had been moved too. Her partners had brought their meeting forward.

- **Upward too.** Say what the new date buys, and ask what it is for. Lucía sent: "Tonight you can have the two dates and what each depends on. The cash plan is checked on Friday. Which one is tonight for?" Renata: "The dates. They meet in the morning."
- **If you need them.** Ask for the date behind the date: "What are you preparing for, and when is it?" Renata sent her partners' calendar.
- **Peers only.** "That's the third date. Choose one and I'll keep it."
- **When it's you.** Send the reason with the new date.
- **When it repeats.** Keep the dates in one written line: "The 30th, then the 28th, then tonight." Ask who may move it, and how much notice you both accept. *Saying no and pushing back* covers the date you must refuse.

## When the right words fail

A data analyst shows a sales team the numbers every Monday. One Monday the head of sales says, "Story time." She checks, alone: "Is something wrong in the numbers?" "No, no. It's a joke." The next Monday, again. This time she describes it: "On the 5th and the 12th you introduced the numbers as 'story time,' and nobody asked a question after it. Please introduce them as the numbers." He agrees. Two weeks later, with a customer on the call: "And now, story time."

She did every step well. A response works only if the other person keeps their side of it, and he didn't. Asking him a third time would only repeat the second, so she changes the route and writes to her manager:

"On 5, 12 and 26 October the numbers were introduced as 'story time,' the last with a customer present. On the 14th I asked him to stop, and he agreed. I'd like you to take this to his manager, and to tell me by Friday what will happen."

It took two weeks. The jokes stopped. The two of them are polite, and no more than that. The formal route was simply the next step, and her notes were ready for it.

> **From practice:** Write the note the same day, in the words that were said. A note like that can be checked by someone who wasn't there, and it protects the other person from your memory as much as it protects you.

### Practice: the decision that comes undone

**Situation.** Twice, a peer has said yes in a design review and then asked the manager you share to reopen the choice. You write to him.

**First draft (what a careful engineer writes).**
"I wanted to raise something that's been on my mind for a while. In March you were fine with the queue, and then I heard from our manager that there were concerns, so we reopened it. I let that go. Now the same thing has happened with the cache. I may be reading too much into it, and I know you're busy, but I felt a bit undermined. It would be great if we could be more transparent going forward."

**What's wrong.** It is history in the order it happened, with the request at the end. "Undermined" is a label he can deny, and "more transparent" is nothing he could be seen to do. The hedges protect the writer and leave the one real doubt unsaid: half of this is secondhand.

**Better.**
"On Tuesday you said yes to the cache. On Thursday our manager asked me to reopen it and said the concern was yours. The queue went the same way in March. I only have the second half from her, so I may have it wrong. What did you need on Tuesday that the review didn't give you?"

He writes back: "Time. You two had decided before I'd finished reading."

"So you need the design before the meeting, and I'll send it the day before. What I'm unsure of: will you say it in the review if you're still against it?"

**Why it works.** Two dated events, what they rest on, what may be wrong, and a question that assumes he had a reason. He had one, and the writer was part of it. The read-back turns his reason into two things either of them could check.

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Take something that has happened twice. Change the names. Write the latest event with its day, then the earlier one, what you haven't seen yourself, and a question about what they needed. Keep your request until they answer. **Done means** a colleague finds no word in it for what the person is, and can tell what you saw from what you heard.

### Practice: two explanations

**If you do only one, do this one.** **With a person.** Choose someone you have called difficult this month, in a case that doesn't belong under *What is not a wording problem*. Write one line a recording would back, then your part, then two innocent explanations. This week, ask that person one question about the work that could tell the two apart, alone: "Did you get the plan before the call?" Watch for a reason that wasn't on your list. **Done means** someone who likes them reads your line and agrees it happened, and you can say which explanation the answer ruled out.

## Words to use

- **To open.** "Can I check something about Tuesday with you?" "I have only my half of Tuesday. What's yours?"
- **To ask.** "Next time, could you [one act] before [one moment]?"
- **To close.** "So from [day], I'll [act] and you'll [act]. What would you change?"
- **To change the route.** "I don't think the two of us can settle this. I'm asking [name] to help, and I wanted you to hear it from me." (Only when it is safe to tell them.)

### Phrases you will hear

- "No offense, but..." (a criticism follows; listen for the fact in it)
- "I'm just saying." (that was a complaint, and I'd rather not defend it)
- "With all due respect..." (I disagree strongly)

## When it backfires

**They hold the power.** Naming a boss's behavior, however well, can cost you, and anything written can be forwarded. Ask a question about the work, keep your notes, and find out who could act before you need them.

**They deny it, or push harder.** Don't argue the account a second time. "We remember it differently. From next week, I'm asking for [one act]." Then write your note. If pushing harder becomes a threat or a penalty for speaking, go to *What is not a wording problem*.

**A private word isn't private everywhere.** In some teams an email describing someone's behavior is an accusation, and in others so is a closed door. Ask someone who knows the team which is lighter.

**An assistant as the judge.** Paste in the thread, ask "Is she undermining me?", and it has only your side to answer from. Ask it to strike every word a recording wouldn't back.

**Too much of it.** A note on every joke makes you hard to work with. If three people separately describe the same act of yours, take it as yours.

**In a second language.** You don't need a delicate phrase. Write short sentences first: the day, what they did, what you haven't seen, your question. If you can't tell whether a line was a dig, ask if there's something they'd like changed; you may be right to wonder, and the question is safe either way.

## What is not a wording problem

Some behavior has no entry here, on purpose. Harassment, discrimination, threats, retaliation for speaking up, a risk to someone's safety, anything unlawful: none of these is a wording problem. If one is happening to you, it isn't your fault, and it isn't your job to phrase your way out. You don't owe the person a private word first. This book gives you no script for it, because you shouldn't have to handle it alone.

What can help is a record and other people. Write dated notes: what was said or done, where, who was there. Keep them where you will still be able to reach them, within the rules on confidential material. Use formal channels: your employer's complaint procedure, human resources, a manager you trust. If you work as a contractor or alone, ask whether the client's procedure covers you. Look for outside help too: a lawyer, a public body that takes complaints, a support line. If anyone is in danger now, call the emergency services first.

If you see it happen to someone else, write down what you saw and ask them what help they want. Some roles carry a duty to report; find out whether yours does. If your company is too small for any procedure, write one page before it is needed: whom to tell, and a second name in case the first is the problem.

Rules and time limits differ by country and by employer, some are short, and this is not legal advice. Ask someone qualified where you are, and ask early.
