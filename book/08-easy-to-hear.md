# Easy to hear

Fifteen or fifty?

Nobody asked it at Tolva's stand-up on a Tuesday early in June. Two days later a test answered it, with a door that stood empty for forty minutes.

Tyler, the delivery lead, runs Tolva's stand-ups, planning and retrospectives. English is one of the languages he grew up with. He is the fastest speaker on the call, and nobody has ever told him so.

That Tuesday he passed on a request from the manager of site one, the first of Veldhaven's three warehouses due to go live with Tolva's dock scheduler, in September. "They want a late truck to lose its slot after fifteen minutes, so let's set that before Thursday's replay." An engineer in Montevideo heard fifty and typed it into the test configuration.

On Thursday the team replayed a busy morning at site one on the test copy of Veldhaven's warehouse system. Diego, the engineer Tolva placed in Rotterdam, reported back in the team channel. "Door nine has been empty for forty minutes, held for a late truck, while two trucks that arrived on time are waiting. The configuration says fifty minutes. Is that what site one asked for?"

At Friday's stand-up the engineer said, "Tyler said fifty." Tyler said, "I said fifteen." Both were sure. "It doesn't matter who said what," Tyler said. "We set fifteen and run it again on Monday." He moved to the next item.

It was a generous thing to say, and it settled nothing: the team never decided how the next number would travel from a site manager to the configuration. Tyler is growing toward the problem-solver, one of the six thinker types: the habit of taking a mess with people in it to a decision that lasts.

## Tyler's sheet

Tyler couldn't prove what he had said on Tuesday. He could find out which words his calls were losing. For two weeks he wrote down every word someone asked to hear again, and every number or name someone acted on in the wrong version. Most were his own. One was Diego's: a sentence about site two's door list that Bianca, Tolva's product manager, asked him to repeat. By the third week of June Tyler had a page, one line per habit, each in his team's own words:

| Said | Came back as | The habit |
|---|---|---|
| "after fifteen minutes" | "fifty" | Numbers and names at half speed, then another way |
| "can't send it before Friday" | "Can or can't?" | Finish the ends of words |
| "By six, forty trucks" | "Six-forty?" | Pause where a comma would be |
| "The replay moves to Monday" | "What moves?" | Stress the one word that carries the point |
| "so we test on Thursday" | "Test when?" | Breathe before the sentence that matters |
| "Morning, Tyler here, quick one" | "You cut out at the start" | Slow the first sentence |

Half speed is the pace this book sets for numbers and names. The first line is Tuesday's sentence, so start there.

### Practice: one number, heard the first time

**Situation.** Tuesday's stand-up. Tyler passes on site one's rule for late trucks before Thursday's replay.

**First draft (what a fluent speaker says at full speed).** "They want a late truck to lose its slot after fifteen minutes, so let's set that before Thursday's replay."

**What's wrong.** Every word goes at the same speed, so nothing tells the listener which one to keep. The number sits in the middle, just before "minutes". Said alone, "fifteen" has its weight on "-teen". Before another stressed word, the weight can move to "fif-", and "fifty" has its weight there too. The final "n" that would still tell them apart is a quiet sound at the end of a word, and quiet sounds are the easiest to lose. And the number exists only in the air.

**Better.** "One rule from site one, for Thursday's replay. A late truck loses its slot after fifteen minutes. That's one-five. I'll put it in the chat." Pause and breathe after "replay". Say the number at half speed, with the weight on "-teen" and the "n" finished.

**Why it works.** The first sentence says what is coming before it comes. The rule gets a short sentence of its own, and the weight stays on "-teen" even before "minutes". Said another way, as "one-five", the number can't be heard as fifty. The chat holds it for anyone whose line dropped.

**Your turn (5 minutes).** Take one sentence with a number from tomorrow's update and fit it to this pattern: `[the number, in a short sentence]. That's [the same number another way]. [Where it is written].` The last three sentences of the better version fill it in. Say yours aloud three times, slowing down only for the number. **Done means** a colleague who hears it once writes the number down correctly.

## Six drills, five minutes

Each line has a drill of its own. They are optional, and together they take five minutes, a target this book sets. Tyler did all six before every stand-up; your own sheet, below, drills one line a week:

- **Fifteen (one minute).** Count from thirteen to nineteen with the weight on "-teen" and every final "n" finished, then the tens, thirty to ninety, with the weight up front. Take three numbers from your week, a door, a version, a price, and say each at half speed, then another way. Say a name the listener hasn't met at half speed. Your own name is the word you have said most often and your listener has heard least.
- **Can't (one minute).** Say pairs from your week, finishing the last sound of every word: "We can test Thursday." "We can't test Thursday." "We test the feed." "We tested the feed." Each pair differs at the end of a word. When a "not" decides something, don't trust it to one letter: say "cannot", or say what is true: "The door list comes on Friday at the earliest."
- **Six, forty (thirty seconds).** Read aloud: "By six, forty trucks." "By six-forty, four trucks." Pause at the comma long enough to breathe. Then mark the commas in tomorrow's notes, above all where two numbers meet.
- **Monday (one minute).** Say "The replay moves to Monday" three times, with the weight on "replay", then on "moves", then on "Monday". Each weight answers a different question. The listener who asked "What moves?" didn't know what was moving, so "replay" was the news; for someone who knew, it would have been "Monday". Before a call, underline the one word in each important sentence that is new to the listener, and give it the weight.
- **Thursday (one minute).** Find the sentence in tomorrow's update that carries the decision. Breathe in before it starts, not halfway through, and say it on that breath with the last word as strong as the first. If it won't fit on one breath, make it two sentences.
- **The first sentence (thirty seconds).** Say tomorrow's first sentence at half your usual speed, then at full speed, then somewhere between, and keep that one. Make it short: who is speaking and what is coming. Someone may still be unmuting.

None of the six says to slow everything down. Slow down where a lost word would cost the most: the first sentence, a number, a name, the word that carries the point. Everywhere else, your own pace is fine. Slow every word, and the ones that matter stop standing out.

At the last stand-up in June, Tyler passed on site two's version of the rule: "Site two wants thirty minutes. Three-zero. It's in the chat." Nobody had to ask.

## The one person who never hears it

On Tuesday Tyler said what he meant, and the engineer typed what they heard. Tolva's stand-up is a video call, at ten in the morning in Montevideo and three in the afternoon in Rotterdam, and everyone joins from their own screen. A video call is harder on words than a room is: sound arrives late and thinned, faces are small or missing, and someone may be unmuting as you start. A listener who loses a word may not stop you, because it is easier to fill the gap with the likeliest word and keep listening. Fifty was a likely word. So was fifteen. And the speaker is the one person who never hears the mistake: Tyler heard himself say fifteen.

Neither side catches the mistake as it happens, so the speaker has to prevent it. Being easy to hear is a handful of physical habits, used on the words someone will act on. They are small enough to train in five minutes a day, and they work with the accent you have. You can start before tomorrow's first call.

> **Line to keep:** Every word you rush, someone at the other end has to rebuild.

### Practice: your own sheet

**If you do only one, do this one.** It takes ten minutes today and five a day after that.

1. Write down the last words someone asked you to repeat, or acted on wrongly: numbers, names, dates, a "not", a term your team shortens. Then play one of your recordings from *Start here* to a colleague, and add every word they had to guess.
2. Give each word its habit from Tyler's sheet, and keep one line per habit.
3. Write each line as a sentence you will really say this month.
4. Each day before your first call, read the sheet aloud once (a minute), drill this week's line (three minutes) and say that call's first sentence (a minute). Change the line each week, and add a word whenever someone says "sorry?"
5. Once a week, record the sheet. Listen once for pace, once for a pause where each comma is, and once for whether every word can be heard. Record again the one sentence that didn't come through.

*Safe practice: record only yourself, or others only with their consent and within your employer's rules.*

**Done means** a colleague who hears your sheet's sentences once writes down every number, name and date in them correctly.

## Practice: sixty seconds, interrupted

**Timed drill (60 seconds).** Say your next update live to a colleague, who interrupts once, whenever they choose, with "Sorry, which number?" Constraint: you may not say any number the same way twice. Saying the same words louder only repeats the puzzle. Answer in another form, then carry on from where you stopped, not from the beginning.

**Written form.** Before your next call, write another form beside every number, name and date in your notes, and use it the first time anyone asks.

**Done means** your colleague writes down every number and name in your update correctly, including the one they asked about.

## Words to use

- **A number someone will act on.** "Fifteen minutes. That's one-five." "It's in the chat now."
- **A date.** "[day] [month name], a [weekday]." Use the month's name, because the order of day and month in figures depends on where your listener works.
- **A time, across time zones.** "[time], [city] time."
- **A version.** "[version], that's [the digits one by one]."
- **A letter that sounds like another.** "[letter] as in [word]." Use the same word every time.
- **A "not" that decides something.** "We cannot test on Thursday. The earliest is Friday."
- **The first sentence.** "[Name] here. One change to [what]."
- **A name.** "My name is [name]. I'll type it in the chat." "Could you spell that for me?"
- **When you didn't hear.** "Sorry, fifteen or fifty?" "Was that 'can' or 'cannot'?" "I missed the end of that sentence. Could you say it again?"
- **When someone asks again, or repeats it wrong.** "Let me say it another way: [the other form]. I'll type it now."

### Phrases you will hear

- "You're breaking up." Your sound is cutting out.
- "I didn't catch that." I didn't hear it.
- "I lost you there." Either your sound dropped or they lost the thread: ask which.
- "Loud and clear." I can hear you well.
- "A couple of days." Two days, or a few: ask for a date.
- "Half eight." In some varieties of English, half past eight. Someone translating word for word from another language may mean half past seven. Say "eight thirty".

## When it backfires

**Overuse.** Give every figure twice and you sound like someone reading out a phone number, and the listener may hear that you doubt them. Keep the second form for numbers someone will act on, and drop it once they say "got it".

**When they hold the power.** Someone senior and in a hurry may hear half speed as hesitation. Keep it for the number and the name, and say the rest at your normal pace. If a senior person repeats your number wrong, it is tempting to let it pass. Correct it at once: say it another way, and type it. A number needs no apology.

**Where asking feels rude.** In some teams people would rather guess than ask a colleague to say something again. Don't wait to be asked. The second form and the chat do the asking for them.

**When the line fails.** No habit survives a connection that drops every third word. After the second "You're breaking up", stop and type, or call back. Notes an assistant writes from a call transcript hold only what the transcript caught, so check their numbers before anyone acts on them.

**In a second language.** Start with the numbers line: it uses only sounds you already make. The stress drill may feel strange if your first language spreads its weight more evenly; stress only the one word that carries the point. If "can" and "can't" are hard to tell apart in your own mouth or ear, say "cannot" whenever it decides something. The drills never ask you to lose your accent; they ask whether a word arrived.

**If you stutter.** Nothing here asks you to be fluent. Skip any timing, and judge a take only on whether each number and name arrived.
