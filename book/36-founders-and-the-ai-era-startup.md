# Founders and the AI-era startup

Hugo has read the proposal twice. He runs operations at Sarnik, a chilled and frozen food distributor near Lyon. On Tuesday 3 November he is forty minutes into a video call with Lucía, co-founder and chief executive of Tolva, a dock-scheduling company. The proposal is good, and an assistant helped draft it.

He has one question left, which the proposal does not answer.

"You are fourteen people," he says. "Say I sign. It is two in the morning in my busiest week, your scheduler stops, and forty trucks are waiting at my gate. Who picks up the phone?"

"For the first ninety days, I do, with our support engineer. Two in the morning in Lyon is ten at night in Montevideo. After that, a team of three, named in the contract. Since our largest client went live in September, its scheduler has failed twice at night. Once it stalled for forty minutes. Once an agent-written change mislabeled doors for four hours. It had passed its tests, and it shipped unread. Now a named engineer reads and signs every change an agent writes, and checks it against tests a person wrote. I can send both incident reports tonight."

"Claire told me the same in October," Hugo says. Claire runs operations at that client; he had called her for a reference in its worst month. "I wanted to hear whether you would. Send me the reports."

## What is shifting

In many small companies, agents now draft much of the code, tests, proposals and documents (reported). That makes polish cheap: the proposal Hugo read looked like one from a vendor ten times Tolva's size. A person still has to say, on the call, who answers when the system breaks.

*Reported* is testimony, often from people selling the tools; *checked* has a published finding behind it; *our bet* is this book's reasoning. The grades run from *durable* to *open* (full key: *Engineers and architects*).

| What is shifting | Basis | Grade | The step worth taking either way |
|---|---|---|---|
| A few people and agents do the work of a larger team | Reported | Reasonable bet | Write down what agents do and who reads it first |
| A polished proposal no longer shows size or care, so buyers test you live | Our bet | Reasonable bet | Prepare live answers, starting with who picks up the phone |
| People overseeing automation can miss what it missed, even experts, even in pairs | Checked | Durable | Name who signs each agent-written change; test it against work the agent did not write |
| Customers want one named person answering for the result, however it was made | Our bet | Reasonable bet: watch whether contracts name an owner | Agree that name before anyone asks, and have a lawyer read the line |
| Investors ask how far a few people and agents can go before hiring | Our bet | Open: watch what investors ask | Know your answer, with numbers, before the meeting |

The smaller the company, the more of its trust runs through a few live conversations, and most of them are the founder's (our bet). Five of them change when agents do much of the work. They are selling with no team behind you, telling a customer what a machine did and who checked it, hiring and letting go, the co-founder disagreement and the investor's timeline. Hold them well, and a company of fourteen, or a founder alone, can be trusted with work that matters.

> **Line to keep:** However many agents do the work, the customer still asks for one name.

## Sell the names you have

In the second week of November Sarnik's finance director, who signs, read a line of the contract aloud. "A team of three. Who are they?"

"I promised three, and I have two," Lucía said. "Our support engineer, and Mateo, my co-founder, who shares the nights. The third isn't hired. Until there's a name in that line, I stay on the phone, past the ninety days."

"So while it's empty, I get the chief executive," Hugo said.

"You do."

"Then hire slowly."

On Wednesday 18 November Sarnik signed for one warehouse, at August's price of 5,000 euros a month. Each further one will cost 4,400, because the connection is built once. The third name is blank, with its rule beside it.

What a customer needs is who answers, from when until when, and what happens where there's nobody. An empty line with a rule can be planned around; an invented name can't.

### Practice: the name on the phone

**If you do only one, do this one.** Take a customer's worst hour and fill this in: `From [when] to [when], [name] answers, by [how]. Nobody holds [which line] yet. Until somebody does, [what happens].`

Naomi's, for the forecast she sells alone from Seattle: "From ten in the morning to ten at night, Seattle time, I answer, by phone. That's your night. Nobody holds your day yet. Until somebody does, your planners use the carriers' booked times."

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Say yours aloud to someone outside your company, or send it. **Done means** they can tell you who answers at that hour, and which line is empty.

## Say what the check can miss

On Friday 6 November Hugo called back, both reports read. "Your engineer signs. Engineers get tired. What happens when the signature is wrong?"

The reports could be prepared. This answer has to be known.

### Practice: when the signature is wrong

**First draft (what a careful founder says).** "We take that very seriously. There's a human in the loop on every change, and since October we're far more rigorous."

**What's wrong.** "A human in the loop" has no name, nobody can check "rigorous", and he asked about the day the check fails.

**Better.** "Then a wrong change goes live. In October our client's reviewer hid a fault in each of three changes: reading found two, and tests a person wrote found the third. Three is a small test. If both miss, your supervisors can still move a truck from the dock screen, and the phone you asked about rings."

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Take the last machine-drafted thing that reached a customer. Say who checked it against what, what that check could miss, and what is left then. **Done means** a listener can name the person, the check and its gap.

**Spar with an AI.** Aloud or in writing:

```text
Play an operations director deciding whether to depend on my very small company.
Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer.
Ask who answers when it breaks, what machines made, and who checked it.
When I give you a label where you need a name or a number, ask again.
After six questions, list the answers you could repeat to your finance director.
My company: [two lines, names and figures changed]
```

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## "Is it the agents?"

On Friday 13 November Lucía called an engineer on contract since August and gave the news first. "Your contract ends with site three, on the 23rd, as written, and I'm not extending it. Nothing in your work is the reason." Then she stopped.

"Is it the agents?" the engineer asked.

### Practice: the part you chose

**First draft (what a kind founder says).** "No, not at all. It's only the budget, and the timing. I hope we'll work together again."

**What's wrong.** She has seen what the agents draft, so the "no" won't hold, and "only the budget" leaves out the second support hire.

**Better.** "Partly. Agents draft some of what I'd have asked you to do next, and I can't tell you how much that weighed. The rest I chose: the next salary goes to a second support person, because only two people share our nights. Your reference will name your work, and you'll read it first."

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Write the reason for a decision about somebody's work in three lines: the part machines play, the part you chose, the part you don't know. **Done means** a listener can say which part you chose.

A candidate for that salary asked it from the other side: "If agents keep improving, which part of this job is mine in a year?"

"Agents draft our migrations and some tests," Lucía said. "You'd decide what a customer hears at two in the morning, with the access to fix it. What they'll draft in a year, I don't know."

Describe a job by what its holder answers for, and it needs less rewriting when drafting changes (our bet).

## Write down who decides

On Friday 20 November Lucía and Mateo, who runs technology, held the conversation they had put off since July: how much to hand to agents, and who reviews it. She wanted agents on anything with a test. He wanted them only where he could read every line. October's change went out unread because neither had said whose hours reading takes.

They sorted first. October's test had mostly settled what is true: how much reading catches. The page of standards Tolva wrote on 4 November says what to do. What was left was something limited: hours for reading, and who spends them.

"You gave an agent the invoices," Mateo said, "and I found out from the log."

"You took one off the test scripts, and I found out in a meeting."

"So the agents had two bosses."

Each wrote a page alone: ten recent decisions, one name beside each. Seven names matched; three took the afternoon. What agents may write is Mateo's; what customers are told about it is Lucía's; how many hours go to reading is Mateo's, once he has asked her.

Then the harder page. "If you left in March," Lucía said, "nobody could run Veldhaven's integration."

"If you left, nobody could answer Hugo."

So by the end of January another engineer runs a night without Mateo, and Mateo holds a customer call without Lucía. When their first investor came in, lawyers wrote down who owns what, and nobody wrote down who decides what, or what a founder who leaves keeps. That list now has a lawyer and a date.

Do it while you agree. Shares, pay and tax depend on your country and your contracts: take the list to a lawyer where you're registered, and copy nobody's split. None of this is legal advice.

### Practice: two pages, swapped

**Your turn (15 minutes, then the conversation).** Alone, list the last ten decisions that moved money, people or a customer, one name beside each. Add who decides, and what stops, in the month after either of you goes. Swap pages and talk only about the lines that differ. Open with: "I've put this off since [month], and that part is mine. Where do we both think we decide?" **Done means** a colleague can say who decides each line without asking either of you.

## Ask what their date rests on

On Thursday 12 November Tolva's first investor, Renata, phoned from São Paulo. "Could you open the next round in January, while people are talking about you?"

Lucía asked what she had learned in October to ask. "What does your fund need, and by when?"

"This fund can add money to Tolva until June. After that I can only give you advice." The date was real, and it was the fund's.

### Practice: two clocks

**First draft (what a founder says without asking).** "January could work. With agents we can grow without hiring, so the numbers should look great."

**What's wrong.** It agrees to a date nobody has explained, and nothing stands under its claim about agents.

**Better.** "Then your date is June. Mine rests on Veldhaven's December, which has to be counted. I'd open in March if its gate logs are in by 15 February; if not, April. You'll hear that day. Cash runs to September, counting only what's signed, if site three goes live on the 23rd."

"And how far do fourteen people and agents go before you hire?"

"Nights run out first. Two people share them, and each new customer needs one more. I reasoned that from six weeks at one site, so I'll count again in January."

That view came from her own nights, in words Renata could test. It is worth more than a borrowed one.

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Ask one person whose money has a date (an investor, a bank, a budget holder) what it rests on. Write yours under it. **Done means** they confirm theirs in one sentence, and yours names the one thing it depends on.

## If the team is you

Naomi, a data consultant, holds all five alone. Her rule for nights covers the weeks she is ill. When an assistant drafts her weekly note, the customer knows, and knows what she checked it against. Write what stops when you stop, and who tells the customer.

## Three moves for this month

1. **Put a name where a phrase is.** First step: find "dedicated support" in a contract you signed, and send that customer a name and hours.
2. **Count what runs out first.** First step: for four weeks, log hours spent reading machine-made work and calls answered at night.
3. **Hold the conversation you have put off.** First step: send one sentence with the subject and an hour in it.

Put figures in writing; say live who answers, what you got wrong and whose work is ending. One sentence only you can stand behind: "I chose what we check, I know what we don't check yet, and I answer for both."

To see whether this still holds, once a year ask two customers what they had to ask you live, and look for names in your last three contracts. If both come up empty, our bets were wrong.

## For everyone who works with a founder

Ask who answers when it breaks, and until when. Ask what a machine made, who checked it against what, and what nobody checks yet. If you work there, ask which decisions are one founder's alone. If you invest, say what your date rests on.

Don't stop at the team's size: it doesn't say who will answer. Stop asking "Do you use AI?": every vendor can say yes.

## Words to use

- "I promised [three], and I have [two]. Until that line is filled, [what happens]."
- "A machine made [what]. [Name] checked it against [what]. It could still miss [what]."
- "Partly. The part I chose is [what]."
- "What does your date rest on?" "Mine rests on [one thing]."

## When it backfires

**They want a bigger vendor.** An empty line can lose you the sale. Never fill it with a name that hasn't agreed.

**The customer holds the power.** Answer their question first, live; then send the names in writing.

**The failure isn't yours alone to tell.** Lucía left the client's name out. Check what your contract lets you say, and ask a lawyer before you add a name to one.

**Every name is yours.** Then you run out first. Lucía's ninety days had an end; give yours one.

**You are the one ending the work.** A reason you give someone you're letting go is for them; if it only eases you, shorten it. Rules on notice, and on stating a reason, differ by country: ask someone qualified first.

**A page can't settle it.** If one founder no longer feels free to disagree, bring in somebody from outside first.

**In a second language.** Send names, hours and dates in writing the same day, with the month spelled out.
