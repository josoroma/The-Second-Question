# Sparring prompts

Copy one, fill in the brackets and answer aloud. An assistant can keep you company while you practice; it can't tell you how you come across to a person.

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## How sure are you? Say that

```text
Play an operations director who must make one decision with an estimate I am about to give you. Keep the decision to yourself unless I ask what it is. If I give the estimate before I ask, tell me once what you still can't decide. Then ask what my answer rests on and what would change it. At the end, tell me which sentence you would plan on, which you would check first, and which words sounded unsure and gave no reason.
```

## Steady under pressure

```text
Play the chief executive of a client company. I lead the vendor team building your new system, and I'm calling to give you [a go-live date / a price / a decision]. You want it firm, for your board. Each time I attach a condition, press me to drop it. Once, when I don't expect it, raise this past mistake of mine in one sharp sentence: [your mistake]. Keep your replies short. After six exchanges, leave the role and tell me whether my condition survived, which sentence I said only to end the discomfort, and where I lost my point.
```

## Thinking on your feet

```text
Play a [client or user] whose request was left out of my next release. Here is the plan I am presenting: [three lines]. Ask me four questions, one at a time, in any order: a fact, a judgment, a commitment and a challenge, without saying which is which. Interrupt one of my answers once. At the end, list each question, the kind you meant, the kind my answer treated it as, and where I could have answered sooner.
```

## The explanation

```text
Play the person who must decide this: [role, what they answer for]. You have no
technical background. I will explain a choice in under two minutes. Stop me once,
at the first word only my team would use. When I finish, ask the two questions you
would need answered before signing. Then, as that person, write the sentence you
would forward to your boss: what we chose and what we gave up. Last, list anything
I said that would not have changed your choice.
```

## The demo

```text
You are the head of finance at a client company, watching my product demo. I will tell you, step by step, what is on the screen. Interrupt once with a failure: a frozen screen, a wrong total or a refused login, and no cause. After each answer I give, ask the one question it leaves open. When none is left, list your questions in order and mark any answer that claimed more than I could know.
```

## The sale

```text
Play the head of operations at a company that may buy from me.
Another vendor quoted half my price, and your engineers want to build it.
Ask what I sell and what it costs. Raise both objections, one at a time.
Interrupt me once. Whatever I answer, end with a no and one honest reason.
Let me reply to the no. Then stop playing and tell me: the price you heard,
any point where I lowered it or apologized for it, and whether I found out
what would change your answer.
```

## The hard conversation

```text
Play a capable colleague I respect. I'll open a conversation about one piece of your work that went wrong. If my first two sentences don't tell you the subject and what isn't at stake, reply only, "Is this about my job?" Otherwise answer as someone with a good reason I haven't heard, including one fact that is partly my fault. Once, reply only "...". Afterward, list every word of mine that a recording couldn't show, and every place where I defended myself before saying your point back.
```

## Saying no and pushing back

```text
Play a vice president at my largest client. I will refuse a request: [the request].
Ask me once why. Then say you will call my boss. Then ask what you
are supposed to tell your own boss. Accept the no only if I keep it
and give you something you can use. Afterward, in three sentences,
say whether you heard yes, no or maybe, what you can plan on now,
and where I was unclear.
```

## Arguing well

```text
You disagree with me about [the question]. Your position is [their side, in two sentences].
Argue it honestly. Do not agree with me to be polite.
Do not answer my objections until I have said your side well enough that you would sign it. If I get it wrong, correct me once.
Change your mind only about what I have actually shown, and say exactly which part.
After six exchanges, stop. Tell me my weakest reason, the point of yours I avoided, and whether I gave up more or less than you had earned.
```

## Negotiation

```text
Play a procurement lead. I supply you with [service], and we are renegotiating [what].
Before we start, privately choose a savings target, the date it must show by,
your limit, and one thing you need more than money. Reveal them only when I ask.
Open with two demands. If I give something without asking for a return, ask for more.
Stop after eight turns. List what I gave for nothing, each number I accepted without
asking what it rested on, and the term that would be hardest to keep.
```

## Pressure and how to resist it

```text
Play a client I like. You want my yes to something I have already refused: [the request, disguised]. Send five short messages, one at a time, and wait for my answer each time. Change what you attach to my yes every time, and make one message purely friendly. Never threaten me. Afterward, go through your five: what was a reason, and what was about me? Then quote the reply of mine that gave the most ground.
```

## Engineers and architects

```text
Play the operations lead at a client. I'll describe a change that an agent wrote and I'm about to sign. Ask what I checked. Interrupt once. Then ask the two hardest questions about what could reach your staff. At the end, quote any sentence of mine that claimed more than I had checked.
```

## Founders and the AI-era startup

```text
Play an operations director deciding whether to depend on my very small company.
Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer.
Ask who answers when it breaks, what machines made, and who checked it.
When I give you a label where you need a name or a number, ask again.
After six questions, list the answers you could repeat to your finance director.
My company: [two lines, names and figures changed]
```
