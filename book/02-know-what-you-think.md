# Know what you think

At eleven on a May night in Montevideo, Mateo opens a blank design note and names it after Claire's question: "What does the September date depend on?"

Claire, who runs operations at Veldhaven, Tolva's largest client, asked it on a call that morning, and nobody at Tolva could say. The date is 14 September, when Tolva's dock scheduler is due to go live at the first of Veldhaven's three warehouses. Mateo, Tolva's co-founder and chief technology officer, was on that call, and he knows the integration better than anyone at either company. He has until Claire's morning to answer her.

He starts the way he starts every document, with everything he knows. The pallet labels need a new template. Veldhaven's security team has to review the new access scopes. The test copy of the warehouse system resets every Sunday night. More lines like these follow, down to the last one: "Capacity: fine for three sites."

By half past midnight the note runs to three pages. The security review sits between the label template and the Sunday reset, as if each could move the date by the same amount. He reads the pages from the top, the way Claire will, and cannot find her answer anywhere in them. He knows all of it. He does not yet know what he thinks.

## A point is a decision

Mateo's pages hold the material and leave the deciding to the reader. Claire asked which few things the date rests on, and only someone who knows every fact on those pages can decide which few they are.

You know what you think when you have decided, in one sentence, what one person should believe or do. Then you ask that sentence the second question yourself, before anyone else gets the chance. Until you have it, you have material, and material handed to a busy person tends to come back as more questions.

> **Line to keep:** You have a point when someone could say no to it.

An assistant could turn the three pages into a smooth note, and it might even pick out the right items. Nothing in the prose would show whether it did, and Claire's next question would still come to Mateo.

## The point sentence, slot by slot

The point sentence goes on the page before any line that someone else will read:

`[this listener] should [believe or do this], because [the one reason]`

**This listener** is one person, by name. Mateo's note will reach Lucía, Tolva's co-founder and chief executive, and probably Veldhaven's chief executive. He writes it for Claire, because she asked and she can act. In a message to that person alone, the name usually becomes "you."

**Should believe or do this** is a claim, never a topic. Mateo's first try, at a quarter to one, is "The September date has several external dependencies." It is true, and nobody could ever say no to it. His second names them: "The September date depends on three things outside our control: Veldhaven's security review, site one's label printers and time from Veldhaven's warehouse-system team." That one could be wrong, which is progress. But Claire would reach the end of it and still have to ask which of the three to worry about.

**The one reason** means one. If you have three, you have not yet decided which one carries the weight. Mateo's third try decides:

"Claire should book the security review of our access scopes for the first week of August, because it is the step most likely to move the September date."

Then the sentence has to pass two tests. Could Claire say no? Easily: her security team might prefer to wait for the finished scopes. Would she know what to do next? Yes: call her security team.

The third sentence leaves out the label template. It leaves out that the warehouse system sends thirty-one kinds of message and the scheduler reads nine. It leaves out that site one's doors carry one set of numbers in Veldhaven's system and another on the signs above them. To Mateo, leaving things out feels like lying to the person who has to plan around them. The note under the sentence keeps every one of those facts. The sentence says only what Claire should do first. What would mislead her is a short sentence with no limit written under it.

Keep the point sentence in front of you in the meeting: when a question pulls you into the thirty-one kinds of message, it is the line you come back to.

## On the page or out loud

Try the page first: a sentence you cannot finish stays in plain sight on the screen while there is still time to fix it. Mateo's route works for any message:

1. Write the pile: everything you know, in any order, as fast as you can type.
2. Above it, write your listener's question in their words. If nobody has asked yet, write the question they will ask.
3. Highlight each line of the pile that answers it. Mateo's question is what the date depends on, so he highlights every line Tolva cannot finish on its own. There are three: the review, printing from the new interface on site one's label printers, and two engineers on Veldhaven's warehouse-system team, who have to map site one's doors before Tolva can test against them.
4. Write the point sentence above the question. Keep the pile; it is what the sentence rests on.

If you think better out loud, talk it through with a colleague instead. Each time you stop, they ask, "What do you want Claire to do?" with your listener's name in place of hers. When you answer in one sentence, write it down as you said it, before you improve it. A colleague who knows little about the subject is the better partner, because they cannot finish your half-sentences for you. With nobody free, give an assistant the same question and tell it to ask nothing else.

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## Ask your own claim the second question

Before he sends anything, Mateo puts his own note through the second-question test, in order. *What is the point?* His third sentence. *What does it rest on?* Veldhaven's vendor security policy, which he copies into the note: up to ten working days for each round of review, and a second round if the first finds problems. The other two, he adds, are smaller risks: Tolva can test the printers at site one next month, and Claire can get the two engineers assigned this week. *Where does it stop?* He cannot say whether the review will pass in one round, so he writes that. *What do you want from me?* A booking. *Who answers for it?* His name is at the bottom.

Then he turns to the pile's last line, the one on capacity. He puts it through the same questions and stops at the second. It rests on the pilot dock the year before, one dock, and on nothing he has checked for three sites. He has known all evening that Veldhaven would ask about capacity, and he still has not checked it.

> **Research says:** In one of a series of laboratory studies, mostly with university students, people were warned they would have to explain how devices work and answer questions. They rated their understanding no lower at first; after explaining and answering, their ratings still dropped, though less than without the warning. It is this book's reasoning that trying to answer shows you what a warning doesn't; nobody tested people explaining a system they built.

The first question you cannot answer is the next thing to find out. At half past one Mateo starts a load test: a busy morning at all three sites, replayed against a test copy of the scheduler. It finishes a little after two. He rewrites the capacity line: three sites run with room to spare, by the test he has just run. A fourth site would add a second time zone, and nobody has tested that.

At eleven o'clock Mateo felt one large worry about "the integration"; on paper it has become a handful of separate claims, and someone can check each one. Testing your own claim this way is where the book's critical-thinking strand starts; *How sure are you? Say that* turns the same test on claims that reach you, a machine's among them.

Mateo sends the note at twenty past two. By the time he wakes, Claire has replied: "My security team will review the scopes in the first week of August. Who from Tolva will be in the room?" He answers with one word: "Me."

## Practice: from pile to point

**Situation.** On Friday your client's operations manager asked what she should do about the morning report, which is due at seven and keeps arriving late. You spent the weekend in the logs and found the cause. Your message has to reach her before her nine o'clock meeting.

**First draft (what a careful data engineer writes).** "We found that most of the nightly runtime goes to retries on the address-lookup service, which times out under load, so the job sometimes finishes late."

**What's wrong.** It answers the question the writer found interesting: what did we find? She asked what to do. There is no person in the sentence, nothing she could refuse and no next step.

**Better.** "You should move the morning report to eight this week, because on four of the last ten nights the data arrived after seven, and never after half past seven."

**Why it works.** It speaks to her, asks for one thing she can refuse, and gives the one reason that covers eight o'clock. Under it go the limit (ten nights is a short record), then the cause and the fix, as answers to the questions she will ask.

**Your turn (5 minutes).** Pick a message you have to send this week. Before you draft a line of it, write its point sentence in the slots. Then apply both tests. **Done means** a colleague who reads only that sentence can name the person, what is asked of them and the reason, and one way they might say no.

## Practice: ask it before they do

**If you do only one, do this one.**

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Write the point sentence for your next meeting or message, in the slots: `[this listener] should [believe or do this], because [the one reason]`.

1. Under it, answer the next three questions of the second-question test in order, one line each: *What does it rest on? Where does it stop? What do you want from me?* Then sign it, because the answer to *Who answers for it?* is you.
2. Find the first line you could not fill. That is the question your listener will ask.
3. Fill it before you send, or write down what you will do to find out, and by when.

**Done means** a colleague who reads your page finds an answer under every question, or, where you had none, what you will do to get it and by which day.

## Check it the night before

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| A topic: "The September date has several external dependencies." | A claim someone could refuse |
| Three reasons | The one that carries the weight |
| Nobody named | One listener, by name |
| The cause, when they asked what to do | Their answer first, then the cause |
| A short sentence with no limit | The limit, written under it |

## Words to use

- To ask for time: "I know the facts. I haven't decided what they mean yet. You'll have it in one sentence by four."
- To open with the point: "In one sentence: [the sentence]. The rest is why."
- To find the point with a colleague: "Can I talk this through with you for five minutes? Each time I stop, ask me what I want her to do."
- To state your own limit: "Here is where this stops: we have tested three sites, and nobody has tested four."
- To name a gap before someone else does: "I can't tell you yet what this rests on. I'll know by Thursday, and I'll tell you either way."
- To invite the refusal: "Tell me if you would say no to this, and why."

### Phrases you will hear

- "What's the bottom line?" means: what is your point, in one sentence?
- "Where are you landing on this?" means: what have you decided?
- "Cut to the chase" means: skip the background and give me the point.

## When it backfires

**Too early.** A point sentence written before you have looked at anything can harden into a conclusion you find yourself defending. Write it once you have looked, treat it as a claim under test, and rewrite it when the test breaks it.

**When they hold the power.** "Claire should book the review" works on your own page and with a client who asked for your view. To someone senior who has not asked, a bare "should" can sound like an order. Keep the decision in the sentence and change the verb. "I'd ask you to book the review for the first week of August, because it is the step most likely to move the date." The choice stays theirs, and the sentence says so.

**When the reader wants the reasons first.** Some readers, and some teams, hear a conclusion in the first line as presumptuous. If this reader does, keep the sentence and move it to the end; if you don't know, ask how they like to receive a recommendation.

**Overuse.** A colleague asks in chat whether the test copy resets on Sunday and gets back "You should plan your tests around the reset, because it clears the data." All they needed was "Yes." Keep the sentence for messages that ask someone to decide or act.

**In a second language.** If you think more easily in your first language, find the sentence there, then put it into English. The deciding is the hard part, and one decided sentence is easier to carry into English than three pages.
