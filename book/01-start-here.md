# Start here

Before you read another page, take out your phone and open the voice recorder.

Answer one question aloud, for sixty seconds: **What are you working on, and why does it matter?**

Don't prepare: you want the version you give on an ordinary day. Press record, talk for one minute, stop, and keep the file. Don't play it yet. First write down, in a single sentence, the point you meant to make. Fold the paper and set it aside. Soon one listener will tell you how much of that sentence reached them.

Allow fifteen minutes. At the end you record again and hear the difference.

*Safe practice: record only yourself, or others only with their consent and within your employer's rules.*

No way to record? Write for three minutes without stopping instead; every check below works on paper too.

## Check the recording

Play it twice, pen in hand, and pause when you need to. The fifth check needs a listener.

1. **Sentences before the point.** Count the sentences before the first one that says why your work matters. If no sentence says it, write "never".
2. **Hedges with no basis.** Every *probably*, *I think*, *should* or *hopefully* with nothing after it to say why.
3. **One example.** One concrete case that shows why it matters: a person, a number, a thing that happened. Background, such as when you started or where the client is, does not count. Yes or no.
4. **The ending.** An ask or a next step at the end? Yes or no.
5. **Said back.** This one matters most. Play the recording once to a colleague or a friend, who writes your point in one sentence. Then unfold your paper and compare.

Counting fillers (um, like, basically) is optional. Pauses, restarts and searching for a word are thinking and never count; if you stutter, work in a second language or pause to think, skip it.

Compare your results only with your own next recording.

No listener? An assistant given the transcript can stand in.

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

Here is a first take from Lucía, co-founder and chief executive of Tolva, a fourteen-person company in Montevideo. Its software decides which truck goes to which warehouse door, and when. She recorded this in April.

"So, Tolva started three years ago as a tool for booking truck slots. Then we saw the real problem was inside the warehouse, at the doors, so we rebuilt most of it. This year the big thing is Veldhaven, a food wholesaler in Rotterdam with three distribution centers. We signed in March, and the integration with their warehouse system is a lot of work. I think it's going well. We should be live at the first site in September, hopefully. And it matters because if it works there, it probably opens the door to bigger customers."

Her checks: six sentences before the point, four hedges with no basis, no example and no ask. Before playing it, she had written: "Veldhaven is our first proof at full size, so until September it gets every engineer we have." Her listener, a friend outside software, wrote: "Tolva is three years old and has a big new client in Rotterdam."

The take arrived smaller than what was in her head, and she couldn't hear that until someone said it back.

> **Research says:** In laboratory experiments, speakers said ambiguous sentences with one meaning in mind and believed they had got it across more often than they had. People who overheard, knowing the intended meaning, showed no such bias, so this book's reasoning is to trust a listener's version over your own sense of being clear. The sentences were ambiguous on purpose, so this shows the direction of the mistake, not its size in your next meeting.

## One move: the point, then four questions

**Write the point sentence.** One sentence, on paper, before you say anything:

`[this listener] should [believe or do this], because [the one reason]`

Test it twice. Could the listener say no to it? If not, you have a topic, such as "an update on the rollout". Would they know what to do next? If not, the sentence is still about you.

Lucía pictured someone smart who does not work with her. Her first try, "Veldhaven is our most important project", was a topic: nobody outside Tolva could accept or refuse it. Her second could be refused, so it was a point. "You should judge Tolva by the Veldhaven rollout, because it is our first proof that the scheduler works at full size."

**Then answer four questions, in this order.**

1. **What is the point?** Your point sentence, said plainly.
2. **What does it rest on?** One fact the listener could look up or test: a number, a record, a thing that happened.
3. **Where does it stop?** What you have not checked yet, or what would change your mind.
4. **What do you want from me?** Your ask or your next step, with a date if you have one.

That comes to about fifty words, twenty seconds aloud, a target this book sets. These are the first four questions of the book's central tool, **the second-question test**, named for the question that follows your first answer; the fifth comes later.

Lucía's second take:

"I'm putting Tolva's scheduler into Veldhaven's three warehouses: it's our first proof at full size. At one pilot dock, the average truck waited about twenty minutes less, by Veldhaven's own gate log. We haven't yet shown it works with their warehouse system at three sites. If you know someone who has done that integration, I'd like an introduction."

Her checks: no sentences before the point, no hedge without a basis, one example and an ask, in about twenty-five seconds. Her listener wrote: "Tolva has to prove at Veldhaven that its scheduler works at full size, and Lucía wants an introduction to someone who has done a big warehouse integration."

"About twenty minutes" stays, because the gate log stands behind it. Cut only the hedges with nothing behind them.

### Practice: your second sixty seconds

**Your turn (5 minutes).** Choose one listener: someone smart who does not work with you. Write your point sentence for them and test it. Under it, write the four questions with a few words of your own beside each. Record again, and if you finish in twenty-five seconds, stop. Run the first four checks, then play the take once to the same listener.

*Safe practice: record only yourself, or others only with their consent and within your employer's rules.*

**Done means** your listener's one sentence names the point in your point sentence and the ask you wrote beside *What do you want from me?*

**If you do only one, do this one.** Keep this take with your first; every later recording is measured against the pair.

## The second question

Play your two recordings one after the other. The second is shorter and says more. The only thing that changed between them is that you decided what you meant. That difference is the argument of this book.

Smooth text is now free. An assistant will draft a clean update, a tidy spec or a polite reply in seconds. A polished memo used to suggest that the writer understood the work, because polish took effort. Now that a machine can supply the polish, it suggests much less, so the test moves to the moment nobody can draft for you.

In May, Lucía sent Veldhaven a status summary that an assistant had helped her write. On the call the next morning, Claire, Veldhaven's vice president of operations, asked: "You say the integration is on track. What does the September date depend on?"

Lucía talked for two minutes about the architecture, the team and the history. She knew what Tolva had to prove. She had never decided what the date itself rested on: which review, which parts, which people.

Claire was not being difficult. A board approved this project on her word, and her peak season starts in December, so testing a claim before she acts on it is her job. A document can answer the first question; the second is answered by a person, live.

This book is named for that moment. Anyone, or any assistant, can prepare the first answer; the second question shows whether you understand what you said. The book trains you to ask it of your own claims, of a colleague's and of a machine's, before someone asks it of you.

> **Research says:** In laboratory studies, people rated how well they understood a device such as a zipper, then wrote out step by step how it works and rated themselves lower. Outside judges who read the explanations rated them lower still, and in other studies the drop was much smaller for facts and film plots and absent for procedures. None of the studies used machine-written text, so applying this to a fluent draft in your hands is this book's reasoning, not a finding.

You may object: the assistant will write my update, my spec and my reply, so why train this? Three reasons.

It cannot decide what you mean. It will hand you ten well-formed versions of a point, and choosing the one that is true, that you will stand behind and that this listener needs is the work. That work is thinking.

When you are challenged live, the other person is testing your understanding, whatever is open on your screen.

And you answer for what goes out under your name. If the summary says "on track" and the date slips, nobody asks the software to explain. That is the fifth question of the test: **Who answers for it?** For anything you send, the answer is you, however the draft was made.

The work this book bets on can be trained: choosing what is worth doing, framing the problem, judging what comes back, and getting all of it into another mind intact. It keeps its value for as long as people decide, sign and answer for the result, and it is one skill with two kinds of listener: a colleague, who asks what you meant, and an agent, which usually guesses.

Articulation is thinking made usable by other people. You just trained it for fifteen minutes.

> **Line to keep:** The draft can come from anywhere; the point has to come from you.

## When it backfires

Point first is for moments when someone has to act on what you say. When a person needs to be heard first, after bad news or in front of an angry client, start with a question and keep your point for later.

With a senior person, a conclusion they did not ask for can sound like a verdict. Ask first: "May I give you the short version of where we stand?"

A listener who knows your work fills your gaps, so for the said-back check, choose someone outside it. In a second language, the four questions may help more, because short sentences in a fixed order are easier to plan and to follow.

## Where this book is going

The book trains three strands together. **Language** is the exact word, in your first language or your second. **Articulation** is the point in a shape a busy person can follow. **Critical thinking** is testing a claim before you act on it, and it runs through six chapters: *Know what you think*, *How sure are you? Say that*, *Thinking on your feet*, *Arguing well*, *Pressure and how to resist it* and *Seeing the system*.

Six habits of thinking, this book bets, gain value as machines draft more of the work, and you will grow into at least one:

- **The framer** decides what the question is before anyone answers it.
- **The calibrator** says how sure we are, and tests a claim before the team acts on it.
- **The system reader** asks what a change sets off after its first effect, and who feels it next.
- **The strategist** chooses among paths when nobody knows the answer, and says what would change the choice.
- **The problem-solver** takes a mess with people in it to a decision that lasts.
- **The translator** carries meaning across a boundary intact: between disciplines, between a client and a team, between people and machines.

Each is a habit, not a personality, and each person in this book grows toward one. Lucía, who in May wrote "on track" before she knew what the date rested on, is growing toward the strategist.

She, Claire and nine others, all invented, take you through nine months: a slipping release, a client who nearly leaves, a team that hands work to agents.

You will record again at the end of each part, starting with Part I, *Clear head*, and once more at the end of the book. If writing the point sentence was the hard part, good: the next chapter, *Know what you think*, starts there.
