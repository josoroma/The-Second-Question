# Thinking on your feet

Bianca, Tolva's product manager, was ten minutes into a roadmap call when site two's manager stopped her.

14:12, site two's manager: "Sorry, before the next slide. The one thing I asked you for, texts to my drivers, is after the peak. Then our systems team tells me my printers need new firmware, and my labels move three weeks. So why do I hear about my own site from them, and never from you?"

14:12, Bianca: "The new label interface needs newer firmware than the printers at your site have. Site one's printers already had it, so our test there passed. Your systems team can update a site in about half a day, and Diego is agreeing the dates with them this week."

14:13, site two's manager: "I know about the firmware. That's not what I asked."

The roadmap showed what Tolva would build next, for which warehouse, and when. Tolva's software sends arriving trucks to warehouse doors. On the video call were the managers of Veldhaven's three distribution centers and Diego, the engineer Tolva placed with Veldhaven, its largest client.

Earlier in June, Claire, who runs operations at Veldhaven, had chosen between the two changes her managers asked for most, because Tolva could finish only one before September. She chose the override, which lets supervisors move a truck to another door from the dock screen. The texts would wait until after the December peak.

## 14:13: the right facts, the wrong question

Bianca told him nothing new: his own team had already explained the firmware. She heard "why" as a question of fact, what had made the date move, and gave him the cause. He was asking whether Tolva listens to site two and keeps it informed. His request would wait until after the peak, and news of his printers had come from someone else. That is a challenge, and no cause settles it.

Bianca got the step before the answer wrong: deciding what she had been asked.

Before you answer a hard question, decide what kind it is, because each kind is settled by something different. Then answer the kind you were asked, even when it arrives hostile or in the middle of your sentence. Take about two seconds to decide, a target this book sets. Those two seconds turn the hardest question in the room into one you know how to answer.

> **Line to keep:** Facts settle only one kind of question.

## Sort first

The tool for those two seconds is **sort first**. Before answering a hard question or joining a disagreement, say to yourself what kind it is, so you know what would settle it. A question is a **fact**, a **judgment**, a **commitment** or a **challenge**.

| Kind | At 14:12 it might have sounded like | What settles it |
|---|---|---|
| Fact | "Which firmware do our printers need?" | The fact and how you know it; if you don't have it, when you will |
| Judgment | "Was the override the right choice?" | Your view, what you weighed, and whose decision it is |
| Commitment | "Will site two get the texts before the peak?" | Yes or no, with its condition, from someone who can keep it |
| Challenge | "Why do I never hear it from you?" | The true part, said first; then sort what is left |

To catch a wrong sort, imagine giving the answer you have in mind: would it end the question? A "why" can ask for a cause, for a reason, or for you to defend yourself. In a design review, "Why a queue?" can mean any of the three. If you hear "That's not what I asked," sort again.

A challenge asks whether your word holds, or whether you listened, and the person asking may already have the facts, so more facts can sound like a defense. Say first whatever part of it is true, with no "but" after it. If none of it is true ("Why did you hide the delay?" when nobody did), say once what did happen, and how you know. Then sort what is left, because a challenge can carry another kind of question inside it. His carried a commitment: will you tell me yourself next time?

A disagreement is about **what is true** (agree the test before anyone argues the answer) or **what to do** (agree the goal). Or it is about **who gets something limited** (agree a fair rule and name who decides). The override against the texts was the third kind: one change before September, and Claire to decide which. *Arguing well* takes disagreements in full.

## Say that you are thinking

In a room, people can watch you think; on a call, a silent pause can look like a frozen screen. Either way, say what the pause is for, "Let me think," and sort.

One question gets no pause: a question about what you did or knew. "Did you test site one's printers?" needs no thinking, so a pause before it earns no credit for effort. A slow "yes" can sound less sincere than a quick one, so answer first.

> **Research says:** Across fourteen experiments, people judged slower answers as less sincere than faster ones, in settings from everyday conversation to police questioning. The effect was smaller when the delay could be put down to thinking hard, or when the answer was socially undesirable. The studies measured how answers were judged, not whether slow speakers lie, and saying "let me think" aloud is this book's advice, built on the effort result.

## 14:14: the same question, sorted again

Back on the call, Bianca said, "Let me think," and sorted again. Then:

"That's fair. You should have heard it from us, and before the date moved. From today, if a date for site two moves, I'll call you myself, the same day. And by tomorrow, every date on this slide will say whether we checked it or are still assuming it."

He was quiet for a moment. "Which of mine are you assuming?"

"Your label date. The old one assumed your printers matched site one's, and nobody checked. The new one assumes your systems team updates them by mid-July. They told us they can; nobody has done it yet. The others will be marked tomorrow."

She made it easier for him to hear and changed none of the content: nothing on the roadmap moved. His follow-up was a fact question: he had stopped asking whether Tolva would tell him anything and started testing what it told him. Her answer was a sized claim, with its basis (told) and its limit.

## 14:17: a judgment, then an interruption

Site three's manager unmuted. "Honestly, was the override the right choice? The texts would help all three of us."

It was a judgment, and Bianca gave her view first. "Yes, for September. Site one goes live first, and when a door breaks, a supervisor has to be able to move a truck at once. The texts save each driver a walk to the window. The override keeps one broken door from blocking a whole morning. Claire made the choice, and I—"

"So my drivers walk to the window all through the peak." Site two's manager again.

Treat an interruption as a question that won't wait its turn, and sort it like any other. This one was a commitment question in the form of a complaint: can you do anything for site two before December? If it carries what matters most to the person asking, take it now, after one finished sentence. If it jumps to something you will cover anyway, say when, and keep that promise.

Bianca took it. "One sentence to finish site three's answer, then yours. Claire made the choice, and I'd make the same one." Then, to site two: "I can't promise texts before the peak. I can find out whether texts at your gate alone would fit before November, and you'll have a yes or no from me by the end of July."

## 14:26: a missing number

Site three's manager asked how long drivers wait at her gate on a normal morning. Bianca didn't know. Diego answered: "That's in the gate log. I'll pull last month's mornings for all three sites and send them tonight." A fact nobody has on hand is settled by a source and a time.

The session ended at 14:31 with site two's manager still unconvinced. Only July would settle his challenge: whether the next news about site two came from Bianca. At 14:12 Bianca had answered the question she was ready for; after that, she decided before each answer what she had been asked. That is the habit of the framer, the thinker type she is growing toward.

## Practice: sort, then answer

**Situation.** A video call with an investor you have met twice. You have just shown your pricing, and she says: "Has anyone actually signed at this price, or is that a hope?"

**First draft (what a careful founder says).**
"We priced it from the hours it saves. A team of ten saves about a day a week, and we charge less than a quarter of what that day costs them."

**What's wrong.** None of it answers her. She asked whether anyone has signed at this price; the draft argues that the price is fair and leaves her to work out that the answer is no. Her challenge carries a fact about what you did, and that answer comes first, with no pause.

**Better.**
"No, not at this price yet. Our two customers pay about two-thirds of it, on contracts we signed last year. The first quote at this price goes out on Monday, and I'll send you their answer, whatever it is."

**Why it works.** It answers the fact her challenge carried, then gives it a size. The last sentence turns her doubt into a test she can watch, with a date, and promises the result either way.

**Your turn (5 minutes).** Write down the question you would least like to hear about your work this week. Sort it, and if it is a challenge, sort what is left and write that question underneath. Then write a first sentence whose first words answer it. **Done means** a colleague who reads only that sentence can tell which kind of question you heard and what your answer is.

**Timed drill (60 seconds).** **If you do only one, do this one.** Hand a colleague your question. They ask it, then cut in once, without warning, with "That's not what I asked" or a question of their own. Sort before you begin, and say "Let me think" only if you need the pause. If the question is about what you did or knew, answer at once. When they cut in, sort again, then answer now or say when you will. Constraint: no reason before the answer, either time. **Done means** your colleague can name the kind you took each question to be and repeat both answers in a sentence each.

**Written form.** Write the question, its kind and your first sentence. Your colleague writes an interruption under it, and you reply in one written sentence.

**Mistakes and fixes.**

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| A judgment answered with all you weighed and no view | Your view, then what you weighed, then who decides |
| A plain fact question heard as an attack | The fact, in the voice you would use for any other |
| A promise someone else must keep | Name who decides, and promise an answer by a date |
| A "but" after the true part | A full stop, then what you will do |

**Spar with an AI.** Rehearse against someone whose request was left out.

```text
Play a [client or user] whose request was left out of my next release. Here is the plan I am presenting: [three lines]. Ask me four questions, one at a time, in any order: a fact, a judgment, a commitment and a challenge, without saying which is which. Interrupt one of my answers once. At the end, list each question, the kind you meant, the kind my answer treated it as, and where I could have answered sooner.
```

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## Words to use

- **To sort before you answer.** "Let me think."
- **When you can't tell the kind.** "Do you need a date from me, or the reason it moved?"
- **A challenge.** "That's fair: [the true part]. From [when], [commitment]." "That's not quite what happened. Here's what did, and how we know: [what]."
- **A judgment.** "My view is [view], because [the one thing I weighed]. The decision is [name]'s."
- **A commitment you can't make.** "I can't promise that. I can promise you an answer by [day]."
- **An interruption.** "One sentence to finish this, then yours."

### Phrases you will hear

- "On the spot": asked a hard question in public, with no time to prepare.
- "With all due respect": usually comes just before a disagreement.
- "That's a loaded question": the question assumes something you may not accept.

## When it backfires

"Do you need a date from me, or the reason it moved?" after a plain question sounds like stalling. Sort silently whenever the kind is clear, and ask only when you can't tell.

Some challenges outlast the meeting. If the record behind one is bad, nothing you say in the room will settle it; only a kept commitment will, so make the commitment small enough to keep.

When they hold the power, never name the kind aloud: "That sounds like a challenge," said to a client's chief executive, turns a question into a contest. If they interrupt, they have the floor; give them the answer first and put the rest in writing afterward. When you hold the power, your fact questions can land as challenges, so say which you mean: "I'm not testing you. I really don't know."

A hard question may show respect in one team and sound close to an accusation in another. Before you sort one as hostile, ask a colleague who knows the room how sharp questions are meant there.

In a second language, sorting while you translate is a lot for two seconds, so take longer and say why: "Let me make sure I understood the question." The asker's English can mislead you too. When someone is upset in a second language, the softening words may be the first to go. Check whether the edge is in the question or only in the English.
