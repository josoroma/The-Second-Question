# Questions and common ground

The man in front of Lucía in the coffee queue was marking the fair's floor plan with a pen, and he had crossed out more stands than he had circled. It was July, the second morning of a logistics trade fair in Rotterdam. Lucía had stayed on after visiting Veldhaven, a food wholesaler there and Tolva's largest client, to look for the next one. She is co-founder and chief executive of Tolva, fourteen people in Montevideo.

His badge said Hugo, operations director, Sarnik: a distributor of chilled and frozen food with four warehouses outside Lyon.

Lucía nodded at the floor plan. "Anything worth the walk so far?"

"Not yet." He read her badge. "Tolva. What do you sell?"

"Software that decides which truck goes to which door. At our pilot dock last year, the average truck waited about twenty minutes less, by the client's own gate log."

"Everybody in this hall saves me twenty minutes." He tapped the crossed-out names. "What does it cost?"

There was an easy answer, a figure low enough to keep him talking, and Lucía had nothing to base a figure on. "It depends on your doors and your trucks, and I'd be guessing at both. What does your busiest warehouse look like at six in the morning?"

"Six is the middle of it. From five to seven, more trucks than doors."

"What happens to the trucks that don't get one?"

"They wait in the yard with the cooling running. The driver calls his dispatcher, the dispatcher calls my shift lead, and on a bad morning my phone rings before I've had coffee." He looked down the queue. "Like this one."

"We got that problem wrong at first," Lucía said. "Our first version booked every truck a slot at the gate. The trucks came on time and waited anyway, because nobody had planned the doors. We rebuilt most of it."

Hugo folded the plan. "My engineers want to build a scheduler themselves, with a coding assistant. Since my budget was cut in June, I listen to them more."

Lucía let that sit. "Once a truck is through your gate, how long does it wait for a door?"

He started to answer and stopped. "I know when they arrive. From this month a consultant forecasts when they will arrive." Lucía had a good idea which consultant, and where else that forecast ran. Neither was hers to say. "How long they wait inside," Hugo said, "nobody writes down."

It was the first question at the fair he could not answer, and he did not look at the floor plan again.

## A claim he had heard all morning

Lucía's best fact did nothing. Twenty minutes was true, measured and hers, and to Hugo it was one more vendor's number in a hall full of them. He could not check it in a queue, so he asked for the one figure every stand would have to give him: the price. She answered that as far as she honestly could, then asked, because what the price depended on was in his head.

Her questions did what the number could not. Each one handed him the subject he knew best, and each answer told her which of her facts, if any, mattered to him. They reached a problem they both had: trucks that come on time and still wait. Then came a question neither could answer.

Small talk becomes real talk when each question follows the other person's last answer further into their work, and you meet them there with something true of your own. Do that, leave with a next step, and a stranger in a queue can become someone who takes your call.

Lucía asked Hugo a second question about his own yard, and it was Hugo who went home to find out.

> **Line to keep:** Common ground is a question you both want answered.

## Fill in the sketch out loud

The listener sketch from *Who is listening* is three lines about one person: what they know, what they have to decide, what they stand to lose. With a stranger all three are blank, and the conversation is how you fill them. By the time the coffee came, Lucía could have written Hugo's:

- **Knows** when his trucks arrive, and from this month when they will.
- **Has to decide** what a smaller budget pays for, and whether his engineers build it instead.
- **Stands to lose** his mornings: the call that reaches him when the yard fills.

She asked for none of it directly. Open with one line about where you both are. Then ask about their work in the words they would use with a colleague: what they are working on, what they must choose this year. For the third line, ask what happens when the thing goes wrong, and who gets the call.

The follow-up does most of the work. Take the most concrete word in their answer, a time, a number, a place or a person, and ask what happens there. Hugo said "more trucks than doors", so Lucía asked about the trucks without one. A follow-up is built from their words, so it can only come from someone who heard them.

You may hear "never ask why": it can sound like an audit. Keep the rule narrower. A why about the work opens a story: "Why five to seven?" A why about a choice someone made asks a stranger to defend it, so give your reason for asking first: "I ask because we chose wrong the first time. What made you go with a forecast?"

None of this is a discovery call yet. It is the conversation that earns one.

## Answer with something of your own

A question opens the subject, and something of your own shows what kind of answer is safe to give. Hugo's ringing phone was a small admission. Lucía answered with a bigger one, a first version built for the wrong problem. He answered that with something he had held back: his own engineers want to build a scheduler themselves.

Among peers, say something of your own before you ask for theirs. With a client, an investor or a boss, show that you know the work before you say what went wrong, as Lucía's pilot number did. Upward, admit the limits of what you know, never a list of your weaknesses.

## Prepared questions or your own words?

One piece of advice says to prepare your openers, because nerves, a second language or an executive with four minutes leave no time to invent one. The other says never to use lines, because people can hear a canned question. "What keeps you up at night?" tells a buyer he is the tenth person to hear it today.

Prepare what the question does, and let the moment choose its words. Lucía had decided before the fair to ask each operations person about their busiest hour, and for Hugo that meant six in the morning. Then test any move by whether you could name it to the person while you use it. Said aloud, "I ask everyone about their busiest hour, because that's the hour our software is for" would have cost her nothing. A shared hobby you invented fails that test at once, and so does any move that works only if they never notice it.

## Practice: a short answer, then one question back

**Situation.** You are the engineer your company placed with a client. In the elevator after a review, the client's chief operating officer, whom you have never spoken to, asks: "So, how are you finding it here?"

**First draft (an honest status report).** "Good, thanks. We finished connecting your order system on Monday, the test data came in on Tuesday, and we should start on returns next week if nothing else comes up."

**What's wrong.** She asked how you are finding it, and got a status report. It ends without a question, so the ride ends in silence, and she knows you only as the integration.

**Better.** "I like it. Your support team shows me exactly where the old system slows them down, and that makes my job easier. Can I ask you one thing? When the new system is live, what's the first number you'll look at?"

**Why it works.** Her question gets a true answer in plain words, with one thing about her people she can be glad to hear. Then one question about what she will judge the work by, which gives her the next sentence. Whatever she answers goes into her listener sketch.

**Your turn (5 minutes).** Pick someone senior you will meet this week without an agenda. Write a one-sentence answer to "How's it going?" that someone outside your field could repeat, then one question about what they will decide or watch this year, in their words. Say both aloud once. **Done means** a colleague who hears them can repeat your answer and say what the person would have to talk about to answer your question.

## Leave while it is still good

The coffee came while Hugo was still thinking about his yard. The conversation was going well, which was Lucía's reason to end it.

"Let me check I have it," she said. "More trucks than doors before seven, a forecast for when they arrive, and nobody who knows how long they wait. I'll send you one page on how we measure it, by Friday. What I don't know is whether your gate log can show it."

"Send it. And put a price in it."

"I'll put in what the price depends on. The number needs your doors."

"Then call me in August. By then I'll know how long they wait."

That ending is the read-back from *Listening people can feel*, used as a goodbye. Its last sentence held the question they now shared. Then she left, before either of them had to look for a way out.

Don't wait for the right moment to stop.

> **Research says:** In two studies from one paper, people reported afterward when they had wanted a conversation to end, some after talking with someone close to them, some with a stranger. An ending that suited both people was very rare, one that suited either was uncommon, and neither could tell well when the other had wanted to stop. None of this was tested on clients or buyers, so carrying it into work is the book's reasoning: you can't feel the right moment, so choose one.

Hugo had queued with one question for every stand in the hall. He left with one about his own yard. That is a first step toward the framer, who decides what the question is before anyone answers it.

## Practice: from their title to their work

**With a person.** **If you do only one, do this one.** Use your next conversation with someone you don't know well: a new client's first call, a dinner, a fair.

1. Before it, write one question about their work at its busiest or hardest hour, in words they would use.
2. After their first answer, take its most concrete word and ask what happens there.
3. When you reach a problem you also have, say one true thing of your own about it, one step deeper than they went.
4. Look for a question you both want answered, then end it yourself: the read-back from *Listening people can feel*, and a day.

Watch their second answer. If it has a time, a place or a person the first one lacked, they have moved from their job title to their work.

**Done means** that evening your page holds their three lines, each built on something they told you, and a next step with a day they agreed to.

## Words to use

- **To open, after one line about where you are.** "What are you hoping to find here?" "What does your busiest [hour or day] look like?"
- **The follow-up.** "[Their word]. What happens then?" "Who gets the call when that happens?"
- **To go first.** "We had the same problem. [One true thing that happened.]"
- **A price you can't know yet.** "It depends on [what], and I'd be guessing at yours. What does [that] look like?"
- **To leave with a next step.** "I'd rather talk about this properly than in a queue. Can I send you [one thing] by [day]?"
- **To leave without one.** "I'll let you get to the next stand. It was good to hear about [one thing they said]."

### Phrases you will hear

- "Let's touch base." Let's talk again; ask when.
- "I'll let you go." They would like to end the conversation, politely.
- "We should grab a coffee sometime." A friendly close that is often not a plan; ask for a day if you want one.

## When it backfires

**When the price is all they came for.** Some people come to a fair to compare prices, and questions about their mornings can feel like a sales move. Answer what they asked as far as you honestly can, offer one question, and if they don't take it, let them go with your card.

**When the other person sets the depth.** With a client's executive or a buyer, let them. Ask about what they decide and watch, not about them, and keep your admission to what you don't yet know. When the power is yours, with a candidate or a new engineer, your questions can sound like an assessment, so go first.

**When the room opens differently.** How soon talk turns to work, and how directly someone's problems can be asked about, differs from one room to the next. Watch how respected people there open a conversation, and go deeper only after they do.

**When you only ask.** Question after question with nothing of your own is an interrogation, and a first conversation steered into discovery is a sales call nobody agreed to. When you notice you have only asked, tell them something.

**When small talk costs you a lot.** Skip it. One line about where you are is enough before the question about their work.

**In a second language.** Small talk can be harder than a technical answer, because its words don't come from your work. A follow-up is easier: it is built from words the other person has just used. Prepare your first question and one true thing about your own work, and let the rest come from theirs.
