# Still human

The seventh line had no reason on it.

It showed a truck of peas, moved to another door at ten past four and confirmed by the night supervisor. He had never decided to move it, and the driver waited at the wrong door. Your name may end up on a line like that.

Early on Monday 23 November, the morning shift lead at Veldhaven's cold store in Rotterdam had pulled off one glove to read the night's page. Site three, the last of the food wholesaler's three warehouses, had been running since midnight on Tolva's scheduler, which tells each truck which door to use. When a door shut or broke, a supervisor still moved the truck by hand on the dock screen. Each move was counted and printed, with two columns left for a pen: *Who* and *Why*.

The night supervisor had written six reasons: *ice on the seal*, *cleaning*, *door shut after frozen*. Line seven had only his initials. On the paper plan, which hung beside the screen and would stay there until January, the peas were still at door two.

"Why did you move the peas?" she asked him.

"I didn't."

"The log has the move, and it has his login," said Mateo, who runs technology at Tolva, from the phone on the counter. In Montevideo it was still night, and five people were up. Beside the phone, the night's report, drafted by an assistant from the log, said: "Seven moves, all confirmed by a supervisor. No exceptions."

The shift lead read it and put it down. "What were you looking at when you pressed?" she asked.

"The driver," the night supervisor said. "He was shouting. I thought it was still the fish truck. I pressed to move that one."

"That's what I saw," said Diego, the engineer Tolva had placed with Veldhaven, who had been on the dock with a second engineer since Sunday evening. "I was beside you. Door six had just shut for its twelve minutes, so you moved the fish to nine. You pressed, and nothing changed, so you pressed again, harder. By then the screen had taken the fish and put up the peas, with the button in the same place." He held up a freezer glove. "In these you can't feel whether it worked. Your second press was for the fish too. It moved the peas."

Then he gave them the part that was his. "Last Tuesday I pressed that button in these gloves, and I wrote down that it works. I pressed it once, with nobody waiting. I never tried a second press."

The peas had stood for eighteen minutes at a door nobody was coming to, with the cooling running. Nothing had thawed.

"So you pressed right," the shift lead told the night supervisor.

"Twice," he said.

Montevideo offered a bigger button, then a beep. "Before the button," said Bianca, Tolva's product manager. "You move one truck, in gloves, with a driver shouting. How do you know it moved?" Nobody could say, so they stopped talking about the button.

An engineer whose contract ended that day had the presses open. "Five of his six moves have a second press within three seconds," she said. "One hit a truck. Four came when the list was empty. We could ignore any press that quick."

"Then a door breaks, and he has three trucks to move," said Mateo. "The screen ignores him, and in gloves that feels the same as not being heard. He presses harder. We'd be teaching the double press."

"So it stays on the truck he moved," she said, "with the new door filling the screen, until he touches *next*."

A message arrived from Naomi, the data consultant in Seattle: "Five of six is counted, for one night. The gloves were seen, once. Please don't let them become one sentence." Under it was one from Otávio, who heads information security at Veldhaven: "If it's signed today, we read it tomorrow. Put what you haven't tried at the top."

"Two decisions," said Tyler, who leads delivery at Tolva. "The screen is ours. Tonight isn't." He asked the night supervisor first. "What would you do?"

"Press once. Read the door out loud before I touch it again."

"Out loud to whom?" asked the shift lead.

"To me," said Diego. "I'm at this screen from ten tonight, and tomorrow night as well."

An agent could draft the change in minutes. It stopped and asked, as Tolva's standards tell it to, whose name went on the first line.

"I'll write the tests," said the engineer on contract. "Don't use my name. It can't be live before Wednesday, and on Wednesday I don't work here. Put somebody he can phone."

"Mine," said Mateo. "Once you've shown me what's wrong with it."

That left two nights. Lucía, Tolva's chief executive, wanted to spend them on the screen. "If one more truck moves that nobody chose, I'd ask for paper until the change is in. Claire, that's yours to say."

"One more, and it's paper," said Claire, who runs operations at Veldhaven and was on the same call. "He gives the word, and nobody asks him why until morning."

The shift lead turned the page around and held the pen out to Diego. "You saw it," she said. "You write it."

The pen wouldn't start in the cold. He took off his glove, warmed the pen in his fist and wrote on the seventh line: *Second press for the fish. Nobody chose this.* Beside the night supervisor's initials he put his own.

## What the questions were for

We are still humans. This book closes on that conviction, and I mean it as a description of work.

A better report is easy to imagine. Say the next log keeps every press, and a camera over the counter keeps the glove. The shift lead would still want to know what Diego hadn't tried, and who would be listening at ten that night. Claire would still leave the word *paper* to somebody standing in the cold. The night supervisor would still look to see whose initials went beside his.

They weren't asking for more text. Each of them was about to act, with somebody else's frozen food, on an answer there was no time to check. So each wanted it from somebody who had given their word. That is what they were asking a person for: an answer at its real size, and the same person there for the next question.

Here is the book's bet, marked as one: that is the human part of the work, because people will keep asking people for it. They'll ask whoever decided what was meant, and can say what it rests on and where it stops. They'll want the same person there when it turns out wrong. If they stop asking who, the bet is lost, and you can tear out the next page.

> **Line to keep:** Your name tells people where to bring their questions.

The point you decide, the question you take next and the name you put under both make one job. Here it is, in the form jobs usually come in.

## The job

**Title.** The person to ask. Keep the title you have; this one goes under it.

**What you'll answer for.** Whatever goes out under your name, however it was made. A founder writes in the price, and the phone at two in the morning. An engineer writes in the change; a data scientist, the number; a product manager, the question and the no. Write in yours.

**What you'll do.**

- Choose the question before anything drafts an answer to it.
- Say your point in one sentence to the person who has to act on it.
- Read what comes back. Say what you checked, what you were told and what nobody has tried.
- Stand where it will be used, and watch one person use it.
- Give people the bad part yourself, the day you know it.
- Say "I don't know" with the day you will.
- When you're right, leave the other person room to have been right too.
- Before you go, say who to ask next.

**What you bring.** One craft in which you can tell good from plausible. Any first language, any accent.

**How you'll be judged.** Somebody who hears you once can say your point back, and what you need from them. Your claims hold at the size you gave them, and when one doesn't, people hear it from you. They bring you the next question.

**Hours.** The ones you say out loud, to somebody who will need them.

You've practiced every line of it. Sign it, or change the line that's wrong for your work and sign that. Then give a copy to somebody who acts on what you say.

## When it backfires

Without the hours to read something and the power to refuse it, your name sends the questions to the wrong place. Ask for both, or ask for another name, as the engineer on contract did. If somebody above you wants the signature anyway, write under it what you read and what you didn't.

## Practice: your last sixty seconds

**If you do only one, do this one.** The prompt is the one you began with: what are you working on, and why does it matter? Choose one listener. Write the point you mean in one sentence, write what you want from them under it, and turn the page over.

**Timed drill (60 seconds).** Record it once, without reading, and stop on what you need from that listener. Then play it and check it as you did the first time:

1. **Sentences before the point.** Count the sentences before the first one that says why your work matters. If no sentence says it, write "never".
2. **Hedges with no basis.** Each *probably*, *I think*, *should*, *hopefully* with nothing after it to say why.
3. **One example.** One concrete case that shows why it matters: a person, a number, a thing that happened. Background doesn't count. Yes or no.
4. **The ending.** Does it end on an ask or a next step? Yes or no.
5. **Said back.** Your listener, a colleague or a friend, hears it once and writes the point in one sentence. Compare the two: theirs should name your point and the ask you wrote.

**Written form.** Write for three minutes without stopping on the same prompt; the same checks apply. Pauses and restarts never count, in speech or on the page.

*Safe practice: record only yourself, or others only with their consent and within your employer's rules.*

Put the results beside your first two takes and every one since: this is the eighth, if you made them all. **Done means** a stranger who reads your sentence and your listener's finds the same point and the same ask in both.

Then ask your listener which question that minute left them with. Put the phone away before you answer.

Diego recorded his at nine that morning, in the drivers' room, with his coat on. "I'm making site three's screen show what really moved, because the whole dock works from it. At ten past four it read a second press as a second move, and a driver waited eighteen minutes at the wrong door. I saw that one; I didn't see the other four. If the screen surprises you today, call me before you fix it."

The shift lead listened once and wrote: *Call Diego today before we fix the screen, because it can make a move nobody chose.* She read it to him. Then she asked about the one thing he had left out.

"And if you're asleep?"

He put the phone in his pocket. "I said today," he told her. "Wake me."
