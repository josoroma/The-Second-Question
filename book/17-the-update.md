# The update

Renata: "Someone at Veldhaven says site one is the 28th now. True?"

Lucía: "Yes. I told their chief executive this morning. You'll have it in the August update."

Renata: "He also says the label work for sites two and three slipped three weeks. In June. Your July update says on track."

Renata: "Why am I hearing this from someone else?"

It was 20 August. Lucía is co-founder and chief executive of Tolva, fourteen people in Montevideo whose scheduler decides which truck goes to which warehouse door. Renata, in São Paulo, was its first investor and holds the third seat on its board. Veldhaven, a food wholesaler in Rotterdam, is Tolva's largest client. The first of its three warehouses had been due to go live on 14 September.

Lucía had a defense. The printers at sites two and three had been updated in July. Both sites had kept their November dates. So "on track" had been right about every date Renata knew. She typed half of it and read Renata's question again. None of it was an answer.

In June she had given Veldhaven's chief executive a whole date. It was the 14th if his security review passed in one round, the 28th if it needed a second, and she would know by 20 August. The review needed a second round, and that morning she had phoned him, as promised. Renata had heard the "if" once, on the May board call. In July Lucía gave her two words.

Renata had turned the two words into a date. At the start of August she told her partners that Veldhaven would be live on the 14th. All that morning, people in Rotterdam had known her sentence was wrong, and she had not.

## A date travels without you

A date leaves your hands when you say it. Claire, who runs operations at Veldhaven, builds shifts on it. Renata takes it to her partners. They speak on your word, and they can't see it move.

So an update has one job: keeping what those people say true. Put the answer first. Send every date with the thing it depends on. When it moves, tell whoever has repeated it that same day, whenever the next report is due.

> **Line to keep:** A date you move in silence is still being repeated.

People can stake their own word on yours, because your dates arrive with their conditions and your bad news arrives from you.

Two habits get you there.

**Write the names.** Before you announce a change, list everyone who has repeated the old version. Lucía's list that morning would have had four lines: Veldhaven's chief executive; Claire; Bianca, the product manager whose Friday report goes to both companies; and Renata. Whoever has to act on the change, or holds your promise, comes first, so the chief executive was the right first call. Next comes whoever will be asked about it soonest. Renata was the next call, and Lucía had planned to tell her in the August update.

**Let the report carry the month and the phone carry the change.** A monthly update is a good place for what you built, spent and learned. A moved date can't wait there for its turn.

## The test becomes the shape

Lucía left the defense unsent and phoned.

"You should have heard both from me. Site one goes live on 28 September if the second review round passes. I'll know by 4 September. If it fails, I don't have a date yet. The November sites need the same review, with two months of room. Your cash number counts no Veldhaven money. Who have you given the 14th to?"

That is the second-question test from *Start here*, answered in order:

1. **What is the point?** The answer, with its date.
2. **What does it rest on?** The one thing the date depends on, and what moved since they last heard.
3. **Where does it stop?** What isn't affected, and what you don't know yet.
4. **What do you want from me?** A decision, one question, or "nothing today".
5. **Who answers for it?** A name, and the day they will hear next.

Answer-first is old advice, and incident reports have long said what broke, what is affected and what comes next. The test insists on two more: what you don't know yet, and a name. The date inside it is a sized claim, from *How sure are you? Say that*: the date, what it depends on, the other date, and when you'll know. Lucía had no other date. She said so instead of inventing one.

Now test it. A busy executive may leave after the sentence with the date. If they left the call there, would they repeat something true? "We're on track for September" fails. "28 September if the second review round passes" survives, because the "if" is inside the sentence.

The shape holds from room to room, and the first sentence changes:

| Who hears it | Start with | Have ready |
|---|---|---|
| Your team, on a status call | What moved since last time | Who it blocks today |
| An executive with two minutes | The date or the decision, with its "if" | The cost of each choice |
| Someone who can decide what you can't | The decision, and by when | What happens if nobody decides |
| An investor | The date or number they repeat to others | What you got wrong since the last update |

## What they can see before the date

"My partners," Renata said. "All of them. And the labels?"

"I left them out because no date had moved. You should have had that in July, to judge for yourself."

Then Renata asked the second question: "How will I know, before the 28th, if the 28th is moving?"

"On two days," Lucía said. "Their policy gives each round ten working days, so by 4 September you'll know whether it passed, in Veldhaven's words. On 18 September the scheduler plans a full day at site one beside their paper plan, and I'll send you how many doors the supervisors changed. If either day goes by without that, I'll call you before you have to ask."

"Write me that. I'll forward it as it is."

> **From practice:** Slow work is quiet between its start and its date, and people fill the quiet with the last thing they heard. Before you begin, agree on points along the way that an outsider could see pass or fail: a result in somebody else's writing, a count, something that ran. "Nearly done" is no use as a point, because only you can see it.

A missed point is bad news while there is still time to plan around it. That evening Renata wrote again: "Sent as it was. They asked what happens if the review fails. I pointed to your fourth sentence."

## Practice: the July paragraph

**Situation.** The last day of July. Lucía writes the Veldhaven paragraph of her monthly update. The security review starts next week.

**First draft (what a careful founder writes).**
"Veldhaven: a strong month, and we remain on track for September. Integration tests at site one are nearly finished, and the printer updates at sites two and three went smoothly. The site teams have been great partners throughout. At the Rotterdam trade fair we had several very promising conversations. More next month!"

**What's wrong.** "On track" drops the condition Renata last heard in May. "Went smoothly" is the last scene of a slip she never heard about. She can't tell which piece of the good news she will be asked to defend.

**Better.**
"Site one goes live on 14 September if Veldhaven's security review passes in one round. If it needs a second, the 28th. I'll know by 20 August and call you that day.

"What moved: the label work for sites two and three slipped three weeks in June. We assumed their printers matched site one's, and nobody had checked. The printers are updated, and Diego, our engineer there, has since printed our labels at both sites. Their November dates still hold; they need the same review, with room for a second round.

"Cash still runs to late April, counting no Veldhaven money.

"Have you given your partners a date for site one? If so, tell me which, and I'll keep it right."

**Why it works.** The date she will repeat comes first and is hard to repeat without its "if". The slip arrives from Lucía, with its cause and its limit. The question at the end puts a name on the list.

**Your turn (10 minutes).** **If you do only one, do this one.** Take the last status you sent and underline the one date or number a reader would repeat. Rewrite that sentence with its condition, and add a line that begins "What moved:". **With a person.** Send it to someone who speaks on your word, then ask, "If [name] asks you about this tomorrow, what will you say?" Listen for the "if". **Done means** their answer has your date and its condition, and you corrected nothing.

## The bad part first

You may want to warm someone up before bad news. The warm-up mostly serves you, because a listener who senses bad news may hear nothing else until it comes.

> **Research says:** In one paper, not directly repeated, people giving mixed news and those receiving it preferred different orders; receivers wanted the bad part first. Hearing it first eased worry, and the relief left them less motivated to change their behavior. Applying that to a slipped date is this book's reasoning: open with the bad part, and when they need to act, put the request right behind it.

One sentence of ownership is enough at the top: "You should have heard both from me." The apology, if you owe one, is a conversation of its own (*The hard conversation*).

## When someone above you has to decide

An escalation is an update whose fourth answer is a decision you can't make. It needs one more rule: the person it is about reads it before it goes. Knowing they will read it takes the complaint out of your draft, and their reply often brings the fact you lacked.

### Practice: the escalation

**Situation.** You are the engineer on a client project. The client's network team has to open one test connection, and acceptance testing starts on the 24th. You write to the project's sponsor, a vice president.

**First draft (what a patient engineer writes).**
"Just flagging that we're still waiting on the network team. I raised it on the 3rd, followed up on the 7th and asked again on Monday. I know they're busy, but it's becoming a real blocker on our side. Any help appreciated."

**What's wrong.** It is a record of the writer's patience. The sponsor can't find a decision, a date or a cost, and the network lead will be asked about a message she has never seen.

**Better.**
"I need a decision from you by Thursday noon: open our test connection this week, or move acceptance testing from the 24th to the 31st. We need five working days between the open connection and the first test. Nothing else is waiting on it. The network lead has read this. Her team is moving the data center until the 21st, and our connection would stop that for half a day."

The sponsor phones: "So whose delay is this?"

"The connection is theirs. I asked three times without saying what it would cost, and that part is mine. What I need is which one moves: the data center by half a day, or the testing by a week."

**Why it works.** She can decide from the first sentence. The network lead's reason is there because the network lead read it. Asked about blame, you give two sentences, your own part included, and go back to the decision.

**Your turn (10 minutes, written).** Take one thing you are waiting for. Write the decision, its deadline and the cost of each choice, and send it first to the person it is about: "Is any of this wrong?" **Done means** they have corrected it or agreed to it, and whoever decides could answer in one line.

## Words to use

- **When a date moves.** "You'll hear this from others today. I wanted you to have it from me." "[New date], if [one thing]. I'll know by [day]."
- **When there is no other date.** "If that fails, I don't have a date yet. I'll have one by [day]."
- **To write the names.** "Who have you given the old date to?" "Who will ask you about this first?"
- **At the start of slow work.** "Before [date], you'll see two things: [one] on [day] and [one] on [day]."
- **Before you escalate.** "I'm asking [name] to decide this. Here is what I'll send. Is any of it wrong?"
- **When someone wants "on track".** "I can send the date with its condition. Without it, people would repeat something we don't know."

### Phrases you will hear

- "Just a heads-up": early notice. Ask whether you need to do anything.
- "Keep me in the loop": tell me when something changes. Ask which changes count.
- "It's green": the report says all is well. Ask what would turn it red.

## When it backfires

**Too many changes.** If you report every wobble, the one that matters looks like the rest. Break the rhythm only when something a listener has repeated stops being true, or a point along the way is missed.

**News that isn't yours.** Lucía could tell Renata her own date. What the reviewers found belonged to Veldhaven. A contract or a confidence comes before speed.

**When the other person holds the power.** If your boss punishes bad news, send it in writing, early, with a choice attached. If someone senior wants "on track", offer the sentence with its condition; they can send another one under their own name. If you can't refuse, keep your version, with its date.

**When going up is read as going around.** Teams differ on who may tell whom. Ask when the work starts: "If this date moves, who hears first, and from whom?" Showing someone the message first is for blocked work; harm or misconduct goes by a formal route (*Difficult people*).

**A second slip.** When the same date moves twice, a clear sentence is no longer enough. Say what you assumed both times, and what you now check before you give a date.

**In a second language.** An "if" at the end of a long sentence is easily lost on a call. Put it first: "If the review passes, the date is 28 September."
