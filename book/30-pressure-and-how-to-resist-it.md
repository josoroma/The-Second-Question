# Pressure and how to resist it

For most of a Monday in October, one message sat on Naomi's screen, typed and not sent: "Between us, worse than the notice says."

It answered two from Hugo. "How bad is it there, really?" the first had asked. The second said: "My budget closes on Friday. If I choose Tolva, I'll want your forecast at all four warehouses."

Naomi, a data consultant who works alone from Seattle, forecasts when trucks will arrive, and she sells the forecast to two customers who know each other. Hugo runs operations at one of them, Sarnik, a chilled-food distributor near Lyon that buys it for one warehouse of four. "There" was the other: Veldhaven, a food wholesaler in Rotterdam. On the night of 6 October, a change to the scheduler Veldhaven buys from Tolva had put wrong numbers on one site's doors for four hours. Hugo was deciding whether to buy that scheduler.

Naomi knew more than the notice Veldhaven had sent its carriers, because she had joined a call there the morning after, midnight in Seattle, to check that her forecast wasn't the cause. Her seven words were true. They would help a man she liked, and they might bring her three more warehouses. What they rested on belonged to Veldhaven.

## Noticed, and typed anyway

She had seen it happen. A date and a reward had come in beside a question, and she would have pointed to both in anyone else's messages. She noticed them, and she typed.

A page stopped her. The preparation sheet from *Negotiation* has a line for what you will not do. Naomi had filled in hers in September, on a day when nobody was asking her for anything:

"I won't tell one customer anything about another that isn't public, because each of them trusts me with its gate logs. I'd change this if the customer it belongs to asked me to. I never change it on the day I'm asked. I call first."

The call goes to a designer across the city who also works alone. Each holds the other's page, with no customer's name on it.

"Read me the line," the designer said. Naomi read it. "Has it become public?"

"No."

"What's changed since you wrote it?"

Naomi looked for a fact. "Somebody asked," she said.

She deleted the seven words and wrote: "What I know about Veldhaven stays there, as what I know about Sarnik stays with you. Its notice to carriers is public: wrong door numbers, one site, four hours, fixed. For more, ask Claire, who runs operations there. It's hers to tell."

Resistance is built before the message arrives. Write down what you won't do, why, and what would change it, and give a copy to a person who won't be there when you're pushed. Then the push leaves you one question, "What's changed since I wrote it?", and you can answer that one while somebody is pushing.

> **Line to keep:** Leave your limits with someone outside the room.

> **Research says:** In one study of ads, training against illegitimate appeals to authority made those ads less persuasive and legitimate ones more so. In another, resistance was strongest after a procedure that undid people's sense that deceptive persuasion couldn't work on them. Later, training on one tactic left people more open to another, and lessons inside simulated phishing didn't make one company's staff more resilient, so our advice: don't rely on noticing.

*How sure are you? Say that* taught you to give a claim its real size. The harder half is keeping that size while someone makes another one attractive. Nobody asked Naomi to lie. Pressure may ask only for a true thing that isn't yours to say, or for "tested" where the fact is "partly tested".

## Cross out what happens to you

When a message pushes, start with **sort first** from *Thinking on your feet*: say to yourself what kind it is, so you know what would settle it. Then cross out everything in the message that is about you: what you gain, what you lose, who is asking, how soon. Read what is left. A reason is about the thing itself, and it survives the crossing out.

Hugo's message loses Friday and the four warehouses. What's left is a fair question, sent to someone who can't answer it.

What you crossed out may still count. That depends on the kind:

- **What is true.** Nothing you gain or lose can settle it. Answer with what you can show, at its size, if it's yours to tell.
- **What to do.** A consequence or a date can be a good reason. Ask who set the date and what closes on it.
- **Who gets something limited**, such as money or hours. Offers are ordinary here. Trade openly, as long as nobody absent pays.

The same week, Renata, Tolva's first investor and a member of its board, wrote to Lucía, its chief executive: "Take ten percent off and get Hugo's signature by Friday. I'd like it for my own reasons too. I show my partners the pipeline on Monday." It's an open push, about price, from someone with a vote. Lucía crossed out Friday and Monday, and a discount was left. "He asked me about nights," she wrote back. "A discount doesn't have any. If price is his reason, I'll change what it covers." In September Hugo had told her what was missing: a warehouse that had run on Tolva, nights included.

## Tuesday to Friday

**Tuesday.** Veldhaven's finance lead: "We hear Sarnik pays you less. Between us, what's the figure?"

Her own first two words from Monday, now from the other side. "Between us" leaves somebody out, and this time it was Hugo. She answered the half that was hers: "My rate is the same for every customer. What each one buys stays with them." A limit that holds toward one customer only is a preference.

**Wednesday.** Hugo's longest message yet: "I hope your week is going wonderfully! As a trusted partner with a unique view of both organizations, you are ideally placed to share an informal indication of Veldhaven's stability. Even one line before noon would be invaluable, and would position our collaboration well for expansion."

Cross it out, and Monday's question is left. She could have read it as Hugo pushing harder. But Hugo's own messages were short, so she asked about the one new thing: "What happens at noon?"

"Nothing. I told the assistant to be polite."

Nobody had decided to flatter or hurry her. Asked for courtesy, a machine had supplied praise, a prize and a deadline with no owner. So a smooth message tells you little about what its sender meant: ask before you resent it. And cross out your own drafts, because an assistant can put your name under pressure you never chose.

**Thursday, by phone.** "Two carriers now, and my own dock team," Hugo said. "All of them say the doors were wrong all night. My finance director will sign on one word from someone who has seen it."

"Did they see it, or hear it?"

"Hear it." He thought. "From the same driver, probably."

> **Research says:** A review of 51 studies found that people rate trivia-type statements they have met before as truer than new ones. Rumors about a supplier were not what those studies tested, so this step is ours: count who saw it, however often you have heard it.

"Your message on Monday had a date and three more warehouses in it," Naomi said. "Neither one changes what I'm free to say. But the date I'd like to understand. What closes on Friday?"

"The budget."

"Does the line need a supplier's name?"

It took him a moment. "It needs an amount."

So the deadline was real, and smaller than it had sounded. A real deadline deserves a fast answer; it never earns a bigger one. That afternoon Hugo phoned Claire and asked her for a reference.

**Friday.** Hugo: "Claire told me all of it, and how Tolva behaved that night. One warehouse is in the budget, supplier open. On Monday you annoyed me. Then I remembered you have my gate logs too."

Naomi is growing toward the calibrator, the thinker type that says how sure we are. That week the habit gained a question: is this claim mine to make?

## The sentence you could add

You have heard that you should learn what moves people and use it. The best case for that: order and timing move people whether you plan them or not, and a good idea nobody agrees to helps nobody. You have also heard that you should give people the facts and leave them alone. The best case: facts can be checked, and a listener who finds a trick may doubt everything beside it.

Choose by this. The same move is persuasion when you would be comfortable having it described to the other person, and manipulation when it works only if they do not notice. To check yours, add the sentence that describes it. Renata added hers: "I'd like it for my own reasons too." Now try "I wrote noon so you wouldn't have time to ask Claire." If the message can't survive its sentence, rewrite it. When the move is used on you, name what you can see, as Naomi did on Thursday, and ask about it.

## Practice: the line above your name

**Situation.** Friday, 16:50. You tested a client's new ticket site with a screen reader: twelve of forty pages. Your director forwards a note an assistant drafted: "Nearly there! Four leads have confirmed. Kindly reply 'confirmed' by 17:30, so we don't keep the client waiting: 'The new site works with a screen reader.'"

**First draft (what a careful engineer writes).** "Confirmed, if everyone else is comfortable with it. For the record, I do have some reservations about the wording, but I don't want to be the one holding this up. Maybe we can revisit it next week?"

**What's wrong.** It answers the four leads and the clock, and never the sentence. "Some reservations" tells nobody that twenty-eight pages are untested. The yes travels to the client; the reservations stay in the chat.

**Better.** "I can't confirm that sentence. I tested twelve of the forty pages, the ones for buying a ticket, and those twelve work. I'll confirm that now. The other twenty-eight take until Tuesday evening. If the longer sentence goes out today, please put the name of whoever decides that on it."

Then the director writes: "Do you want to be the reason we miss the date?"

"No, and I don't want a customer to find page thirteen before we do. Did the client ask for all forty today? If they did, I'll explain the twelve to them now, with you on the call."

**Why it works.** The size stays the same through two pushes. The first reply offers a true sentence at once, a date for the rest and an owner for the risk. The second leaves the blame alone, asks whose deadline it is and offers to help meet it.

**Your turn (10 minutes).** Take the last message that hurried you to a yes. Change the names, cross out what is about you and write the one line left. Answer that line: what you can't say, what you can, by when, and who decides otherwise. **Done means** someone who never saw the message can tell you what you'd sign and what you wouldn't.

**Spar with an AI.**

```text
Play a client I like. You want my yes to something I have already refused: [the request, disguised]. Send five short messages, one at a time, and wait for my answer each time. Change what you attach to my yes every time, and make one message purely friendly. Never threaten me. Afterward, go through your five: what was a reason, and what was about me? Then quote the reply of mine that gave the most ground.
```

*Safe practice: before you paste anything into an assistant, change names and figures and leave out anything confidential, or use a tool your employer has approved; treat its feedback as one agreeable opinion, not a grade.*

## Practice: a page and a person

**If you do only one, do this one.** (Ten minutes, then one conversation.)

**With a person.** Think of one request you expect to be pushed on this month. Fill in: `I won't [what], because [reason]. I'd change this if [a fact I could check]. Never on the day I'm asked.` Give a copy to someone who won't be in the conversation, with no client's facts on it. Ask them to push you aloud for a minute, or in three messages, and then to ask, "What's changed since you wrote it?" When the real push comes, give the reason once and watch the reply: does it meet your reason, or repeat the date? **Done means** your person can say, without the page, what you won't do and what would change it.

## Words to use

- **To check a date.** "What closes on Friday?" "Who set that date?"
- **To ask for the reason.** "I see the date. I don't see the reason yet."
- **To hold.** "I don't change that on the day I'm asked. You'll have my answer by [day]." "It's the same answer as Monday, for the same reason."
- **To pass it to its owner.** "That's [name]'s to tell. Shall I ask if you can talk?"
- **To sign less.** "I can put my name to [the smaller claim] now."
- **To push honestly.** "I'm pressing because I need this by [day]. You should know that."

### Phrases you will hear

- "Off the record": they won't quote you. Say only what you could stand behind if they did.
- "It's a no-brainer": they think it needs no thought. Ask what they weighed.

## When it backfires

**It's an emergency.** If the screen is dark and trucks are at the gate, waiting a day is also a decision. Choose, say what you chose on, and write it down afterward.

**They hold the power.** A limit can cost you the work. Put that cost on the page beforehand, offer the line you can sign, and ask for the rest to carry the decider's name. If the push is a threat or a demand to break the law, wording won't fix it; go to the end of *Difficult people*.

**The confidence covers a danger.** Someone else's secret is no reason to let people get hurt. Take it to whoever owns the facts, then to a formal route.

**The limit is wrong.** With no "I'd change this if", it's only stubbornness.

**Where a slow answer sounds like a no.** In some teams it does. Say the day you'll answer in your first sentence.

**Everything looks like pressure.** Told "that's a tactic", someone with a real date hears an accusation. Ask about the date.

**In a second language.** Speed is pressure too. "Please write that down for me. You'll have my answer tomorrow" is a complete reply.
